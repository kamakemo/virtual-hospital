🎯 The case on the table

The reperfusion is done, the scans are stable — and now the outcome hinges on a swallow screen, a physiotherapist, and a well-run system. The most powerful stroke intervention is not a drug at all; it is organised care. This closing session is about the machinery that turns survival into recovery.

1 · The stroke unit — the strongest evidence in the field

Organised, multidisciplinary stroke-unit care reduces death and dependency more than thrombolysis or thrombectomy, and it benefits every stroke patient.

What makes it work: coordinated monitoring, early swallow assessment, early mobilisation, complication prevention, and structured rehabilitation planning by a team (nursing, physio, occupational therapy, speech and language, dietetics, social work) rather than any single intervention.

2 · Swallow, aspiration & nutrition

  • Swallow screen before any oral intake — dysphagia affects up to half of acute strokes and drives aspiration pneumonia, a leading cause of death.
  • Nil-by-mouth until assessed; alternative feeding (NG) if unsafe swallow; dietitian involvement; oral care.

3 · Complications of immobility

4 · Rehabilitation & recovery

💡 Early — but not too aggressive

Early mobilisation and task-specific rehab harness neuroplasticity, but the AVERT trial showed that very early, high-dose, out-of-bed mobilisation within hours can worsen outcomes — the sweet spot is early but measured. Rehab addresses motor recovery, spasticity, aphasia (speech and language therapy), cognition, and post-stroke depression (common, treatable, and a barrier to recovery).

5 · Follow-up, driving & prognosis

  • Secondary-prevention review (BP, lipids, antithrombotics, AF, carotid), risk-factor reinforcement and adherence support.
  • Driving: patients must not drive for a defined period after stroke/TIA and must meet recovery/visual-field criteria — advise and document per local (e.g. DVLA) rules.
  • Realistic, individualised goal-setting; most recovery happens in the first weeks–months but continues longer.

6 · Systems of hyperacute care

Outcomes depend on the whole chain: prehospital pre-notification, telestroke for remote thrombolysis decisions, and thrombectomy networks (the "mothership" vs "drip-and-ship" debate), all measured by door-to-needle and door-to-groin times. Building and auditing this system is how a region turns evidence into saved brains.

7 · The whole conference in eight lines

  1. Time is brain — save the penumbra (S1).
  2. Recognise fast with BE-FAST/NIHSS; beware mimics and posterior chameleons (S2, S7).
  3. CT first to exclude blood; image the vessels and the tissue (S3).
  4. Thrombolysis and thrombectomy — the earlier and the more proximal-selected, the better (S4–S5).
  5. Permissive hypertension and the stroke unit (S6).
  6. Haemorrhage flips the rules: lower BP, reverse anticoagulation; SAH needs securing and nimodipine (S9–S10).
  7. TIA is an emergency; find the cause (TOAST) and match the prevention (S11–S14).
  8. Recovery is won on the stroke unit and in the system (S15–S16).
☠️ M&M — a preventable aspiration death

A recovering stroke patient was given breakfast by a well-meaning relative before the swallow screen was done; he aspirated, developed pneumonia and died. Lesson: the most advanced reperfusion means nothing if a basic swallow screen is skipped. The unglamorous fundamentals of stroke-unit care — swallow, mobilisation, complication prevention — save more lives than any single high-tech intervention.
