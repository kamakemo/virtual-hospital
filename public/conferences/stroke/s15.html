🎯 The case on the table

A 34-year-old with no vascular risk factors has a stroke. The reflex work-up will be normal — because you are looking in the wrong place. Young stroke, pregnancy-related stroke and cerebral venous thrombosis follow different rules, and the mimics can fool you into thrombolysing a brain that never had a clot. This session is about not being caught out.

1 · Young stroke — a different differential

In the young, "atherosclerosis" is rarely the answer — think dissection, a hole in the heart, inflamed or clotting blood, and drugs.

2 · Stroke in pregnancy & the puerperium

Pregnancy is a hypercoagulable, high-risk window. Consider pre-eclampsia/eclampsia and PRES (posterior reversible encephalopathy — headache, seizures, visual loss, occipital oedema), cerebral venous sinus thrombosis (below), and standard ischaemic/haemorrhagic strokes. Reperfusion decisions are individualised with obstetrics; do not withhold work-up because a patient is pregnant.

3 · Cerebral venous sinus thrombosis (CVST)

💡 The stroke that breaks the arterial rules

CVST presents with headache (often progressive), seizures, and focal signs, sometimes with papilloedema. Venous infarcts cross arterial territories, are frequently haemorrhagic, and favour risk factors like pregnancy/puerperium, the oral contraceptive pill, dehydration and thrombophilia. Diagnose with CT/MR venography. The counter-intuitive treatment: anticoagulate — even in the presence of venous haemorrhage — because stopping clot propagation improves outcome.

4 · The mimics — don't lyse a non-stroke

  • Hypoglycaemia (check glucose always), seizure with Todd's paresis, migraine with aura (positive, spreading symptoms), functional deficits, MS/demyelination, tumour, and encephalitis.
  • The safeguards: a clear onset history, the pattern of symptoms, a glucose, and urgent imaging — thrombolysing a mimic is usually low-yield and occasionally harmful, while missing a real stroke costs brain.
☠️ M&M — the missed CVST

A young woman on the combined pill with a week of worsening headache then a seizure was treated for "migraine and a first fit"; her CT showed a small "haemorrhage" that was actually a venous infarct. The diagnosis of cerebral venous sinus thrombosis was made only after she deteriorated. Lesson: progressive headache + seizure + focal signs (especially with a prothrombotic risk factor, or a haemorrhage that crosses arterial territories) should trigger a venogram — and CVST is anticoagulated even when it has bled.

5 · Take-home

  • Young stroke: hunt dissection, PFO, vasculitis, thrombophilia/APS, drugs, genetics — not atherosclerosis.
  • Pregnancy: think pre-eclampsia/PRES and CVST; still investigate fully.
  • CVST — headache + seizure + haemorrhagic cross-territory infarct; diagnose with venography, anticoagulate even if haemorrhagic.
  • Respect the mimics: glucose, history and imaging keep you from lysing a non-stroke.
