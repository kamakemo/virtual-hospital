🎯 The case on the table

Triage hands you two patients in the same minute: one with a drooping face and slurred speech, one who is "just dizzy and vomiting." One is an obvious stroke call; the other is the one that gets missed and dies. Recognition is where strokes are won or lost — before any scanner.

1 · The rule that starts the clock

Any sudden focal neurological deficit is a stroke until proven otherwise — and the proof is a scan, not a hunch. Call the stroke pathway first, think later.

2 · FAST, BE-FAST and the prehospital scales

  • FAST — Face, Arm, Speech, Time — the public/paramedic screen.
  • BE-FAST adds Balance and Eyes (diplopia/visual loss) to catch posterior strokes FAST misses.
  • Prehospital LVO scales (RACE, LAMS) flag a likely large-vessel occlusion so EMS can pre-notify or route to a thrombectomy centre.

3 · The NIHSS — the language of stroke severity

The National Institutes of Health Stroke Scale (0–42) quantifies deficit across level of consciousness, gaze, visual fields, facial/limb power, ataxia, sensation, language, dysarthria and neglect. It drives treatment and prognosis:

NIHSSRough severityImplication
0No stroke signs—
1–4MinorStill treat if disabling
5–15ModerateReperfusion candidate
16–20Moderate–severeOften LVO
21–42SevereLarge territory; higher bleed risk
💡 NIHSS is dominant-hemisphere biased

Language scores heavily, so a dominant (usually left) MCA stroke scores high, while a big non-dominant or posterior stroke can have a deceptively low NIHSS despite a large, disabling infarct. A low score does not mean a small stroke.

5 · Localising signs worth memorising

  • Gaze deviation: a cortical (MCA) stroke pushes the eyes towards the lesion (away from the weak side); a pontine stroke drives them away.
  • Aphasia = dominant (usually left) cortex. Neglect/inattention = non-dominant (usually right).
  • Homonymous hemianopia = contralateral optic radiation/occiput.

6 · Mimics and chameleons — the two ways to be wrong

⚔️ Contrast pair — mimic vs chameleon

A mimic looks like a stroke but isn't (hypoglycaemia, seizure with Todd's paresis, migraine with aura, functional, Bell's palsy, sepsis unmasking an old deficit) — thrombolysing it risks harm. A chameleon is a real stroke that doesn't look like one (posterior circulation vertigo, acute confusion, isolated limb-shaking) — missing it costs brain. Always check a glucose before calling anything a stroke.

☠️ M&M — "just labyrinthitis"

A 55-year-old with sudden vertigo, vomiting and unsteadiness was diagnosed with labyrinthitis and discharged. He had a cerebellar stroke; two days later the swelling compressed his brainstem and he arrested. The bedside HINTS exam (a normal head-impulse test, direction-changing nystagmus, or skew deviation point to a central cause) would have flagged it. Lesson: acute vestibular syndrome with any central feature, or with new headache/inability to walk, is a posterior stroke until imaging says otherwise.

7 · Take-home

  • BE-FAST, not just FAST — catch the posterior stroke.
  • NIHSS quantifies and guides — but a low score can hide a big posterior/non-dominant stroke.
  • Learn TACS/PACS/LACS/POCS — they localise and prognosticate at the bedside.
  • Glucose first, then treat every sudden focal deficit as a stroke until scanned.
