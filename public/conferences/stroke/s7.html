🎯 The case on the table

A 60-year-old with sudden vertigo, double vision and slurred speech is drowsy by the time you review him. The pupils are pinpoint, the limbs stiffen. This is the posterior circulation stroke — the one that hides as "dizziness" and then kills. Learn its faces and you will save lives others miss.

1 · Why posterior strokes are dangerous

The brainstem packs consciousness, breathing, eye movements and every long tract into a few cubic centimetres — a small posterior stroke can be catastrophic, and its early symptoms are vague.

2 · Central vs peripheral vertigo — the HINTS exam

💡 HINTS (in acute continuous vertigo with nystagmus)

Paradoxically, reassuring peripheral findings are the abnormal head-impulse test. Central (stroke) features: a normal/negative head-impulse test, direction-changing nystagmus, and skew deviation ("HINTS: Impulse Normal, Fast-phase Alternating, Skew" = INFARCT). Inability to walk unaided or a new headache also point central.

3 · Basilar artery occlusion — the deadly one

Fluctuating consciousness, quadriparesis, cranial nerve signs, "locked-in syndrome" (aware but only vertical eye movement), or coma. Mortality is very high untreated. It warrants aggressive reperfusion — thrombolysis and thrombectomy (ATTENTION/BAOCHE) even in extended windows, because the alternative is death or locked-in.

4 · Cerebellar stroke — the surgical trap

⚠️ Posterior fossa is a closed box

A cerebellar infarct can swell over 2–4 days and compress the fourth ventricle (→ obstructive hydrocephalus) and the brainstem (→ death). Warning signs: worsening headache, vomiting, declining GCS, new gaze palsy. Management is urgent suboccipital decompression ± EVD — one of the few strokes cured by a surgeon.

6 · Arterial dissection — the young posterior stroke

Carotid or vertebral artery dissection is a leading cause of stroke in the young: neck/head pain or a partial Horner's syndrome, often after minor trauma, neck manipulation or connective-tissue disease. Diagnose with CTA/MRA (crescent sign). Management is antithrombotic (antiplatelet or anticoagulation — broadly equivalent per CADISS); consider thrombolysis if otherwise eligible.

☠️ M&M — "metabolic encephalopathy"

A drowsy, dysarthric patient with roving eyes was worked up for sepsis and metabolic causes for hours; the fluctuating consciousness was a basilar artery occlusion, and by the time an MRI was done the brainstem was infarcted. Lesson: fluctuating consciousness with any cranial-nerve, gaze or motor sign is a basilar occlusion until vessel imaging says otherwise — it is treatable, but only if you image early and reperfuse fast.

7 · Take-home

  • Acute vertigo + a central feature = posterior stroke; use HINTS, image, do not discharge as labyrinthitis.
  • Basilar occlusion — reperfuse aggressively, even late.
  • Cerebellar swelling can kill by day 2–4 — watch for it and call the surgeon.
  • Dissection is the young patient's stroke — neck pain + Horner's; antithrombotics.
