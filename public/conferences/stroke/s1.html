🎯 The case on the table

A 68-year-old is found slumped at breakfast, right arm limp, speech a garble. The clock on the wall says 08:10; his wife last saw him normal at 07:50. Somewhere in his skull, around a dead core of brain, a rim of neurons is still alive but starving. Everything you do in the next hour is a race to save that rim.

1 · The one sentence that governs stroke

Time is brain — roughly 1.9 million neurons die every minute an artery stays blocked. The whole specialty is a fight against that clock to rescue the penumbra.

A stroke is the sudden loss of neurological function from a vascular cause — 85% ischaemic (a vessel blocked) and 15% haemorrhagic (a vessel burst). The ischaemic core dies within minutes; the surrounding penumbra is under-perfused but still viable, kept alive by collateral flow. Reperfusion — thrombolysis or thrombectomy — exists to save the penumbra before it becomes core.

💡 The Circle of Willis

The anterior (carotid) and posterior (vertebrobasilar) circulations are joined by the communicating arteries — a built-in bypass. Good collaterals through this ring are why two people with the same occlusion can have wildly different infarcts: collaterals buy time.

3 · The ischaemic cascade — why the core dies

Understand this and every neuroprotective idea (and why most have failed) makes sense:

  1. Energy failure: no oxygen/glucose → ATP collapses → the Na⁺/K⁺ pump stops.
  2. Depolarisation & excitotoxicity: neurons depolarise and dump glutamate; glutamate opens NMDA channels → massive calcium influx.
  3. Calcium catastrophe: intracellular Ca²⁺ activates proteases, lipases and free-radical generation → membrane and mitochondrial destruction.
  4. Oedema: failed pumps let water in (cytotoxic oedema); later the blood–brain barrier breaks down (vasogenic oedema) → mass effect.
  5. Cell death: necrosis in the core, apoptosis in the penumbra over hours — the window in which reperfusion still helps.

4 · Core vs penumbra — the target of everything

⚔️ Contrast pair — core vs penumbra

Core: CBF below ~10 mL/100g/min — already dead, cannot be saved, and if large it bleeds when reperfused. Penumbra: CBF ~10–20 — electrically silent but structurally alive, recruiting collaterals, salvageable if flow returns. Modern imaging (CT/MR perfusion) literally maps core vs penumbra to select who still benefits — even out to 24 hours.

5 · Watershed & lacunar patterns

  • Watershed (borderzone) infarcts sit between two territories and are driven by global hypoperfusion (sepsis, cardiac arrest, critical carotid stenosis) rather than a single embolus — "the last field to be watered dies first in a drought."
  • Lacunar infarcts are small deep strokes from occlusion of a single perforator, the disease of chronic hypertension and diabetes — pure motor, pure sensory, ataxic hemiparesis, dysarthria–clumsy hand. No cortical signs (no aphasia/neglect).
☠️ M&M — "the collaterals were the only thing keeping him alive"

A patient with a critical carotid stenosis and a borderline penumbra was left flat and dehydrated overnight "to settle." His collateral flow was pressure-dependent; the drop in perfusion converted a salvageable penumbra into a completed infarct by morning. Lesson: in acute ischaemia, perfusion pressure and hydration matter — the penumbra lives on collaterals, and collaterals live on blood pressure. This is the mechanistic reason we allow permissive hypertension (Session 6).

6 · Take-home wiring diagram

  • Stroke = sudden vascular brain injury; 85% ischaemic, 15% haemorrhagic — the first CT decides which.
  • Core dies, penumbra waits — reperfusion exists to save the penumbra.
  • Know the five territories and their signatures — they localise the clot before any scan.
  • The ischaemic cascade (energy → glutamate → calcium → oedema → death) is why every minute counts.
  • Collaterals and perfusion pressure buy the time thrombolysis and thrombectomy then cash in.
