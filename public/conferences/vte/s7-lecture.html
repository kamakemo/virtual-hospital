🎯 The case on the table

A 52-year-old woman with metastatic colon cancer and a new PE. A 30-year-old, 28 weeks pregnant, with a leg DVT. A 40-year-old man with recurrent clots and a prolonged APTT. The same disease, but the "default DOAC" answer is wrong — sometimes dangerously — in every one of them. Special populations are where VTE mastery is proven.

1 · Cancer-associated thrombosis (CAT) — the biggest special case

Cancer ticks all three of Virchow's limbs (hypercoagulable tumour biology, stasis, endothelial injury from lines/chemo). CAT is common, recurs despite anticoagulation, and bleeds more. Historically LMWH was standard (CLOT trial: LMWH beat warfarin). Modern trials (Hokusai-VTE Cancer, SELECT-D, Caravaggio, ADAM-VTE) established that DOACs are effective alternatives — with one crucial caveat.

⚠️ The GI/GU bleeding caveat

DOACs (especially edoxaban and rivaroxaban) increase major GI bleeding in patients with luminal GI or genitourinary tumours. In those patients, prefer LMWH or apixaban (Caravaggio suggested apixaban had less GI bleeding signal). 2023 ITAC/ASH/ASCO guidance: DOAC or LMWH for most CAT; avoid DOACs with unresected luminal GI cancers / high GI-bleed risk; watch drug interactions with systemic anti-cancer therapy.

  • Duration: anticoagulate for at least 6 months, and continue while the cancer is active or on treatment.
  • Interactions: many oral anti-cancer agents affect P-gp/CYP3A4 — a reason LMWH is still chosen.
  • Thrombocytopenia from chemo: dose-modify or hold anticoagulation by platelet count (e.g. full dose if >50; reduce/hold below).

2 · Pregnancy — LMWH, and never a DOAC

In pregnancy the anticoagulant is low-molecular-weight heparin, full stop. DOACs and warfarin are contraindicated.
  • Why not DOACs: they cross the placenta and lack safety data → avoid throughout pregnancy and breastfeeding.
  • Why not warfarin: teratogenic (warfarin embryopathy in the first trimester) and fetal bleeding — reserved only for mechanical heart valves under specialist care.
  • LMWH does not cross the placenta, is dosed by booking weight (often BD), and is monitored by anti-Xa only in extremes of weight or renal impairment.
  • Around delivery: switch to a plan that allows neuraxial anaesthesia — omit LMWH ~24 h before planned delivery; convert to UFH if very high risk. Postpartum, anticoagulation continues for at least 6 weeks and a minimum total of 3 months; warfarin and LMWH are breastfeeding-safe (DOACs are not).

3 · Antiphospholipid syndrome (APS) — the DOAC trap

⚠️ Do not use DOACs in high-risk / triple-positive APS

The TRAPS trial was stopped early because rivaroxaban caused more arterial thrombotic events than warfarin in triple-positive APS. Standard of care for thrombotic APS remains warfarin (INR 2–3). Suspect APS in unprovoked VTE in the young, arterial + venous events, recurrent pregnancy loss, or an unexplained prolonged APTT — confirm with lupus anticoagulant, anticardiolipin, and anti-β2-glycoprotein-I antibodies (repeat at 12 weeks).

4 · Renal failure & dialysis

Severe CKD shifts you away from renally-cleared drugs (see Session 6). Dabigatran is contraindicated <30. Many use UFH acutely then warfarin in dialysis-dependent patients; apixaban is increasingly chosen given its lower renal dependence. LMWH accumulates in renal failure — reduce dose and monitor anti-Xa, or switch to UFH.

5 · Extremes of body weight

  • High weight: earlier caution about DOACs above 120 kg / BMI >40 has softened — apixaban and rivaroxaban now have reasonable data and are considered acceptable by ISTH guidance, but LMWH (weight-based) or warfarin remain options if uncertain.
  • Low weight / frailty: mind dose-reduction criteria (e.g. edoxaban 30 mg if ≤60 kg; apixaban 2.5 mg BD in AF if 2 of 3: age ≥80, weight ≤60 kg, creatinine ≥133 — a rule for AF, not the VTE loading regimen).

6 · Thrombophilia testing — who, and when NOT

💡 The testing discipline

Do not routinely test for thrombophilia after a provoked VTE. It rarely changes management (duration is driven by provoked/unprovoked, not by the assay), the acute clot and anticoagulation distort results, and a positive test causes lifelong anxiety. Consider selective testing (e.g. APS) only when it will change the decision — typically unprovoked VTE in the young, strong family history, or unusual-site thrombosis — and test off anticoagulation, well after the acute event, with haematology input.

7 · Unusual-site thrombosis (brief)

  • Splanchnic vein thrombosis (portal, mesenteric) — think cirrhosis, local malignancy, pancreatitis, and myeloproliferative neoplasms (check JAK2).
  • Cerebral venous sinus thrombosis — headache + focal deficit/seizure; anticoagulate even if there is haemorrhagic infarction.
  • Both often warrant a search for an underlying driver and specialist involvement.
☠️ M&M — the "convenient" DOAC in APS

A 38-year-old with prior DVT, a stroke, and a persistently prolonged APTT was switched from warfarin to rivaroxaban "to avoid INR checks." Three months later he had a second, disabling stroke. He was triple-positive for antiphospholipid antibodies. Lesson: convenience is not a clinical indication. In high-risk APS the DOAC is contraindicated — warfarin's inconvenience is exactly what keeps these patients safe.
