🎯 The case on the table

A 60-year-old man with a saddle embolus becomes hypotensive in resus — SBP 78, mottled, agitated. The clock is now the enemy. What you do in the next ten minutes decides whether he survives. Acute PE management is a triage tree branching off the risk class you assigned in Session 4.

1 · The first move is always the same

Anticoagulate the moment PE is likely — do not wait for the CT if clinical probability is high and bleeding risk is acceptable.

Every PE, from low-risk to arrest, gets anticoagulation. The branch point is whether anticoagulation alone is enough, or whether the clot must be actively removed (reperfusion). That decision follows directly from the ESC risk class.

2 · Branch A — High-risk (massive) PE: reperfuse now

🚨 Systemic thrombolysis is the default

For confirmed (or, in arrest, strongly suspected) high-risk PE with no absolute contraindication: alteplase 100 mg IV over 2 hours (or a weight-based accelerated 0.6 mg/kg over 15 min in cardiac arrest). Give unfractionated heparin alongside/after. Thrombolysis dissolves clot, drops pulmonary pressures, and rescues the RV spiral.

Absolute contraindications to thrombolysis: prior intracranial haemorrhage, ischaemic stroke <6 months, CNS neoplasm, recent major trauma/surgery/head injury <3 weeks, active bleeding, known bleeding diathesis. In these patients — or when thrombolysis fails — go to catheter-directed therapy or surgical embolectomy.

3 · Branch B — Intermediate-risk PE: anticoagulate and watch (mostly)

The pivotal PEITHO trial answered this: in intermediate–high PE, upfront full-dose systemic thrombolysis prevented haemodynamic decompensation but tripled major bleeding and caused a ~2% intracranial haemorrhage rate. Net verdict:

💡 PEITHO one-liner

Do not routinely thrombolyse intermediate-risk PE. Anticoagulate, admit to a monitored bed, and keep thrombolysis (or catheter therapy) in reserve as rescue if they decompensate. The intermediate–high patient is the one to watch like a hawk.

Reduced-dose ("safe-dose") thrombolysis and catheter-directed approaches are active areas of trials (e.g. lower-dose lysis, aspiration thrombectomy) aiming to capture benefit with less bleeding — promising but not yet the routine standard for stable intermediate PE.

4 · Branch C — Low-risk PE: start a DOAC, consider home

Low-risk patients (Session 4) start a direct oral anticoagulant and, if they meet Hestia/outpatient criteria, can be discharged the same day with early follow-up. This is safe, cheaper, and preferred by patients — but only after genuine risk stratification.

5 · The PERT concept — a team for the crashing PE

A Pulmonary Embolism Response Team (PERT) convenes emergency medicine, cardiology, intensive care, interventional radiology/cardiology, and cardiac surgery for high- and intermediate–high-risk PE. Because the best therapy (lysis vs catheter vs surgery vs ECMO) depends on local expertise and the individual patient, a rapid multidisciplinary huddle improves decision-making for exactly the patients most likely to die.

6 · Supportive care while you reperfuse

  • Oxygen to keep SaO₂ ≥90%; high-flow or ventilation as needed (beware intubation — positive pressure can drop RV preload and precipitate arrest; induce cautiously).
  • Vasopressor of choice: noradrenaline — restores systemic and coronary perfusion of the failing RV.
  • Cautious fluids (≤500 mL) only if the RV is under-filled; avoid volume overload.
  • Inotrope: dobutamine can augment RV contractility but may worsen hypotension — use with a pressor.
  • Consider inhaled pulmonary vasodilators (e.g. inhaled nitric oxide) to offload the RV in specialist settings.

7 · Clot in transit & PE in cardiac arrest

A mobile thrombus caught in the right heart on echo is a surgical/interventional emergency. In cardiac arrest with a high suspicion of PE (PEA, unexplained arrest with risk factors, RV dilation on echo), give thrombolysis during CPR and continue resuscitation for 60–90 minutes — recovery after lytic-during-arrest is well described.

☠️ M&M — the contraindication that was overlooked

A 72-year-old with high-risk PE received full-dose alteplase in resus. What the team missed on a rushed history: an ischaemic stroke six weeks earlier. He developed a fatal intracranial haemorrhage. Lesson: thrombolysis saves lives in massive PE, but the contraindication checklist is not optional even when the clock is running — and it is exactly the moment a catheter-directed or surgical option should have been called via PERT.

8 · Take-home decision tree

  1. Everyone: anticoagulate (UFH if reperfusion likely; DOAC/LMWH otherwise).
  2. High-risk: systemic thrombolysis → if contraindicated/failed, catheter or surgical embolectomy ± ECMO. Activate PERT.
  3. Intermediate-risk: anticoagulate + monitor; thrombolysis only as rescue (PEITHO).
  4. Low-risk: DOAC, consider outpatient treatment (Hestia).
  5. Support the RV with noradrenaline, careful fluids, and oxygen — never drown it.
