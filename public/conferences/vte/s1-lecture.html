🎯 Where we start

A 54-year-old flies home from a conference, sits for 11 hours, and two days later collapses reaching for the kettle. To understand why, you do not start at the CT scanner — you start inside the vein. Master the mechanism and every guideline downstream becomes obvious.

1 · The one sentence that holds the whole topic

VTE is one disease with two faces: a clot that stays is a DVT; a clot that flies is a PE. Same thrombus, same patient, same anticoagulant — different postcode.

Deep vein thrombosis (DVT) and pulmonary embolism (PE) are not two diseases that happen to travel together — they are a single continuum called venous thromboembolism (VTE). Roughly half of proximal DVTs embolise if left untreated, and the majority of PEs arise from a leg or pelvic clot that you can no longer see because it has already left. This is why we treat the patient, not the picture.

2 · Virchow's triad — the engine of the disease

Rudolf Virchow, 1856. Three ingredients; you rarely need all three, but the more you stack, the higher the risk. Every risk factor you will ever memorise is just a way of ticking one of these boxes.

Limb of the triadWhat it means mechanisticallyReal-world triggers
🩸 StasisSlow flow lets activated clotting factors accumulate instead of being washed away; hypoxia injures the endothelium behind valve pocketsImmobility, long-haul travel, plaster casts, hospital bed rest, heart failure, paralysis
🔬 Endothelial injuryExposed subendothelial collagen & tissue factor ignite the clotting cascade; loss of natural anticoagulant surfaceSurgery, trauma, central lines, prior DVT, smoking, inflammation/sepsis
⚗️ HypercoagulabilityThe blood itself is primed to clot — too much procoagulant or too little natural anticoagulantCancer, pregnancy/oestrogen, thrombophilias, nephrotic syndrome, sepsis, COVID-19
💡 One-liner

Stasis + Injury + Hypercoagulability = Thrombosis. When a patient presents, silently score them against all three — the answer to "is this VTE?" is usually written in the risk factors before any test.

3 · Where clots are born: the valve pocket

Venous thrombi almost always start in the valve-pocket sinuses of the deep calf veins. Why there? Blood flow eddies and stagnates behind each valve cusp, creating a low-oxygen microenvironment. Hypoxia switches the endothelium from an anti-thrombotic surface to a pro-thrombotic one (down-regulating thrombomodulin, up-regulating tissue factor and P-selectin). Red cells and fibrin pile up — the classic "red thrombus", fibrin- and RBC-rich, unlike the platelet-rich "white thrombus" of arterial disease.

⚔️ Contrast pair — venous vs arterial clot

Venous (red): fibrin + red cells, forms in slow flow, treated with anticoagulants. Arterial (white): platelet-rich, forms on a ruptured plaque in fast flow, treated with antiplatelets. This is why we give heparin/DOACs for VTE and aspirin for MI — the biology dictates the drug.

4 · The vicious cycle — why a small clot becomes a big problem

Once a calf thrombus forms, three feed-forward loops can turn a nuisance into a killer:

  1. Propagation loop: the clot surface is itself thrombogenic → recruits more fibrin → grows proximally from calf → popliteal → femoral → iliac. Proximal clots are the ones that embolise.
  2. Embolisation loop: a fresh, poorly-adherent tail breaks off → travels through the IVC and right heart → lodges in the pulmonary arteries.
  3. RV loop (the PE spiral): obstructed pulmonary vasculature + hypoxic vasoconstriction → acute rise in pulmonary vascular resistance → the thin-walled RV dilates and fails → falling LV preload → falling cardiac output → falling coronary perfusion of the RV → worse RV failure. This spiral is what kills within the first hours, and we cover it in depth in Session 4.

5 · Epidemiology — why this matters on every ward

  • VTE incidence ≈ 1–2 per 1,000 person-years, rising steeply with age (>1% per year over age 80).
  • PE is one of the leading preventable causes of in-hospital death — the reason mandatory VTE risk assessment exists.
  • ~25–30% of untreated symptomatic PE is fatal; with anticoagulation, mortality falls dramatically.
  • Up to 50% of VTE is associated with a hospital admission in the preceding 90 days — most is preventable with prophylaxis (Session 8).
  • After a first event, cumulative recurrence approaches 30% at 10 years — which is why duration of therapy is a whole session by itself.

6 · Provoked vs unprovoked — the fork that decides duration

The single most useful classification you will make at diagnosis is whether the event was provoked (a transient or persistent trigger is present) or unprovoked (none found). It drives how long the patient stays on anticoagulation for the rest of their life.

Provoked — major transientSurgery >30 min under GA, major trauma, hospitalisation ≥3 days with immobility, C-sectionLow (~3%/yr) → typically 3 months

☠️ M&M — "It was just a swollen calf"

A 61-year-old man was sent home from an urgent care clinic with a calf strain and a compression sock. The swelling was a distal DVT; over five days it propagated to the femoral vein and threw a saddle embolus. He arrested in his kitchen. Lesson: "muscle strain" with unilateral swelling and a risk factor is DVT until an ultrasound says otherwise. The disease is silent until it is catastrophic — respect the pretest probability, which is exactly what Session 2 teaches you to quantify.

7 · Take-home wiring diagram

  • One disease, two faces: DVT and PE are the same clot in different places.
  • Virchow's triad is the generator; every risk factor plugs into stasis, injury, or hypercoagulability.
  • Clots are born in calf valve pockets, propagate proximally, and embolise from the proximal veins.
  • The RV spiral is the acute killer; recurrence is the chronic threat.
  • Provoked vs unprovoked is the classification you must make on day one.
