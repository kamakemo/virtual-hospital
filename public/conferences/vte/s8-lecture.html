🎯 The case on the table

Your PE patient from Session 5 is now three months in, feeling well, and asks the question that every VTE patient asks: "Doctor, can I stop the tablets?" This is the hardest question in the whole topic — because the honest answer is "it depends on why you clotted, and whether you would bleed." This final session closes the loop: how long to treat, how to prevent the next one, and the long-term scars VTE can leave.

1 · The duration decision — a three-part question

Every duration decision balances recurrence risk if you stop against bleeding risk if you continue. Three questions frame it:

  1. Was it provoked or unprovoked? (Session 1's fork)
  2. What is the bleeding risk?
  3. What does the patient value?
ScenarioRecommended duration
Provoked by a major transient risk factor (surgery, trauma, hospitalisation)3 months, then stop — recurrence risk is low once the trigger is gone
Provoked by a minor transient factor (flight, oestrogen, minor injury)3 months; individualise
Unprovoked (first event)≥3 months, then consider indefinite/extended therapy — high recurrence (~10%/yr)
Recurrent unprovoked VTEIndefinite anticoagulation
Active cancerContinue while cancer is active/on treatment (Session 7)
💡 One-liner

Provoked-and-gone = 3 months. Unprovoked or persistent = think long-term. "3 months" is a minimum, not an automatic stop date.

2 · Tools to refine recurrence risk after unprovoked VTE

  • HERDOO2 (validated in women): stop after short-term therapy if a woman has ≤1 of — Hyperpigmentation/oedema/redness of the leg, D-dimer ≥250 (on treatment), Obesity (BMI ≥30), Older age ≥65. Men remain higher-risk and generally continue.
  • DASH and Vienna scores use D-dimer, age, sex, and hormonal factors to estimate recurrence.
  • Post-anticoagulation D-dimer: a rise after stopping predicts recurrence and can prompt restarting.

Men, proximal DVT/PE (vs distal), a persistently raised D-dimer, and residual vein obstruction all push toward extended therapy.

3 · Extended therapy — you can lower the dose

If you decide to continue beyond the initial period, two DOACs have reduced-dose extended regimens that cut bleeding while preserving protection:

4 · Assessing bleeding risk before committing

Weigh continuing against bleeding: prior bleeding, age >65–75, renal/hepatic failure, thrombocytopenia, concomitant antiplatelets, uncontrolled hypertension, prior stroke, alcohol, and cancer all raise risk. There is no perfect VTE bleeding score, but a high-risk profile shifts you toward a defined stop or a reduced-dose extended regimen and periodic reassessment — the decision is not "set and forget."

5 · Prevention — stopping the first (or next) clot

Most hospital-associated VTE is preventable. Risk-assess every admission.

SettingRisk toolProphylaxis
Medical inpatientsPadua (or IMPROVE) scoreLMWH/fondaparinux if high risk & low bleeding risk; mechanical (IPC) if bleeding risk high

Major orthopaedic (hip/knee arthroplasty, hip fracture)All high-riskExtended prophylaxis: LMWH, or low-dose DOAC (e.g. rivaroxaban 10 mg, apixaban 2.5 mg BD), or aspirin per protocol; up to 35 days for hip
⚠️ Mechanical is not a free pass

Intermittent pneumatic compression is for patients who cannot receive pharmacologic prophylaxis (active bleeding/high bleeding risk) — it is less effective than anticoagulation and must be worn to work. Do not substitute it for a needle in a patient who could safely have heparin.

6 · The long-term scars of VTE

  • Post-thrombotic syndrome (PTS): after ~20–50% of DVTs. Chronic valvular damage → venous hypertension → leg pain, swelling, hyperpigmentation, and ulceration. Reduce risk with adequate anticoagulation and early mobilisation; graduated compression stockings treat symptoms (they do not reliably prevent PTS — SOX trial was negative for routine use).
  • Chronic thromboembolic pulmonary hypertension (CTEPH): after ~2–4% of PEs, organised clot becomes fibrotic webs in the pulmonary arteries → progressive pulmonary hypertension and RV failure. Suspect it in any patient with persistent breathlessness 3+ months after PE — screen with echo then V/Q (more sensitive than CTPA for chronic disease). It is potentially curable by pulmonary endarterectomy; balloon pulmonary angioplasty and riociguat help inoperable disease. Missing CTEPH is missing a curable cause of pulmonary hypertension.

7 · IVC filters — narrow indications, real harms

⚠️ A filter is not an anticoagulant substitute of convenience

Indication: acute proximal VTE with an absolute contraindication to anticoagulation (e.g. active major bleeding). Retrieve it as soon as anticoagulation can be started — retained filters cause IVC thrombosis, fracture, and migration, and paradoxically increase recurrent DVT. Routine filters added to anticoagulation do not reduce mortality (PREPIC/PREPIC-2).

8 · Heparin-induced thrombocytopenia (HIT) — the anticoagulant that clots

An immune reaction to heparin–PF4 complexes causing a paradoxical prothrombotic state. Suspect with the 4Ts (Thrombocytopenia — usually a 30–50% fall at day 5–10, Timing, Thrombosis, no oTher cause). Stop all heparin (including flushes), do not give platelets, do not start warfarin acutely (risk of venous limb gangrene), and switch to a non-heparin anticoagulant (argatroban, bivalirudin, fondaparinux, or a DOAC). Confirm with anti-PF4 ELISA + a functional (SRA) assay.

☠️ M&M — the breathlessness that was "deconditioning"

A 55-year-old, six months after a large PE, was told her persistent exertional dyspnoea was "deconditioning after the clot." She was never re-imaged. Two years later she presented with overt right heart failure — CTEPH that, caught earlier, could have been cured by pulmonary endarterectomy. Lesson: breathlessness that fails to resolve months after PE is CTEPH until a V/Q scan proves otherwise. The disease does not end when the anticoagulation starts.

9 · The whole conference in eight lines

  1. VTE is one disease — Virchow's triad is the engine (S1).
  2. Diagnose DVT and PE by probability first, then test (S2–S3).
  3. The RV, not the clot, is the prognosis (S4).
  4. Reperfuse the unstable, anticoagulate-and-watch the rest (S5).
  5. Know your four drug families, doses, and reversal agents (S6).
  6. Special populations break the "default DOAC" rule (S7).
  7. Provoked-and-gone = 3 months; unprovoked/persistent = long-term (S8).
  8. Prevent the next clot, and never miss CTEPH or HIT (S8).
