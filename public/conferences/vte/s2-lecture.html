🎯 The case on the table

A 47-year-old woman, 6 days after a knee arthroscopy, has a warm, swollen, tender right calf. Do you scan? Do you D-dimer? Do you anticoagulate while you wait? Get the order of operations right and you will never miss a leg clot — or irradiate a normal one.

1 · The golden rule of DVT diagnosis

Never order a D-dimer before you have decided how likely the clot is. The test only means something after you have scored the pretest probability.

D-dimer is a rule-out test, not a rule-in test. It is exquisitely sensitive but hopelessly non-specific — it rises with infection, surgery, pregnancy, cancer, trauma, and age. Fire it blindly at a high-probability patient and a "negative" will falsely reassure you; fire it at everyone and a "positive" sends half your clinic to ultrasound. The pretest probability decides everything.

2 · Step 1 — Wells score for DVT

Interpretation (two-level): ≥2 = DVT "likely"; ≤1 = DVT "unlikely."

3 · Step 2 — the two pathways

💡 The algorithm in one breath

Unlikely (Wells ≤1) → D-dimer. Negative ⇒ DVT excluded, stop. Positive ⇒ ultrasound.
Likely (Wells ≥2) → go straight to ultrasound (a D-dimer cannot rescue you here).

  • DVT-unlikely + negative D-dimer: the combination has a >99% negative predictive value. No imaging, no anticoagulation, safe to discharge. This is the single most valuable manoeuvre in the whole pathway — it spares thousands of scans.
  • DVT-likely, or positive D-dimer: proximal (or whole-leg) compression ultrasound is the reference test.

4 · Age-adjusted D-dimer — stop over-scanning the elderly

D-dimer rises with age, so a fixed 500 µg/L cut-off makes almost every 80-year-old "positive." Use the age-adjusted threshold in patients over 50 with a non-high probability:

Age-adjusted cut-off = age (years) × 10 µg/L (for FEU assays), e.g. a 78-year-old's threshold becomes 780 µg/L, not 500.

Validated by the ADJUST-PE study — it safely raises the proportion of patients ruled out without imaging, at no cost in missed events.

5 · Step 3 — compression ultrasound (the reference test)

The entire test rests on one physical sign: a normal vein collapses fully under gentle probe pressure; a vein packed with thrombus does not. Non-compressibility of the common femoral or popliteal vein is the diagnosis.

⚔️ Contrast pair — proximal vs distal (calf) DVT

Proximal DVT (popliteal and above): high embolic risk → anticoagulate. Isolated distal/calf DVT: lower embolic risk → either anticoagulate or surveillance-scan (serial ultrasound over ~2 weeks) to see if it propagates. The decision hinges on symptoms, risk factors (cancer, prior VTE, D-dimer very high), and bleeding risk.

6 · Do not be fooled — the DVT mimics

  • Ruptured Baker's cyst — sudden calf pain, sometimes a "crescent" bruise below the malleolus; ultrasound shows the cyst, not a clot.
  • Cellulitis — erythema, warmth, fever, a portal of entry; but it can also coexist with DVT.
  • Post-thrombotic syndrome / chronic venous insufficiency — chronic swelling in a leg with old DVT; the ultrasound shows old, recanalised, incompressible vein — beware calling it "acute."
  • Superficial vein thrombosis — a palpable tender cord; if it is long (>5 cm) or near the sapheno-femoral junction it needs treatment because it can extend into the deep system.
  • Muscle tear / haematoma, lymphoedema, heart failure (bilateral).

7 · Upper-extremity DVT — do not forget the arm

Increasingly common because of central lines, PICCs, and pacemakers (secondary UEDVT), or effort-related in young athletes (Paget–Schroetter, primary UEDVT). Diagnose with ultrasound ± venography. Line-associated clots are anticoagulated; the line can often stay if still needed and functioning. Primary effort thrombosis in young patients may warrant thrombolysis and thoracic-outlet decompression.

☠️ M&M — the D-dimer that gave false comfort

A 68-year-old with a hugely swollen, tender thigh (Wells = 3, "likely") had a D-dimer sent by a busy SHO; it came back mildly elevated and was read as "non-specific, probably her cellulitis." No scan was done. Forty-eight hours later she presented with syncopal PE. Lesson: in a DVT-likely patient the D-dimer is meaningless — you must image. The score, not the assay, was giving the right answer all along.

8 · Take-home pathway

  1. Score first (Wells): likely vs unlikely.
  2. Unlikely → D-dimer (age-adjusted if >50); negative ends it.
  3. Likely or positive D-dimer → compression ultrasound.
  4. Proximal clot → treat. Isolated calf clot → treat or surveil.
  5. Always ask: could this be a mimic? And is the arm the real story?
