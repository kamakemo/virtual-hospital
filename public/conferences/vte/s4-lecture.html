🎯 The case on the table

Two patients, same CT: bilateral PE. One is chatting on his phone with a normal blood pressure; the other is grey, clammy, and hypotensive. They will receive completely different treatment in the next 30 minutes. Risk stratification — not clot burden — decides who lives.

1 · The principle that governs everything

In PE, the enemy is not the clot — it is the right ventricle. Prognosis is decided by how the RV is coping, not by how much CT lights up.

A large clot in a fit young RV may be tolerated; a modest clot in a diseased RV can kill. This is why the 2019 ESC guideline classifies severity by haemodynamic status, RV function, and biomarkers — never by clot size alone.

2 · The RV spiral — the mechanism you must be able to draw

Understand this loop and every treatment decision (thrombolysis, fluids, pressors, ECMO) becomes logical:

  1. Clot obstructs pulmonary arteries + hypoxic vasoconstriction → pulmonary vascular resistance rises abruptly.
  2. The thin-walled RV was never built for high afterload → it dilates and its wall tension soars.
  3. The dilating RV bows the septum into the LV ("D-shaped septum") → LV filling falls → cardiac output and systemic BP drop.
  4. Falling aortic pressure + rising RV wall tension → RV coronary perfusion falls → RV ischaemia.
  5. Ischaemic RV contracts even less → back to step 2, only worse. This is the death spiral, and it can complete in minutes.
⚠️ Why fluids can kill here

Reflex to give a litre of saline to a hypotensive PE? An already dilated RV pushed further left worsens septal bowing and drops LV output. Give cautious fluid (≤500 mL) only if the RV is under-filled; otherwise reach for noradrenaline to restore coronary perfusion and support the RV.

3 · Step 1 — is the patient haemodynamically unstable?

This single question splits the entire population. High-risk (massive) PE = any of:

  • Cardiac arrest, or
  • Obstructive shock (SBP <90 with hypoperfusion, or needing vasopressors), or
  • Persistent hypotension: SBP <90 mmHg or a drop ≥40 mmHg for >15 minutes not explained by other causes.

High-risk PE carries early mortality often >15% and mandates immediate reperfusion (Session 5). Everyone else is normotensive and moves to Step 2.

4 · Step 2 — sPESI: separating low from not-low

In the normotensive patient, the simplified Pulmonary Embolism Severity Index identifies who is genuinely low-risk (and potentially home-treatable).

sPESI variable (1 point each)
Age >80
History of cancer
Chronic cardiopulmonary disease
Heart rate ≥110
Systolic BP <100 mmHg
Arterial oxygen saturation <90%

sPESI = 0 → 30-day mortality ~1% (low-risk). sPESI ≥1 → intermediate-risk; assess the RV and biomarkers.

5 · Step 3 — RV assessment + biomarkers (splitting the intermediate band)

  • RV dysfunction — echo (RV dilation, RV:LV >1.0, McConnell's sign = free-wall akinesis with apical sparing, TAPSE <16 mm, septal flattening) or CT (RV:LV >1.0).
  • Myocardial injury — elevated troponin (RV micro-infarction) and elevated BNP/NT-proBNP (RV wall stress).

💡 Why the intermediate–high box matters

These patients look stable but have both a strained RV and a leaking troponin — they are the ones who "crash on the ward." They warrant monitored admission and are the group in whom rescue reperfusion is considered if they deteriorate (PEITHO, Session 5).

7 · Who can go home? Hestia & low-risk pathways

Selected low-risk patients (sPESI 0, no RV strain, adequate home support, no other reason to admit) can be treated as outpatients on a DOAC. The Hestia criteria are a practical checklist — if the answer to all is "no" (haemodynamically unstable, needs thrombolysis/embolectomy, active bleeding, O₂ to keep sats >90% for >24h, PE on anticoagulation, severe pain needing IV analgesia, social reason to admit, CrCl <30, severe liver impairment, pregnancy, HIT history), outpatient management is reasonable. HESTIA and the sPESI-based approach are validated and reduce admissions safely.

☠️ M&M — "normotensive, so I relaxed"

A 58-year-old with a submassive PE had a BP of 118/74 on arrival, so was placed on a general ward for "routine anticoagulation." His echo (RV:LV 1.4, positive troponin) was never chased — he was intermediate–high risk. Overnight he became hypotensive and arrested before thrombolysis could be given. Lesson: a normal blood pressure with a strained RV and a positive troponin is a warning label, not reassurance. Stratify fully and monitor these patients where they can be rescued.

8 · Take-home ladder

  1. Unstable? → High-risk → immediate reperfusion.
  2. Stable? → sPESI. Zero and no RV strain → low-risk, consider home.
  3. sPESI ≥1 → assess RV (echo/CT) + troponin.
  4. Both positive → intermediate–high → monitor closely, ready to rescue.
  5. Remember: the RV, not the clot, is the prognosis.
