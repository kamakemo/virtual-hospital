🧭 The dangerous secondary causes you exclude while diagnosing migraine

As you confirm migraine, you are simultaneously screening the can't-miss secondary headaches: subarachnoid haemorrhage (thunderclap), meningitis/encephalitis (fever + meningism), giant cell arteritis (age >50, jaw claudication, raised ESR), raised intracranial pressure / IIH / cerebral venous sinus thrombosis (papilloedema, postural, visual obscurations), and space-occupying lesions (progressive, focal signs). The screen is the SNNOOP10 mnemonic (see S14).

⚠️ The trap of "she always gets migraines"A known migraineur can still have a subarachnoid haemorrhage, a venous sinus thrombosis, or meningitis. The label of a chronic diagnosis is the commonest reason a dangerous new headache is dismissed. Always ask: is THIS attack different from your usual ones?

🧠 Teaching Points + Interactive Simulation

🔬 The mechanism — WHY the aura, WHY the throb (the WHY behind the WHY)

  1. 🌊 Cortical spreading depression (CSD): a slowly propagating wave of neuronal and glial depolarisation sweeps across the cortex at ~3 mm/min — this is the aura. The march of the scintillating scotoma across the visual field mirrors the wave crossing the occipital cortex.
  2. ⚡ Trigeminovascular activation: CSD activates trigeminal sensory afferents around meningeal blood vessels. This is the switch from "aura" to "pain."
  3. 🧬 CGRP release + neurogenic inflammation: activated trigeminal nerves release calcitonin gene-related peptide (CGRP) and other peptides → vasodilation, mast-cell degranulation, plasma extravasation and sensitisation of pain fibres — the throbbing, movement-sensitive headache.
  4. 🔁 Central sensitisation: sustained input sensitises the trigeminocervical complex and thalamus → cutaneous allodynia (the scalp hurts to touch) and photophobia/phonophobia. This is why early treatment, before sensitisation sets in, works better.
⚔️ ONE-LINER RULE"Aura is a wave (cortical spreading depression); pain is a peptide (CGRP-driven trigeminovascular inflammation). Treat EARLY, before central sensitisation locks the attack in."

⚖️ Contrast pair — Migraine vs Cluster (both unilateral, utterly different)

🌈 Migraine

  • Unilateral throbbing, 4–72 h, builds gradually
  • Nausea, photophobia, phonophobia, ± aura
  • Patient lies STILL in a dark room
  • Acute: oral triptan + NSAID

🥊 Cluster (a TAC)

  • Strictly unilateral orbital/temporal, excruciating, 15–180 min
  • Ipsilateral autonomic: tearing, red eye, ptosis, nasal congestion
  • Patient is RESTLESS, paces, cannot keep still
  • Acute: high-flow oxygen + subcut sumatriptan; verapamil for prevention

💡 The behaviour is the giveaway: migraine seeks stillness and darkness; cluster cannot sit still. Same "one-sided headache," opposite bedside picture and opposite acute treatment.

📋 Orders

🖊️ The order set (10:30)

  • 🌑 Dark, quiet room; IV access if vomiting; encourage oral fluids.
  • 💉 Acute migraine treatment: an oral triptan (e.g. sumatriptan 50–100 mg) PLUS an NSAID (e.g. aspirin 900 mg or naproxen); nasal/subcut triptan if vomiting.
  • 🤢 Anti-emetic (e.g. metoclopramide or prochlorperazine) — treats nausea and improves gastric absorption of oral drugs.
  • 🚩 Document the SNNOOP10 red-flag screen as negative — onset gradual, afebrile, exam normal, no papilloedema, no focal deficit, age <50.
  • 🧪 No routine neuroimaging for typical migraine with a normal exam; reserve CT/CT-angiogram/LP or MRI/MRV for red flags.
  • 📆 Preventive plan — offer prophylaxis given frequency + acute-medication overuse; start a headache diary.
  • ⚠️ Safety actions: stop the combined oestrogen contraceptive (migraine with aura); advise on medication-overuse; lifestyle (sleep, meals, hydration, screen breaks).
⚔️ ONE-LINER RULE"Treat migraine EARLY and hard: a triptan plus an NSAID plus an anti-emetic beats a triptan alone — and a dark quiet room is a real intervention, not a courtesy."
S8

👩‍⚕️ Nursing Care

🛏️ Ward/clinic priorities — the nurse makes the treatment work

  • 🌑 Environment: dim lights, quiet space, minimise stimulation — genuinely therapeutic in migraine.
  • 💊 Give acute medication early and by an appropriate route — if she is vomiting, oral triptans will not absorb; use nasal/subcut and an anti-emetic.
  • 😖 Reassess pain and nausea and note response to treatment; a poor response to a usually-effective regimen should prompt a re-look at the diagnosis (red flags).
  • 🚩 Escalate immediately if new features appear: sudden worsening, neck stiffness, fever, drowsiness, new focal weakness, or the "worst-ever" descriptor — these are red flags, not migraine.
  • 📓 Education: start a headache diary, explain triggers and the risk of overusing painkillers, and reinforce the pill-safety message.
  • 💧 Encourage hydration and rest; safeguard against a fall if she is dizzy from the aura or medication.
⚔️ ONE-LINER RULE"The nurse who notices that 'this attack is not responding like her usual migraine' is the tripwire that catches the secondary headache hiding behind the primary one."

S12

📟 Monitoring

📡 What to watch and why

  • 😖 Response to acute treatment — pain and nausea at intervals; a usually-effective regimen that fails should reopen the differential (secondary cause?).
  • 🚩 Evolving red flags — new fever, neck stiffness, drowsiness, focal deficit, or a change to "worst-ever"/sudden character = escalate and investigate.
  • 🌈 Aura characteristics — aura that is prolonged (>60 min), fixed, or with motor/brainstem features needs review (hemiplegic/brainstem migraine, or a mimic like TIA/CVST).
  • 📓 Headache diary — monthly headache days, acute-medication days (medication-overuse surveillance), triggers, and response to any preventive.
  • 💊 Preventive tolerability — mood/cognition on topiramate, bradycardia/BP on propranolol, sedation on amitriptyline.
⚔️ ONE-LINER RULE"The migraine that stops behaving like the patient's usual migraine is the one to investigate. 'Different' is the alarm word in headache medicine."
S13

⚠️ Complications

💀 What can go wrong — of the migraine, and of missing the mimic

  • 💊 Medication-overuse headache — the commonest complication of frequent acute treatment; converts episodic to chronic daily headache.
  • 🧠 Chronic migraine — ≥15 headache days/month for >3 months; disabling, often with overuse and comorbid depression/anxiety.
  • 🩸 Migrainous infarction — rare ischaemic stroke in the territory of a prolonged aura; risk magnified by smoking + combined oestrogen pill.
  • ⚡ Status migrainosus — a debilitating attack >72 h needing rescue therapy.
  • ☠️ Missed secondary headache — the catastrophic "complication": a thunderclap SAH discharged as migraine, a GCA that blinds, a meningitis that is not lumbar-punctured, a CVST in a pregnant/pill-using woman.
⚔️ ONE-LINER RULE"The worst complication of migraine is not the migraine — it is the secondary headache wearing its costume. Every atypical or red-flag attack earns a fresh, sceptical look."

S17

🏠 Discharge

📋 The migraine patient vs the red-flag headache — two different dispositions

🌈 This patient (typical migraine)

  • Acute plan (triptan + NSAID + anti-emetic, early, right route) and a preventive if frequent
  • Headache diary; trigger and lifestyle advice; medication-overuse counselling
  • Stop combined oestrogen pill (aura) → progestogen-only/non-hormonal method
  • Safety-net: return if a NEW, sudden, worst-ever, or different headache, fever, or focal symptoms

🚩 The red-flag headache

  • Any SNNOOP10 positive → the specific workup (CT/LP, ESR + steroids, MRI/MRV) BEFORE discharge
  • Do NOT discharge a thunderclap, a febrile meningism, or a possible GCA on an anti-emetic
  • Document the red-flag screen and the reasoning explicitly
⚔️ ONE-LINER RULE"Safe migraine discharge = typical pattern + normal exam + negative red-flag screen + explicit 'come back if it is ever different' advice. In migraine with aura, always deal with the oestrogen pill."

🔬 Advanced Topics — Fellowship Level

🧠 Beyond the pathway

  • 🧬 The CGRP revolution: monoclonal antibodies to CGRP or its receptor (erenumab, galcanezumab, fremanezumab, eptinezumab) for prevention, and the small-molecule gepants (rimegepant, ubrogepant, atogepant) for acute AND preventive use — a mechanism-targeted era replacing repurposed drugs.
  • 🌀 Ditans: lasmiditan, a selective 5-HT1F agonist with no vasoconstriction — an acute option for patients with cardiovascular contraindications to triptans.
  • 🧠 Aura variants: hemiplegic migraine (familial channelopathies — CACNA1A, ATP1A2, SCN1A), migraine with brainstem aura, and retinal migraine — each a mimic-magnet requiring exclusion of stroke/TIA/CVST before labelling.
  • 🌩️ Thunderclap beyond SAH: reversible cerebral vasoconstriction syndrome (RCVS — "string of beads" on angiography), cerebral venous sinus thrombosis, arterial dissection, pituitary apoplexy and spontaneous intracranial hypotension all present with sudden severe headache.
  • 👁️ Giant cell arteritis pathways: temporal artery ultrasound (halo sign) and fast-track GCA clinics reduce time-to-steroids and permanent visual loss; steroids first, biopsy within ~2 weeks.
  • 💉 Neuromodulation & procedures: greater occipital nerve blocks, non-invasive vagal/trigeminal stimulation, and — for chronic migraine — onabotulinumtoxinA (PREEMPT protocol).

🎭 Morbidity & Mortality Conference

🎭 M&M — "The Migraine That Was a Bleed"

The case: A 41-year-old known migraineur attended a busy ED with a severe headache that, she said, "came on like a thunderclap while I was at the gym — the worst I have ever had, and not like my migraines." She was triaged as "known migraine," given metoclopramide and an oral triptan in a side room, felt somewhat better, and was discharged after 90 minutes without a red-flag screen or imaging. No one asked about the speed of onset. Two days later she collapsed with a catastrophic rebleed from a ruptured aneurysm — the first event had been a subarachnoid haemorrhage. A CT within six hours would almost certainly have shown the blood.

🧀 The Swiss-cheese holes that lined up:

🧠 ANCHORING"Known migraine" label stuck; symptom relief from the anti-emetic falsely reassured.
💥 ONSET IGNOREDNobody asked the single most important question — how fast did it peak? Thunderclap was missed.
🚩 NO RED-FLAG SCREENSNNOOP10 was never applied; "different from my usual" was not acted on.
🧠 NO IMAGINGA CT within 6 h (highly sensitive for SAH) was never done.
🏥 SYSTEMCrowding + a chronic diagnosis label short-circuited the workup.

🛡️ What would have saved her: ask EVERY headache how fast it peaked and whether it is different from the usual pattern; treat a thunderclap or worst-ever headache as subarachnoid haemorrhage until CT (±LP) excludes it; never let symptomatic relief close the door; and apply SNNOOP10 to known migraineurs too. A missed GCA that blinds an eye is the same lesson in a different organ. This is the discipline that protected the patient in Bed 6.

⚔️ ONE-LINER RULE"'She always gets migraines' has killed patients who that day had a bleed. The headache history is not complete until you have asked how fast it peaked and whether it is DIFFERENT."
