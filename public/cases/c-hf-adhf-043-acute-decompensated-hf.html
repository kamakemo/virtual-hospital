🧭 The two things you decide at the bedside first

Before any scan you answer congestion and perfusion. Congestion = orthopnoea, PND, raised JVP, crackles, oedema, S3, weight gain. Perfusion = pulse pressure, peripheral temperature, mentation, urine output, lactate/creatinine. This patient is unambiguously warm and wet → the job is decongestion with IV loop diuretics, continue guideline therapy, and DO NOT give fluid.

⚠️ The trap of the "borderline" blood pressureA congested HF patient with a systolic of 100–110 mmHg is usually still warm and wet, not "dry and hypotensive." Chronic HFrEF patients live at low pressures. Do NOT reach for a fluid bolus — assess perfusion (feet, brain, pulse pressure, lactate). Fluid into a congested patient floods the lungs.

🧪 Resident's self-testThe BP dips to 106/72 after the first diuretic. A colleague suggests a 500 mL saline bolus "to protect the kidneys." Right call?

No. He is still warm and wet — warm feet, alert, good pulse pressure. A systolic of 106 in a chronic HFrEF patient is not "dry." Saline reverses the decongestion you just started and re-floods the lungs. If you're worried about perfusion, assess it directly (feet, mentation, lactate, urine output) — don't give reflex fluid. A small creatinine rise during effective decongestion is acceptable and expected.

🧠 Teaching Points + Interactive Simulation

🔬 The mechanism — WHY he's congested (the WHY behind the WHY)

  1. 💔 Failing pump, falling output: the damaged LV can't maintain cardiac output → the body senses "underfilling."
  2. 🧬 Neurohormonal activation: RAAS (angiotensin II, aldosterone), the sympathetic nervous system, and vasopressin (ADH) switch on → sodium and water retention + venoconstriction.
  3. 🌊 Rising filling pressures: retained volume and venoconstriction shift blood centrally → left- and right-heart filling pressures climb (↑LVEDP, ↑wedge, ↑RA pressure).
  4. 🫁 Congestion — backward failure: high left-sided pressures → pulmonary congestion (orthopnoea, PND, crackles); high right-sided pressures → systemic congestion (raised JVP, oedema, hepatic congestion, ascites). The S3 is the sound of a volume-overloaded ventricle.
  5. ⚠️ The vicious spiral: congestion itself worsens renal and myocardial function (venous congestion → ↑renal venous pressure → ↓GFR = "cardiorenal") — which is why decongestion, not fluid, protects the kidney here.
⚔️ ONE-LINER RULE"Acute heart failure is mostly a plumbing problem of PRESSURE, not a pump that needs more filling. The lungs and legs are wet because pressures are high — so you lower pressure and volume, you don't add to it."

⚖️ Contrast pair — Congestion (backward) vs Hypoperfusion (forward)

🌊 Congestion (backward failure)

  • Orthopnoea, PND, raised JVP, crackles, oedema, S3, weight gain
  • High filling pressures
  • Treat: decongest — IV loop diuretic ± vasodilator

🥶 Hypoperfusion (forward failure)

  • Cool mottled peripheries, narrow pulse pressure, drowsiness, oliguria, rising lactate/creatinine
  • Low cardiac output
  • Treat: restore perfusion — inotrope/vasopressor ± mechanical support, then decongest

💡 A patient can have BOTH (cold & wet). The mistake is treating congestion with fluid, or treating hypoperfusion with diuretic alone.

👩‍⚕️ Nursing Care

🛏️ Ward priorities — the nurse runs the decongestion safety net

  • ⚖️ Daily weights, same time, same scales — the truest decongestion trend; a falling weight is success, a rising one is failure.
  • 🚽 Strict fluid balance & hourly urine output post-diuretic — flag if output < 100 mL/h (the loop dose may be too small).
  • 🧪 Send the 2-hour spot urine sodium exactly on time; label accurately (the whole titration decision hinges on it).
  • 🌬️ Sit the patient upright; monitor RR, SpO₂ and work of breathing; escalate for NIV if tiring — don't wait for a crash.
  • 💉 Confirm GDMT is continued — challenge any order that reflexively "holds the beta-blocker"; ensure the SGLT2i and ARNI/MRA are given.
  • 🩸 Watch K⁺, Mg²⁺, Na⁺ and renal function on diuresis; report symptomatic hypotension, cramps or rising creatinine to the team (a small rise is expected).
  • 🍽️ Reinforce fluid (~1.5 L) and salt restriction; begin patient education early — this is where readmissions are prevented.
⚔️ ONE-LINER RULE"In heart failure the scales and the urine jug are the two most honest monitors on the ward — a nurse tracking weight and output catches under-diuresis before the patient re-floods."

📟 Monitoring

📡 What to watch and why

  • ⚖️ Daily weight + strict fluid balance — the primary decongestion endpoint; aim for a net negative balance and steady weight loss to dry weight.
  • 🚽 Hourly urine output + the 2-hour urine sodium — grade the diuretic response early; escalate the dose if output/natriuresis is poor.
  • 🩸 U&E, K⁺, Mg²⁺ & creatinine daily (or more) — diuresis drops potassium/magnesium; a small creatinine rise is acceptable, a steep one prompts reassessment of volume/perfusion.
  • 🫁 SpO₂, RR and work of breathing — escalate to NIV if tiring; congestion score / recheck lung B-lines.
  • 📈 Continuous telemetry — AF rate control and arrhythmia; reassess perfusion (feet, mentation, pulse pressure, lactate) — a warm patient turning cold is the alarm.
⚔️ ONE-LINER RULE"Weight and urine output tell you if you're winning; potassium and creatinine tell you the cost. Watch all four and the patient dries out safely."
S13

⚠️ Complications

💀 What can go wrong in the acute HF phase

  • 🫁 Flash / acute pulmonary oedema & respiratory failure — worsened by fluid, under-diuresis, or uncontrolled hypertension; may need NIV or intubation.
  • 🥶 Progression to cardiogenic shock (warm-wet → cold-wet) — hypoperfusion, rising lactate/creatinine, needing inotropes/mechanical support.
  • 💧 Over-diuresis → intravascular depletion, hypotension, worsening (pre-renal) AKI — the mirror-image error to under-diuresis.
  • 🧂 Electrolyte & metabolic: hypokalaemia/hypomagnesaemia (→ arrhythmia), hyponatraemia, metabolic alkalosis (contraction).
  • 🫀 Arrhythmia: fast AF driving decompensation; VT/VF risk in ischaemic HFrEF.
  • 🔁 Diuretic resistance & cardiorenal syndrome — the response wanes; needs sequential nephron blockade.
  • 🚪 The system complication — residual congestion at discharge: the biggest driver of 30-day readmission and death.
⚔️ ONE-LINER RULE"The two ways to fail at decongestion are opposite: leave them WET (readmission) or make them DRY-and-empty (AKI, hypotension). Aim for euvolaemia, and let weight, output and perfusion steer you between the ditches."

🗺️ Plan + Procedure Guides with Checklists

🧭 The management plan

  1. 💧 Diagnosis: acute decompensated HFrEF, warm & wet (Stevenson B), hypertensive phenotype, triggered by diuretic non-adherence + salt + fast AF.
  2. 💊 Decongest: IV loop ≥ 2.5× home dose; grade the 2-h urine sodium/output; if poor → double dose, then sequential nephron blockade (thiazide/acetazolamide/SGLT2i). IV nitrate for BP/afterload.
  3. 💉 Continue & uptitrate GDMT (ARNI, beta-blocker, MRA, SGLT2i); rate-control the AF; treat the trigger.
  4. 🏠 Before discharge: confirm euvolaemia, four pillars started/uptitrated, education, and early follow-up (STRONG-HF) within 1–2 weeks.
⚔️ ONE-LINER RULE"Admission is for decongestion; discharge is for GDMT. A patient sent home wet, or on last-year's tablets, is a readmission you built yourself."

📋 The two ends of the HF admission — done right vs done wrong

✅ This patient (decongested + optimised)

  • Euvolaemic at dry weight, on oral diuretic at the right dose
  • Four pillars: ARNI, bisoprolol, spironolactone, dapagliflozin — uptitrated
  • Daily weights, salt/fluid restriction, adherence plan, IV iron given
  • Red flags: >2 kg gain in 2–3 days, worse breathlessness, syncope → call
  • HF-nurse + cardiology follow-up within 1–2 weeks (STRONG-HF); AF/anticoagulation reviewed

🟥 The dangerous discharge

  • Still congested (raised JVP, residual crackles/oedema) — "sent home a bit early"
  • Beta-blocker left held; GDMT never restarted or uptitrated
  • No education, no early follow-up, no weight-monitoring plan
  • Predictably readmitted within 30 days — or worse
⚔️ ONE-LINER RULE"A safe HF discharge is euvolaemia + all four pillars + education + early follow-up — never 'breathing a bit better, send him home.' Residual congestion is a readmission with a date on it."

💎 Clinical Pearls

💧 Warm & wet = decongestMost ADHF is congested with good perfusion — treat with IV diuretics, not fluid, and never a bolus for a low-ish BP.
🧭 Two questionsCongestion (wet/dry?) and perfusion (warm/cold?) place the Stevenson quadrant and choose therapy at the bedside.
💊 2.5× ruleStart IV loop at ≥ 2.5× the home oral dose (DOSE); bolus and infusion are equivalent.
🧪 Urine sodium at 2 h< 50–70 mmol/L = poor response → double the dose, then sequential nephron blockade.
🚫 Don't stop the beta-blockerContinue unless cardiogenic shock (B-CONVINCED) — stopping it costs lives.
🩸 Creatinine will nudge upA small rise during effective decongestion is the price of getting dry, not a reason to stop.
🏛️ Four pillarsARNI/ACEi + beta-blocker + MRA + SGLT2i — start/uptitrate in-hospital (EMPULSE, STRONG-HF).
📅 Get dry, then get followedEuvolaemia + GDMT + education + early review prevents the 30-day bounce-back.

🔬 Advanced Topics — Fellowship Level

🧠 Beyond the pathway

  • 🧭 Haemodynamic phenotyping: the Forrester classification (cardiac index vs pulmonary capillary wedge pressure) and its bedside surrogate, the Stevenson warm/cold–wet/dry quadrants. Congestion (wet) drives symptoms and readmission; hypoperfusion (cold) drives mortality. Phenotype first, treat second.
  • 🧪 Natriuretic peptides & the "obese BNP" trap: BNP/NT-proBNP reflect wall stress with high NPV; they are lowered in obesity (a deceptively "normal" value in a large breathless patient) and raised by age, AF, renal impairment and PE. Use trends and clinical context; consider imaging (lung/echo POCUS) to confirm congestion.
  • 💊 Diuretic pharmacology & resistance: loops have a threshold/ceiling and steep dose–response; braking, distal-tubule hypertrophy and reduced delivery cause resistance. Combat with adequate dosing, continuous infusion in resistance, and sequential nephron blockade (thiazide/metolazone — CLOROTIC; acetazolamide — ADVOR; SGLT2i — EMPULSE).
  • 🧫 Spot urine sodium-guided diuresis: a 1–2 h post-dose urine Na⁺ < 50–70 mmol/L (and/or output < 100–150 mL/h) defines inadequate response → escalate now rather than waiting for daily weights (2021 ESC decongestion algorithm).
  • 💉 Vasodilators in hypertensive AHF: IV nitrates reduce preload/afterload and filling pressure in the "wet & hypertensive"/flash pulmonary oedema phenotype; benefit is haemodynamic/symptomatic (GALACTIC/large RCTs did not show a hard-outcome mortality benefit from routine early high-dose vasodilators).
  • 🧬 When newer agents come in: start/continue SGLT2i during admission (EMPULSE); vericiguat (soluble guanylate cyclase stimulator, VICTORIA) for HFrEF with a recent worsening event; consider IV iron (AFFIRM-AHF/IRONMAN) for iron deficiency to reduce readmissions.
  • 🥶 Cardiogenic-shock recognition & escalation: the cold-and-wet patient — narrowing pulse pressure, mottling, oliguria, rising lactate/creatinine — needs inotropes/vasopressors and consideration of mechanical support; note CULPRIT-SHOCK (culprit-only PCI) and the disappointing routine-IABP data (IABP-SHOCK II). SCAI shock stages A–E frame escalation.

🎭 Morbidity & Mortality Conference

🎭 M&M — "The Litre of Saline That Drowned Him"

The case: A 72-year-old man with known HFrEF (EF 25%) came in breathless, unable to lie flat, JVP up to the jaw, crackles to mid-zones, legs swollen — but with a systolic of 100/70. The covering doctor labelled him "a bit dry and hypotensive", prescribed a 1-litre saline bolus "to support the pressure", and stopped his bisoprolol "to be safe". His feet were warm and he was fully alert the whole time — he was warm and wet, not dry. Over the next few hours he flooded: SpO₂ fell into the 70s, he developed frank pulmonary oedema, and he was intubated overnight. In the churn of the transfer his beta-blocker was never restarted. He decompensated further, developed cardiorenal shock, and died on day 3.

🧀 The Swiss-cheese holes that lined up:

🧭 WRONG PHENOTYPE"Dry & hypotensive" label on a warm, alert, congested patient — he was warm-and-wet.
💧 FLUID INTO CONGESTIONA saline bolus for a "borderline" BP re-flooded already high filling pressures.
🚫 BETA-BLOCKER STOPPEDHeld reflexively (against B-CONVINCED) — and then never restarted.
🔎 NO PERFUSION CHECKWarm feet, good pulse pressure, clear sensorium were never used to read perfusion.
🩺 DECONGESTION DELAYEDThe one thing he needed — a proper IV diuretic — was displaced by fluid.

🛡️ What would have saved him: read the two axes — warm feet + alert + good pulse pressure = perfused; raised JVP + crackles + oedema = congested → warm and wet. Give an adequate IV loop diuretic, a vasodilator for the pressure, and continue the beta-blocker; never bolus fluid into a congested patient for a low-ish systolic; and if a beta-blocker ever is reduced, document a plan to restart it. This is exactly the discipline that dried out the patient in Bed 1.

⚔️ ONE-LINER RULE"'A bit dry and hypotensive' has drowned more congested patients than it has ever rescued. In warm-and-wet heart failure the treatment is decongestion — never a fluid bolus, and never a stopped beta-blocker."
