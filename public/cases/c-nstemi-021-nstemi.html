🧬 Why THIS patient? — non-occlusive thrombus on a vulnerable plaque

NSTE-ACS is a partially or intermittently occluding coronary thrombus — or a severe fixed stenosis with rising demand — sitting on a ruptured or eroded atherosclerotic plaque. Unlike a STEMI (a fully occluded artery transmurally starving its territory), here flow is reduced but not abolished. The result is subendocardial ischemia: the innermost layer of myocardium is the watershed, furthest from the coronary supply and last to be perfused, so it suffers first. That subendocardial injury produces ST depression and T-wave inversion rather than the ST elevation of a transmural (full-thickness) infarct. If enough myocytes die, troponin rises and falls — that makes it NSTEMI; if the troponin stays negative with the same picture, it is unstable angina. Tonight, diabetes, smoking and untreated lipids built the plaque; today it cracked.

🔥 Risk factors / drivers of NSTE-ACS 🩸 Diabetes · 🚬 Smoking · 🧈 Dyslipidemia · 🫀 Hypertension · 🧬 Family history of premature CAD · 👴 Older age · 🧫 Chronic kidney disease · ⚙️ Prior CAD/PCI/CABG · 🔥 Anything that raises myocardial demand or lowers supply (anemia, tachyarrhythmia, sepsis, hypoxia → also drives type 2 MI)

🤔 "Should we give thrombolysis since we can't get to cath immediately?"

"No — and this is a classic, dangerous error. Fibrinolysis has no benefit in NSTE-ACS and only adds bleeding risk. The thrombus here is platelet-rich and non-occlusive; lytics are for the fibrin-rich, fully occlusive clot of a STEMI when timely PCI isn't available. In NSTEMI the answer to 'we can't cath right now' is optimal antithrombotic therapy and an early-invasive plan, not a lytic."

🧪 Resident's self-test (answer before reading on) A colleague wants to send a comfortable-looking NSTE-ACS patient home from the obs unit because "the troponin's only mildly up and there's no ST elevation." Safe?

No. A rising-and-falling troponin with ischemic symptoms is an MI regardless of how well he looks, and ST depression can be the only surface sign of a STEMI-equivalent (posterior MI, de Winter, Wellens, left-main pattern with aVR elevation). Discharging him risks completing a large infarct or a sudden occlusion. He stays, gets the full bundle, and is risk-stratified for early angiography.

"Before I accept a 24-hour plan, I want the ECG re-read for the patterns that look like NSTEMI but are actually occlusions: posterior MI (ST depression V1–V3 with a tall R wave — get posterior leads V7–V9 looking for ST elevation); de Winter T waves (upsloping ST depression with tall, symmetric T waves at the J point — a proximal LAD occlusion equivalent); Wellens syndrome (deep or biphasic T waves in V2–V3, often pain-free, = critical proximal LAD); and diffuse ST depression with ST elevation in aVR (left main or severe triple-vessel disease). 📖 Teaching Points

🔬 The mechanism — WHY subendocardial ischemia gives ST DEPRESSION (the WHY behind the WHY)

  1. 🧱 The substrate: a lipid-rich atherosclerotic plaque ruptures or erodes, exposing thrombogenic material. Platelets aggregate and a non-occlusive (or intermittently occlusive) thrombus forms — flow is reduced, not abolished.
  2. 🧅 The watershed suffers first: the subendocardium — the innermost layer — sits furthest from the epicardial coronary supply, has the highest wall stress, and is perfused last. When supply falls, it is the first to go ischemic.
  3. 📉 Why ST depression, not elevation: ischemia of only the inner layer creates a repolarization gradient pointing away from the overlying leads → ST depression and T-wave inversion. A transmural (full-thickness) injury of a STEMI points toward the leads → ST elevation. Depth of injury = direction of the ST segment.
  4. 🧪 Why troponin rises and falls: if enough subendocardial myocytes die, troponin leaks, peaks, then clears — a dynamic rise-and-fall defines acute MI. The same ECG with a negative troponin is unstable angina; a flat chronically high troponin (CKD, HF) is a baseline, not an event.
  5. 💥 Why it still kills: the non-occlusive clot can propagate to full occlusion at any moment, the ischemic subendocardium is electrically unstable (arrhythmia), and an unrecognized STEMI-equivalent can complete a large infarct silently. NSTEMI is not "the safe MI."
⚔️ ONE-LINER RULE"Depth decides direction: subendocardial ischemia depresses the ST segment, transmural ischemia elevates it. A rise-and-fall troponin turns ischemia into infarction."

⚖️ Contrast pair #1 — NSTEMI vs STEMI vs Unstable Angina

📉 NSTEMI / NSTE-ACS (this patient)

  • ST depression / T-wave inversion; no persistent ST elevation
  • Non-/intermittently-occlusive thrombus
  • Troponin rises and falls (positive)
  • Treat now; invasive timing is risk-based; NO fibrinolysis

📈 STEMI & 🫀 Unstable Angina

  • STEMI: persistent ST elevation, occlusive clot, immediate reperfusion (PCI or lysis)
  • Unstable angina: same ischemic symptoms/ECG as NSTEMI but troponin negative — ischemia without detectable necrosis
  • The troponin is the dividing line between UA and NSTEMI

😈 The trap: "no ST elevation" gets equated with "low acuity," so therapy is delayed and STEMI-equivalents are missed. The cost of treating an NSTEMI too casually (a completed infarct, a missed left-main) far exceeds the cost of being appropriately aggressive. Treat early, stratify properly, and never lyse.

⚖️ Contrast pair #2 — Type 1 vs Type 2 MI

🧱 Type 1 MI (plaque event)

  • Atherothrombosis: plaque rupture/erosion + thrombus
  • The classic ACS — antiplatelets, anticoagulation, angiography
  • This patient's likely mechanism

⚖️ Type 2 MI (supply–demand)

  • Ischemia from a demand/supply mismatch without acute plaque rupture
  • Triggers: anemia, sepsis, tachyarrhythmia, hypoxia, hypotension, severe hypertension
  • Treat the underlying driver first; routine antiplatelet/cath is not automatic

💡 Why it matters: not every troponin rise needs a cath lab. A fast AF in an anemic septic patient can lift the troponin through pure demand ischemia — fix the anemia, the sepsis and the rate, and the troponin settles. Mistaking type 2 for type 1 leads to unnecessary anticoagulation and angiography; mistaking type 1 for type 2 misses a culprit lesion.

🔬 Fellowship Corner — Advanced Topics

  • ⏱️ High-sensitivity troponin pathways: 0/1-h and 0/2-h algorithms rapidly rule-in/rule-out; the delta (rise/fall) matters more than a single value, distinguishing acute injury from chronic elevation.
  • 💊 Antiplatelet nuance: ticagrelor/prasugrel vs clopidogrel; prasugrel avoided after prior stroke/TIA; the debate over pre-treatment with a P2Y12 inhibitor before known coronary anatomy in NSTE-ACS.
  • 🩸 Anticoagulant choice: fondaparinux (best efficacy–safety, give additional UFH at PCI to avoid catheter thrombus), enoxaparin, or UFH; bivalirudin in selected PCI.
  • 🕳️ STEMI-equivalents & "OMI" thinking: the occlusion-MI paradigm reframes posterior MI, de Winter, Wellens and aVR-elevation patterns as occlusions needing emergent cath despite no classic ST elevation.
  • 🧫 MINOCA (MI with non-obstructive coronary arteries): plaque disruption, coronary spasm, spontaneous coronary dissection (SCAD), microvascular disease, embolism — a workup, not a dismissal.
  • ⚖️ Bleeding–ischemia balance: ARC-HBR criteria, radial access, shortened or de-escalated DAPT in high-bleeding-risk patients; weigh GRACE (ischemia) against bleeding risk.

👩‍⚕️ Nursing Care

🛏️ CCU nursing priorities — hour by hour

  • 📟 Continuous telemetry with the defibrillator at the bedside. NSTE-ACS carries arrhythmia risk; record and report any new ectopy, VT, or conduction change.
  • 📈 Repeat the 12-lead with every change in pain and at set intervals — ST depression is dynamic; a new pattern (or a STEMI-equivalent) changes everything. Get posterior leads V7–V9 if asked.
  • 💢 Track and score the chest pain (site, severity, response to nitrate). Refractory or recurrent pain despite therapy is a very-high-risk feature — escalate, don't normalize it.
  • 🩸 BP and HR regularly; nitrates and beta-blockers can drop the pressure — hold and report if hypotensive. Note any recent PDE5 inhibitor before giving nitrates.
  • 💊 Give the antithrombotic bundle accurately (aspirin, P2Y12, anticoagulant) and watch for bleeding — gums, puncture sites, stool, neuro changes; the more potent the regimen, the higher the bleeding risk.
  • 🧪 Draw serial troponins on time — the rise-and-fall is the diagnosis; mistimed samples blur it.
  • 🫁 Only give oxygen if SpO₂ < 90%; routine oxygen in normoxic ACS is not helpful.
  • 🧭 Prepare for the cath lab: consent support, NPO status, IV access, groin/wrist prep, allergy and renal function flagged for contrast.
  • 👨‍👩‍👦 Keep the patient and family informed (see S16) — explain the "treat now, then angiogram" plan; fear and pain raise demand.

📈 ECG / Imaging

🫀 The presenting ECG — read it like a detective

Sinus rhythm with horizontal/down-sloping ST depression and T-wave inversion in the lateral leads, and no persistent ST elevation. These are the surface marks of subendocardial ischemia — the signature of NSTE-ACS. The job now is to confirm the rise-and-fall troponin and to exclude a STEMI-equivalent hiding in this depression.

🔎 The "is this really just NSTEMI?" toolkit — hunt the STEMI-equivalents

PatternWhy it's an emergency disguised as 'ST depression'
🪞 Posterior MIST depression V1–V3 with a tall R wave (and upright T) = the mirror image of posterior ST elevation. Confirm with posterior leads V7–V9 (ST elevation there). A true posterior STEMI needs emergent cath — usually an occluded circumflex.
🌀 de Winter T wavesUpsloping ST depression at the J point with tall, symmetric T waves in the precordials = a proximal LAD occlusion equivalent. No classic ST elevation, but it is an occlusion — emergent cath.
🪝 Wellens syndromeDeep or biphasic T waves in V2–V3, often when pain-free, = critical proximal LAD stenosis. Do not stress-test; angiography — a widow-maker is waiting.
🆘 aVR elevation + diffuse ST depressionST elevation in aVR with widespread ST depression elsewhere suggests left main or severe triple-vessel disease — high-risk, often urgent revascularization.
📐 Dynamic / transient changesST depression that comes and goes with pain confirms active ischemia and raises the risk tier; capture it on serial/continuous ECG.
🧭 Compare with a prior ECGNew ST/T changes vs an old baseline (e.g. LVH strain, prior infarct) change the meaning — always seek the comparison tracing.
⚔️ ONE-LINER RULE"ST depression is a question, not an answer. Tall R in V1–V3 → do posterior leads; tall T at the J point → de Winter; deep T in V2–V3 → Wellens; aVR up → left main. Any 'yes' and the morning cath becomes a midnight one."

🫀 Adjunctive imaging

  • 📈 Posterior & right-sided leads: V7–V9 for posterior MI (given anterior ST depression); V3R–V4R if inferior involvement appears.
  • 🩻 Bedside echo: regional wall-motion abnormality supports a culprit territory and assesses LV function; screens for mechanical complications and mimics (pericardial effusion, RV strain of PE, aortic root in dissection).
  • 🫀 Coronary angiography: the definitive test and treatment route — defines the culprit, vessel anatomy and guides PCI vs CABG. Timing is the whole point of risk stratification.
  • 🧲 CT coronary / cardiac MRI (selected): when the diagnosis is uncertain or MINOCA is suspected after a non-obstructive angiogram.
⚠️ The ECG's favorite lie in NSTE-ACS"There's no ST elevation, so the artery isn't occluded." Posterior MI, de Winter and Wellens all can represent occlusion or imminent occlusion with little or no classic ST elevation. The reassuring read is only earned after you've looked for these patterns and, where relevant, recorded posterior leads.

🚫 The 'never / careful' list in NSTE-ACS❌ Fibrinolysis (no benefit, net harm in NSTE-ACS) · ❌ Routine oxygen if SpO₂ ≥ 90% · ⚠️ Nitrates with hypotension / RV infarct / recent PDE5 inhibitor · ⚠️ Early IV beta-blocker in acute heart failure or shock · ⚠️ NSAIDs (pro-thrombotic, increase events) · ⚠️ Two anticoagulants at once (bleeding). When unsure, optimize antithrombotics and escalate the invasive timing, don't lyse.

🏠 Secondary prevention (this admission, not just tonight)

  • 💙 DAPT (aspirin + P2Y12) typically ~12 months, individualized by ischemic vs bleeding risk.
  • 🧈 High-intensity statin ± ezetimibe/PCSK9 to LDL < 55 mg/dL.
  • 🫀 Beta-blocker, ACEi/ARB (especially with LV dysfunction/diabetes), aldosterone antagonist if EF low.
  • 🩸 Aggressive risk-factor control: smoking cessation (the single highest-yield action here), diabetes and BP optimization, cardiac rehabilitation.
S12

📟 Monitoring

📡 What to watch and why

  • 📈 Continuous telemetry with the defibrillator nearby — ischemic myocardium is arrhythmogenic; watch for VT/VF and conduction disturbance.
  • 📟 Serial / continuous ST-segment monitoring and a fresh 12-lead with every change in pain — ST depression is dynamic and a new pattern (or STEMI-equivalent) re-times the cath.
  • 🧪 Serial high-sensitivity troponin on protocol (0/1-h or 0/2-h) — the rise-and-fall confirms NSTEMI and the magnitude informs prognosis.
  • 🩸 BP and HR through nitrate/beta-blocker titration; hypotension means hold and reassess.
  • 🩹 Bleeding surveillance on DAPT + anticoagulation — access sites, GI, neuro; serial hemoglobin.
  • 💢 Pain trajectory — recurrent/refractory pain is a very-high-risk feature that escalates to immediate angiography.
  • 🫀 Renal function before and after contrast (pre-existing CKD here); plan hydration and contrast-minimizing strategy.
⚔️ ONE-LINER RULE"After you've treated NSTE-ACS, the monitor's two jobs are: 'is the troponin still rising?' and 'is the pain coming back?' — either one trending the wrong way moves the cath earlier."
S13

⚠️ Complications

💀 What can go wrong — and the mechanism

  • 📈 Progression to complete occlusion / STEMI — the non-occlusive thrombus propagates; recurrent or escalating pain with new ST elevation is the warning. Pivot to emergent reperfusion.
  • ⚡ Ventricular arrhythmia / sudden death — ischemic, electrically unstable myocardium; the reason for continuous monitoring and a defibrillator nearby.
  • 💔 Heart failure / cardiogenic shock — large or recurrent infarction lowers EF; a very-high-risk feature mandating immediate angiography.
  • 🫀 Mechanical complications (later, but devastating) — papillary muscle rupture (acute MR), VSD, free-wall rupture; a new murmur or sudden decompensation demands urgent echo.
  • 🩸 Bleeding from therapy — DAPT + anticoagulation + access-site complications; balance against ischemic risk (ARC-HBR, radial access).
  • 🧫 Missed STEMI-equivalent — a posterior MI, de Winter or Wellens pattern dismissed as "just ST depression" completes a large infarct silently. The classic, preventable catastrophe.

🎭 Morbidity & Mortality Conference — "The ST Depression That Was Really an Occlusion"

The case: A 64-year-old man arrived at 23:30 with chest pain and an ECG showing ST depression in V1–V3 with prominent R waves. The night team read it as "NSTEMI, troponin pending — admit, cardiology in the morning." Posterior leads were never recorded. He was given aspirin and admitted; his pain eased by 03:00 ("settling, reassuring"). The morning troponin was sharply elevated and the angiogram showed a fully occluded circumflex — a posterior STEMI that had completed overnight. The pain "settled" because the muscle had died. He left hospital with a low EF and chronic heart failure. (A parallel version: Wellens' deep V2–V3 T waves dismissed and stress-tested, with a widow-maker LAD occlusion days later.)

🧀 The Swiss-cheese holes that lined up:

🧠 ANCHORING"No ST elevation = not urgent" — ST depression V1–V3 + tall R is a posterior STEMI mirror.
📈 LEADS NOT DONEPosterior leads V7–V9 were never recorded — the diagnostic test was skipped.
⏱️ TIMING IGNORED"In the morning" deferred a cath that should have been emergent for an occlusion.
🫥 'PAIN SETTLED' MISREADResolving pain was infarct completion, not improvement.
🌙 SYSTEMNight cover, no senior review, no STEMI-equivalent checklist.

🛡️ What would have saved him: treat ST depression as a question — tall R in V1–V3 → record posterior leads; recognize posterior MI, de Winter, Wellens and aVR-elevation as occlusion-equivalents needing emergent cath; risk-stratify with GRACE and escalate timing for refractory/very-high-risk features; never reassure on "pain settled" in an MI; get senior eyes early. This is exactly why you re-read Mr. A.K.'s ECG for an equivalent before settling on a 24-hour plan.

⚔️ ONE-LINER RULE"'It's only ST depression' is written on the chart of patients who then declare a completed posterior STEMI by morning. ST depression earns a hunt, not a shrug."

💎 Clinical Pearls

📉 Depth decides directionSubendocardial ischemia → ST depression / T inversion; transmural ischemia → ST elevation. NSTE-ACS is the inner-layer injury.
🧪 Rise-and-fall = MIA dynamic rising-and-falling troponin with symptoms is an acute MI; troponin-negative with the same picture is unstable angina.
🫥 Flat ≠ acuteA chronically elevated, non-changing troponin (CKD, heart failure) is a baseline, not tonight's event. Don't anticoagulate a flat number.
🚫 No thrombolysisNSTE-ACS gets no fibrinolysis — no benefit, net harm. The lytic target is the occlusive STEMI clot.
🧮 GRACE times the cathGRACE > 140 (with dynamic troponin/ECG) = high risk → angiography within 24 h. Very-high-risk features → immediate.
🕳️ Hunt STEMI-equivalentsST depression isn't always "just NSTEMI" — look for posterior MI, de Winter, Wellens and aVR elevation; they need emergent cath.
🪞 Posterior workupST depression V1–V3 with a tall R → record posterior leads V7–V9; ST elevation there = posterior STEMI.
⚖️ Type 1 vs Type 2Type 1 = plaque event (treat the clot). Type 2 = demand ischemia (anemia, sepsis, tachyarrhythmia, hypoxia) — treat the driver.
🫥 'No ST elevation' ≠ low riskNSTEMI carries serious mortality. The decision is timing of reperfusion, not whether to act.
💊 Treat the whole bundleAntiplatelet + anticoagulant + anti-ischemic + high-intensity statin — and risk-stratify. Each part earns its place.
🩸 Balance bleedingPotent DAPT + anticoagulation save myocardium but cause bleeding — weigh GRACE against bleeding risk (radial access, HBR).
🫥 Settling pain can be infarct completionPain that "settles" in an evolving MI may mean the muscle died — never falsely reassuring.
