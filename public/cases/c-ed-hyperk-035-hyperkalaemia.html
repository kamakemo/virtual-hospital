🧭 The immediate question at the bedside

Before anything else you ask: are there ECG changes of hyperkalaemia? If yes — peaked T waves, wide QRS, lost P waves, or a sine wave — this is severe hyperkalaemia with cardiac toxicity and the treatment clock is now measured in minutes, not the hour it takes a formal potassium to return. Give IV calcium now.

⚠️ The trap of "wait for the confirmed lab potassium"A haemolysed sample can falsely raise potassium (pseudohyperkalaemia — see S20), but you cannot afford to wait for a repeat while a real sine wave evolves. When the ECG shows hyperkalaemic changes in a plausible patient, treat first, confirm in parallel. A point-of-care venous gas gives you a potassium in ~60 seconds.

👨‍⚕️ Resident Review

The EM resident thinks out loud at 14:43:

🧠 Frame it as three jobs, in order — STABILISE, SHIFT, REMOVE

"K⁺ 7.9 with a wide QRS and tented T waves — that's severe hyperkalaemia with cardiac toxicity. My job is three distinct tasks, in strict order: (1) STABILISE the myocardium with IV calcium right now — it protects the heart within minutes but does NOT lower the potassium. (2) SHIFT potassium into cells with insulin + dextrose (and nebulised salbutamol). (3) REMOVE potassium from the body — this anuric patient needs dialysis; binders are too slow to rely on alone. And I stop his ramipril and spironolactone."

🎯 Her order of operations

  1. 🛡️ IV calcium gluconate 10% 10–30 mL (or calcium chloride via a good/central line) over a few minutes — repeat if ECG changes persist after 5 minutes.
  2. 💉 Insulin 10 units actrapid + 25 g dextrose IV to shift K⁺ into cells; check glucose and monitor for hypoglycaemia for hours afterwards.
  3. 💨 Salbutamol 10–20 mg nebulised as an additional shift (additive to insulin).
  4. ☎️ Call renal/ICU for urgent haemodialysis — the only reliable way to REMOVE potassium in an anuric patient.
  5. 🚫 Stop ramipril, spironolactone, any K supplements/salt substitute; review all K-raising drugs.
⚔️ ONE-LINER RULE"Calcium FIRST — it doesn't lower the potassium, it saves the heart while you do. Insulin HIDES potassium; dialysis REMOVES it."
🧪 Resident's self-testShe is tempted to give the insulin-dextrose first because "that's what actually lowers the potassium." Do the calcium and insulin order matter?

Yes — calcium goes first when there are ECG changes. Insulin takes 10–20 minutes to start shifting potassium and hours to move much of it, while the myocardium is unprotected in the meantime. IV calcium antagonises the membrane effect within 1–3 minutes and buys the time the shift needs. Giving insulin without calcium first leaves the heart exposed exactly when a sine wave can degenerate to VF. Order: calcium → shift → remove.

🧠 Teaching Points + Interactive Simulation

🔬 The mechanism — what the potassium is doing to the myocyte (the WHY behind the WHY)

  1. 🔋 Resting membrane potential rises: the resting potential of a cardiac myocyte is set almost entirely by the potassium gradient across the membrane (the Nernst equation for K⁺). Raising extracellular K⁺ shrinks that gradient, so the resting potential becomes less negative (moves from ~−90 mV toward −70 mV).
  2. 🚪 Fast sodium channels inactivate: voltage-gated Na⁺ channels sit at a more depolarised, partially inactivated state when the cell rests less negative. Fewer channels are available to open.
  3. 🐌 Depolarisation & conduction slow: with fewer Na channels firing, phase 0 upstroke is smaller and slower — conduction through atria, AV node and ventricle drags. This is the electrical basis of every ECG change.
  4. 📈 The ECG marches: altered repolarisation gives tall, tented T waves first → then PR prolongation and flattening/loss of P waves (atrial tissue is exquisitely sensitive) → then QRS widening → the widened QRS merges with the T wave into a sine wave → VF or asystole.
🔁 The vicious cycle to never forgetHigh K⁺ → slowed conduction → bradycardia & hypoperfusion → acidosis → potassium shifts OUT of cells (H⁺/K⁺ exchange) → even higher extracellular K⁺ → worse conduction. The spiral tightens toward asystole. Calcium (stabilise) + insulin/salbutamol (shift) + dialysis (remove) break the cycle at three different points.
⚔️ ONE-LINER RULE"A sine-wave ECG is minutes from asystole — give calcium before you finish reading the gas."

⚖️ Contrast triad — STABILISE vs SHIFT vs REMOVE (three different jobs)

🛡️ STABILISE (calcium)

  • IV calcium gluconate/chloride
  • Protects the myocardium in 1–3 min by restoring the threshold gap
  • Does NOT lower serum K⁺ — a bridge, not a cure; repeat if ECG changes persist

🔀 SHIFT (insulin/salbutamol/bicarb)

  • Insulin+dextrose, nebulised salbutamol, bicarbonate if acidotic
  • Drives K⁺ into cells via Na/K-ATPase — lowers the measured level temporarily
  • Hides potassium; it will rebound out of cells within hours
🧹 …and REMOVE (the job the first two can't do)Only dialysis (definitive, essential in anuria/ESKD) and, more slowly, potassium binders (patiromer, sodium zirconium cyclosilicate) actually take potassium out of the body. Stop K-raising drugs and treat the cause. Stabilise buys minutes, shift buys hours, remove buys the cure.

💡 The classic error is to do the shift, watch the number fall, and relax — then the patient rebounds and re-arrests because nobody REMOVED anything.

📋 Orders

🖊️ The order set (14:44)

  • 📈 12-lead ECG now + continuous cardiac monitoring with defib pads on; repeat ECG after each intervention.
  • 🛡️ STABILISE: IV calcium gluconate 10% 10–30 mL over 5–10 min (or calcium chloride 10% 10 mL via central/large-bore line). Repeat in 5 min if ECG changes persist.
  • 💉 SHIFT: 10 units soluble insulin in 25 g dextrose (e.g. 50 mL of 50% or 250 mL of 10%) IV; check capillary glucose now, at 30 min, then hourly ×6.
  • 💨 SHIFT: salbutamol 10–20 mg nebulised.
  • 🧪 SHIFT (only if acidotic): sodium bicarbonate — adjunct, not a substitute; avoid mixing in the same line as calcium (precipitates).
  • ☎️ REMOVE: urgent renal/ICU referral for haemodialysis; enteral potassium binder (patiromer / sodium zirconium cyclosilicate) for ongoing control.
  • 🚫 STOP ramipril, spironolactone, any potassium supplement/salt-substitute; hold other K-raising drugs (NSAIDs, trimethoprim).
  • 🩸 Formal U&E, venous gas, CK (rhabdomyolysis?), glucose, calcium/magnesium; treat the cause (missed dialysis + drugs here).
⚔️ ONE-LINER RULE"Write the orders in the order the heart needs them: calcium, then insulin-dextrose + salbutamol, then the dialysis call — and never mix calcium and bicarbonate in the same line."
S8

👩‍⚕️ Nursing Care

🛏️ Resus-bay priorities — the nurse runs the safety net

  • 📟 Continuous telemetry, defib pads attached, arrest trolley to hand — a sine wave can become VF in seconds.
  • 🛡️ Draw up calcium first and know your two vials apart: calcium gluconate for peripheral use; calcium chloride (3× more elemental calcium) needs a good/central line as it is a vesicant.
  • 🍬 Glucose watch after insulin — check capillary glucose at 30 min then hourly for at least 6 h; hypoglycaemia can arrive late, especially in ESKD. Keep dextrose ready.
  • 💉 Guard the fistula arm — no BP cuffs, cannulae or venepuncture in the AV fistula limb; it is the patient's dialysis lifeline.
  • 🔁 Repeat the ECG after each drug and re-send potassium — the nurse's serial strips show whether calcium is holding and whether the shift is working.
  • 🧾 Time-stamp every drug and every strip precisely; escalate immediately if the QRS re-widens (calcium wearing off → needs repeat).
⚔️ ONE-LINER RULE"The nurse who repeats the ECG after every drug is the person who catches the calcium wearing off before the sine wave comes back."

📟 Monitoring

📡 What to watch and why

  • 📈 Continuous telemetry with defib to hand and a repeat 12-lead after each intervention — the QRS width is your real-time read on whether calcium is holding.
  • 🔁 Serial potassium (venous gas) at ~30–60 min intervals — confirm the shift is working and detect rebound as insulin/salbutamol wear off.
  • 🍬 Capillary glucose at 30 min then hourly for 6 h — insulin-induced hypoglycaemia is common and can be delayed, especially in ESKD.
  • 🩸 BP and conscious level — improving perfusion and rate as potassium falls is a good sign; a re-widening QRS or falling GCS means escalate.
  • 🧮 Track the plan for REMOVAL — has renal accepted for dialysis, is access ready? Shifting without a removal plan is a countdown to rebound.
⚔️ ONE-LINER RULE"After the first calcium and insulin, the two questions are: 'is the QRS narrowing?' and 'when is dialysis?' — the ECG and the removal plan, not a single reassuring number."
S13

⚠️ Complications

💀 What can go wrong in the ED phase

  • ⚡ VF / asystole / cardiac arrest — the endpoint of the untreated march; why pads stay on and calcium goes early.
  • 🔁 Rebound hyperkalaemia — the shift wears off in 2–6 h and potassium climbs again if it was never REMOVED (the anuric patient's trap).
  • 🍬 Hypoglycaemia from insulin — common, sometimes delayed; a treatment complication you must actively prevent and monitor.
  • 💉 Calcium extravasation / tissue necrosis — especially calcium chloride peripherally; and the digoxin-toxicity calcium controversy (S20).
  • 🧊 Over-correction / hypokalaemia after aggressive shift + dialysis — swing the other way and you provoke different arrhythmias.
  • 🩺 The system complication: "treated" and moved to a ward without dialysis arranged — the patient re-arrests where nobody is watching.
⚔️ ONE-LINER RULE"The deadliest complication of 'treated' hyperkalaemia is a falsely reassuring number that let everyone stop watching before the potassium was actually removed."

  • ACEi/spironolactone reviewed before restart; potassium binder if needed
  • Dietary K⁺ counselling; no salt-substitutes (they are KCl)
  • Red flags: weakness, palpitations, missed dialysis → return immediately

🟢 The mild ambulatory hyperkalaemia

  • K⁺ 5.5–6.0, no ECG change, reversible cause
  • Stop/adjust the offending drug, dietary advice, arrange a recheck
  • Safety-net: what to look for, when to come back
  • GP/renal follow-up and a plan for the underlying CKD/drugs
⚔️ ONE-LINER RULE"A safe hyperkalaemia discharge means the cause is fixed, the K-raising drug is addressed, the potassium is stable OFF shifting therapy, and the patient knows the salt-substitute in the cupboard is really potassium."

💎 Clinical Pearls

🛡️ Calcium FIRSTIt doesn't lower the potassium — it saves the heart while you do. Give it before you finish reading the gas.
🔀 Three jobs, in orderSTABILISE (calcium) → SHIFT (insulin/salbutamol) → REMOVE (dialysis/binders). Different jobs, all needed.
🌊 The sine waveQRS merged with T = minutes from asystole. Treat instantly, don't wait for confirmation.
🔁 Shift ≠ cureInsulin HIDES potassium; dialysis REMOVES it. Shifting alone in anuria guarantees rebound.
🍬 Watch the glucoseInsulin causes hypoglycaemia, sometimes late, especially in ESKD — monitor 6 h.
🐢 Not a primary bradyA wide-complex bradycardia in a dialysis patient is potassium — reach for calcium, not atropine.
🧾 Treat the ECG, not the numberAny hyperkalaemic ECG change = severe, whatever the value.
🧂 Hidden potassiumStop ACEi/ARB/spironolactone/NSAIDs and the "low-sodium" salt-substitute (it's KCl).

🔬 Advanced Topics — Fellowship Level

🧠 Beyond the pathway

  • 🔋 The electrophysiology in detail: the resting membrane potential is approximated by the Nernst equation for K⁺, EK = −61·log([K]in/[K]out); raising extracellular K⁺ makes EK (and thus the resting potential) less negative. A less-negative rest voltage leaves voltage-gated fast Na⁺ channels in a partially inactivated state, reducing available current for phase 0 → slower upstroke, slower conduction, wider QRS. Calcium raises the threshold potential, restoring the voltage gap the Na channels need — it treats the electrical effect without touching [K⁺].
  • 🧪 Pseudohyperkalaemia: a spuriously high potassium from sample handling — haemolysis, prolonged tourniquet/fist-clenching, delayed processing, or extreme thrombocytosis/leucocytosis (potassium released after the sample is drawn). Clue: high K⁺ with a completely normal ECG in a well patient — repeat carefully (ideally a heparinised gas, minimal stasis) before over-treating.
  • 💊 Hyperkalaemia in digoxin toxicity — the calcium controversy: hyperkalaemia is a marker of severe digoxin poisoning, and the definitive treatment is digoxin-specific antibody fragments (DigiFab). Classic teaching warned that IV calcium in digoxin toxicity could cause "stone heart" (calcium-mediated tetanic contraction); modern evidence questions how real this is, but the pragmatic approach is to prioritise Fab and use calcium cautiously if at all.
  • 🧹 Newer potassium binders: patiromer and sodium zirconium cyclosilicate (SZC) are gut binders that outperform old sodium/calcium resonium for chronic control and enable continuation of prognostically important RAAS inhibitors in heart failure/CKD. SZC has a faster onset (hours) but neither replaces dialysis in the acute anuric emergency.
  • 🫀 Management in the arrested patient: hyperkalaemia is a reversible cause of cardiac arrest (a "K" among the 4Hs/4Ts). Give empirical IV calcium chloride during CPR when hyperK is plausible, plus insulin-dextrose (± bicarbonate), and activate emergency dialysis. Consider prolonged resuscitation and, in refractory cases, extracorporeal support — these arrests can recover fully once potassium is corrected.
  • 🧊 Why calcium wears off & the low-Ca⁺⁺ amplifier: the membrane-stabilising effect lasts ~30–60 min, so it must be repeated while the shift/removal catch up; concurrent hypocalcaemia (common in CKD) worsens hyperkalaemic cardiotoxicity, so correcting calcium is doubly useful.

🎭 Morbidity & Mortality Conference

🎭 M&M — "The Sine Wave in the Corridor"

The case: A 62-year-old anuric dialysis patient who had missed a session arrived weak and nauseated at a busy ED. The monitor showed a wide-complex bradycardia, read by the team as "a slow rhythm — probably his beta-blocker." A gas showed K⁺ 8.1. He was given insulin-dextrose and salbutamol — but NO calcium, and the referral for dialysis was "for later." The number on the next gas ticked down to 7.0, so he was moved to a monitored ward bed to "await renal." Within the hour the QRS widened into a sine wave and he arrested in VF. Despite prolonged resuscitation and belated calcium, he could not be brought back — the potassium had never been removed and the heart was never protected.

🧀 The Swiss-cheese holes that lined up:

🛡️ NO CALCIUMThe one drug that protects the myocardium was omitted; the heart was unshielded during the whole event.
🔁 SHIFT ≠ REMOVEInsulin/salbutamol "hid" the potassium; nobody REMOVED it, so it rebounded.
📉 FALSE REASSURANCEA falling number let everyone relax before dialysis was even arranged.
🐢 WRONG LABEL"A slow rhythm" instead of "a potassium rhythm" delayed the right treatment.
🏥 SYSTEMMoved to a less-watched bed with definitive treatment still pending.

🛡️ What would have saved him: give IV calcium the moment the wide QRS/high K⁺ was seen — before the gas even finished; recognise that shifting is temporary and book urgent dialysis to REMOVE the potassium; keep him in resus with defib to hand until the potassium was actually falling and being removed; and never let a temporarily improving number substitute for definitive treatment. This is exactly the discipline that saved the patient in Bed 5 — 10 mL of calcium at the door bought the time dialysis needed.

⚔️ ONE-LINER RULE"In a peri-arrest missed-dialysis patient, calcium is a diagnostic and a therapeutic in one — and a falling potassium is not a treated potassium until dialysis has removed it."
