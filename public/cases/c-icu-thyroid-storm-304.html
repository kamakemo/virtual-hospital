Multi-organ failure (hyperpyrexia, arrhythmia, high-output cardiac failure, delirium, hepatic dysfunction) is the diagnosis.

🧭 The reflex differential you address at the bedside

"Hot, fast, confused" also fits sepsis, serotonin syndrome / NMS, anticholinergic or sympathomimetic toxicity, heat stroke, and alcohol withdrawal (see S14). The goitre, ophthalmopathy, thyroid bruit and the story of stopped antithyroid drugs point straight at the thyroid — but you still send cultures and screen for co-existing infection, because infection is the commonest trigger.

⚠️ The trap of "the levels are only mildly high"There is no free-hormone threshold that defines storm. Patients storm at hormone levels that look identical to stable outpatients — because storm is about a surge in / heightened tissue sensitivity to thyroid hormone plus loss of protein binding, not the absolute number. Treat the patient in front of you, not the lab.

👨‍⚕️ Resident Review

The ICU resident thinks out loud at 23:46:

🧠 Frame it as a "block the hormone at every level" problem

"This is thyroid storm — Graves', precipitated by stopping carbimazole plus a chest infection. My Burch-Wartofsky is well over 45. I am not waiting for TFTs. I attack the hormone at four levels, in order: block the adrenergic effect first with propranolol, then stop new synthesis with propylthiouracil, then — at least an hour later — give iodine to block release, and give hydrocortisone to cut peripheral T4→T3 conversion and cover adrenal reserve. Then I treat the chest infection and cool her with paracetamol, never aspirin."

🎯 Her order of operations

  1. 💓 Beta-blocker FIRST — propranolol (controls the adrenergic storm AND blocks peripheral T4→T3 conversion); esmolol infusion if titratable ICU control is needed.
  2. 🛑 Antithyroid drug SECOND — propylthiouracil (PTU) preferred in storm (also blocks peripheral conversion); carbimazole/methimazole otherwise.
  3. 🧂 Iodine THIRD — at least 1 hour AFTER the antithyroid drug (Lugol's / potassium iodide) to block hormone release; never before, or you fuel synthesis.
  4. 💉 Hydrocortisone — reduces T4→T3 conversion and covers relative adrenal insufficiency.
  5. 🦠 Treat the precipitant (antibiotics for the chest infection), active cooling with paracetamol, fluids/electrolytes/glucose, ICU supportive care.
⚔️ ONE-LINER RULE"Block, block, block, then treat the trigger: β-blocker → antithyroid drug → (wait ≥1 h) → iodine → steroid. Get the order wrong and you can make the storm worse."
🧪 Resident's self-testShe reaches for Lugol's iodine to "shut the thyroid down fast" before the PTU is drawn up. Give it?

No. Iodine given before an antithyroid drug provides substrate for brand-new hormone synthesis (the Jod-Basedow phenomenon) and can worsen the storm. Iodine only blocks release once synthesis is already blocked — so give the antithyroid drug first and wait at least 1 hour before the iodine.

🧠 Teaching Points + Interactive Simulation

🔬 The mechanism — WHY thyrotoxicosis becomes a storm (the WHY behind the WHY)

  1. 🦋 Baseline: Graves' TSH-receptor antibodies drive unregulated synthesis and release of T4/T3 — chronic thyrotoxicosis.
  2. 💥 The trigger: a stressor (infection, surgery, trauma, iodine load, DKA, childbirth, or withdrawal of the antithyroid drug) causes a sudden surge in free hormone and/or a heightened tissue sensitivity to it — partly via a fall in binding proteins and a rise in β-adrenergic receptor density.
  3. ⚡ Adrenergic amplification: thyroid hormone up-regulates catecholamine receptors, so a normal catecholamine level produces an exaggerated sympathetic response → tachyarrhythmia, fever, agitation. This is WHY β-blockade is so effective.
  4. 🔥 Hypermetabolic multi-organ crisis: oxygen consumption and heat production soar → hyperpyrexia; the heart runs high-output until it fails; the brain becomes encephalopathic; the gut and liver decompensate. A vicious cycle: fever and tachycardia raise metabolic demand, which raises hormone effect, which raises fever.
⚔️ ONE-LINER RULE"Storm is thyrotoxicosis with the brakes off — a hormone surge amplified by catecholamine hypersensitivity, tipping every organ into a self-feeding hypermetabolic crisis. Break the cycle at every level."

⚖️ Contrast pair — Thyroid Storm vs Myxoedema Coma

🔥 Thyroid Storm

  • Hyper-everything: hyperpyrexia, tachycardia/AF, agitation/delirium, high-output failure, vomiting/diarrhoea
  • Too much hormone effect
  • Treat: β-blocker → antithyroid drug → iodine → steroid + cooling + treat trigger

🧊 Myxoedema Coma

  • Hypo-everything: hypothermia, bradycardia, hypoventilation/CO₂ retention, hyponatraemia, obtunded consciousness
  • Too little hormone
  • Treat: IV levothyroxine (± liothyronine) + IV hydrocortisone + gentle rewarming + supportive care

💡 Two ends of one axis. Both are endocrine emergencies, both need IV hydrocortisone, and both are clinical diagnoses — but everything else is a mirror image.

📋 Orders

🖊️ The order set (23:50) — the bundle in order

  • 💓 Propranolol 60–80 mg PO/NG q4–6h (or IV 0.5–1 mg slowly, titrated) — first; use esmolol infusion for titratable control in the ICU. Caution in overt high-output failure — titrate.
  • 🛑 Propylthiouracil (PTU) 500–1000 mg loading then 250 mg q4h PO/NG — second (blocks synthesis + peripheral conversion). Alternative: carbimazole/methimazole 20 mg q4–6h.
  • 🧂 Iodine — at least 1 hour AFTER PTU: Lugol's iodine 8–10 drops q6h, or potassium iodide (SSKI) 5 drops q6h. Never before the antithyroid drug.
  • 💉 Hydrocortisone 100 mg IV q8h (or dexamethasone 2 mg q6h) — cuts T4→T3 conversion + covers adrenal reserve.
  • 🦠 Treat the precipitant: cultures + empirical antibiotics for the chest infection; hunt for other triggers.
  • 🧊 Active cooling with paracetamol (± cooling blankets/tepid sponging). NO aspirin — it displaces T4 from binding proteins and worsens the storm.
  • 💧 IV fluids (high insensible losses), correct electrolytes/glucose, thiamine; ×2 IV access; consider NG for enteral drugs.
  • 📟 ICU admission, continuous cardiac monitoring, urinary catheter, VTE prophylaxis; rate/rhythm plan for AF; consider cholestyramine as an adjunct (interrupts enterohepatic hormone recycling).
⚔️ ONE-LINER RULE"β-blocker, then antithyroid drug, then (an hour later) iodine, then steroid — plus paracetamol not aspirin, and antibiotics for the trigger. Same drugs, wrong order = a worse storm."
S8

👩‍⚕️ Nursing Care

🛏️ ICU priorities — the nurse runs the safety net

  • ⏱️ Guard the drug ORDER and TIMING — antithyroid drug in first, then time-stamp it and give iodine only ≥1 hour later. Challenge any iodine drawn up early.
  • 🌡️ Active cooling — cooling blankets, tepid sponging, cool IV fluids; give paracetamol, never aspirin; continuous core-temperature monitoring.
  • 📟 Continuous ECG for the AF/tachyarrhythmia; report rate, rhythm and any hypotension after propranolol.
  • 💧 Strict fluid balance — insensible losses through fever/sweat/diarrhoea are huge; watch for both dehydration and pulmonary congestion in the failing heart.
  • 🧠 Delirium and safety — calm, cool, low-stimulus environment; fall/line-pull precautions; frequent GCS.
  • 🩸 Draw the pre-treatment TFTs, cultures, glucose and electrolytes; label times; do NOT delay the first drugs waiting for results.
⚔️ ONE-LINER RULE"In a storm the nurse who protects the drug order and reaches for paracetamol instead of aspirin is doing life-saving pharmacology at the bedside."

📈 ECG / Imaging + Visual Enhancements

🫀 The ECG — thyrotoxic fast atrial fibrillation

Fast, irregularly irregular narrow-complex tachycardia with no discernible P waves — atrial fibrillation with rapid ventricular response, the classic thyrotoxic arrhythmia. Rate control comes largely from the β-blocker, not just from AV-nodal agents.

🔎 The cardiac and imaging picture you must know

FindingLook forMeaning
💓 Fast AFIrregular, no P waves, rate often > 140Up to ~40% of storms; thyrotoxicosis is a reversible cause — restoring euthyroidism often restores sinus rhythm.
💔 High-output failureRaised JVP, crackles, gallop, wide pulse pressure; echo: hyperdynamic then dilated LVDemand outstrips supply; β-blockade helps but titrate carefully if overtly failing.
🩻 CXRPulmonary congestion; consolidation of the precipitating infectionFinds the trigger AND the complication.
🦋 Thyroid ultrasound / uptakeDiffuse goitre, increased vascularity ("thyroid inferno"); high, diffuse uptake in Graves'Confirms Graves' aetiology — but do NOT delay treatment for imaging.
🧠 Consider CT head / LPIf focal signs or diagnosis unclearExcludes CNS mimics of the encephalopathy.
⚔️ ONE-LINER RULE"Thyrotoxic AF is a treat-the-cause arrhythmia — rate-control with a β-blocker and make the patient euthyroid; do not just chase the ventricular rate and miss the storm driving it."

🩻 Adjuncts

  • Echo (POCUS): hyperdynamic LV, high output, later reduced function; screens for effusion and valve disease.
  • Septic screen: cultures, CXR, urine — the infective trigger must be found and treated in parallel with the blockade.

S12

📟 Monitoring

📡 What to watch and why

  • 🌡️ Core temperature continuously — hyperpyrexia is a leading cause of death; the goal is a falling temperature.
  • 💓 Continuous ECG — rate and rhythm of the AF, and the response to β-blockade; watch for hypotension after propranolol.
  • 💔 Signs of heart failure — JVP, crackles, urine output; the high-output heart can tip into congestion.
  • 🧠 GCS / delirium — improving mentation is a marker of response; worsening (seizure/coma) is ominous.
  • 🧪 Electrolytes, glucose, LFTs, renal function — repeated; K⁺ 4.0–5.0 to reduce arrhythmia; watch PTU hepatotoxicity.
  • 📉 Re-score (BWPS) and reassess the trigger — a rising score or persistent fever means inadequate blockade or an untreated precipitant.
⚔️ ONE-LINER RULE"The three numbers that tell you the storm is breaking are the temperature, the heart rate and the GCS — if they are not improving in hours, re-check your blockade and hunt the precipitant."
S13

⚠️ Complications

💀 What can go wrong

  • 💔 High-output cardiac failure → cardiogenic collapse — the commonest cardiac killer of storm.
  • ⚡ Malignant tachyarrhythmia — fast AF with haemodynamic instability; thromboembolic stroke from AF.
  • 🔥 Hyperpyrexia → multi-organ failure, rhabdomyolysis, DIC — heat is directly cytotoxic.
  • 🧠 Encephalopathy → seizures → coma — a poor-prognosis trajectory.
  • 🟡 Hepatic dysfunction / jaundice — from the storm itself and, separately, PTU hepatotoxicity.
  • 💊 Iatrogenic worsening — iodine before the antithyroid drug, or aspirin for fever (the M&M in S21); agranulocytosis from thionamides.
⚔️ ONE-LINER RULE"In thyroid storm the deadliest complication is often the one you cause — the wrong drug order or the wrong antipyretic. Treat by the sequence, and the storm usually breaks."

🗺️ Plan + Procedure Guides with Checklists

🧭 The management plan

  1. 🔥 Diagnosis: thyroid storm (Graves', BWPS ≈ 115) precipitated by antithyroid-drug withdrawal + chest infection. Treat immediately, TFTs sent but not awaited.
  2. 💊 Blockade in order: propranolol → PTU → (≥1 h) iodine → hydrocortisone; paracetamol for fever, antibiotics for the chest.
  3. 🏥 ICU supportive care: cooling, fluids/electrolytes/glucose, rate control of AF, monitor for heart failure and hepatotoxicity; re-score BWPS.
  4. 🦋 Definitive therapy LATER, once euthyroid and stable: radioactive iodine or total thyroidectomy (with pre-operative optimisation) to prevent recurrence.
⚔️ ONE-LINER RULE"Stabilise the storm first with the medical blockade; definitive cure — radioiodine or thyroidectomy — is a planned, elective decision for a euthyroid patient, never the emergency move."

🏠 Discharge

📋 Recovery & preventing the next storm — two different worlds

🔥 This patient (post-storm)

  • Carbimazole maintenance with a firm supply/adherence plan; explicit "never stop abruptly" counselling
  • Definitive therapy scheduled (radioiodine or thyroidectomy)
  • Red flags: fever, palpitations, agitation, vomiting → return immediately
  • Endocrine follow-up; AF anticoagulation reviewed; treat any residual infection

🧊 The myxoedema-coma survivor (the contrast)

  • Lifelong levothyroxine with clear adherence education
  • Understand the OPPOSITE crisis — cold, slow, drowsy — as their emergency
  • Avoid sedatives/opioids that can precipitate hypoventilation
  • Steroid cover during illness if concurrent adrenal insufficiency
⚔️ ONE-LINER RULE"The storm is not over at discharge — it is over when the patient is euthyroid, adherent, has a guaranteed drug supply, and has a definitive plan. Abrupt antithyroid-drug withdrawal is how the next storm starts."

🔬 Advanced Topics — Fellowship Level

🧠 Beyond the bundle

  • 🧮 Diagnostic frameworks compared: the Burch-Wartofsky scale is sensitive (a prompt to treat) while the Japan Thyroid Association / Akamizu criteria are more specific (requiring thyrotoxicosis plus defined organ dysfunction). Use BWPS to trigger action; recognise its lower specificity.
  • 🩸 Refractory storm — hormone removal: when the blockade is failing, add cholestyramine (interrupts enterohepatic recirculation) and consider therapeutic plasma exchange (TPE / plasmapheresis) to physically remove circulating hormone and antibody, and to bridge to emergency thyroidectomy.
  • 🦋 Emergency thyroidectomy: reserved for storm not controlled medically, thionamide intolerance (agranulocytosis, severe hepatotoxicity) or amiodarone-induced thyrotoxicosis type 2 refractory to steroids — done in an optimised, β-blocked patient by an experienced surgeon.
  • 💠 Amiodarone-induced thyrotoxicosis (AIT): Type 1 (iodine-induced excess synthesis — thionamides ± perchlorate) vs Type 2 (destructive thyroiditis — glucocorticoids). Distinguishing them (colour-Doppler flow, IL-6) changes therapy; mixed forms need both.
  • 💓 Cardiac nuances: in overt high-output failure, ultra-short-acting esmolol allows titratable β-blockade; digoxin often needs higher doses (increased clearance + reduced sensitivity); anticoagulation for thyrotoxic AF is individualised.
  • 🧪 The mechanism frontier: storm reflects an acute rise in the free hormone fraction (fall in binding proteins), up-regulated β-adrenergic signalling, and possibly cytokine surge — explaining why β-blockade, glucocorticoids and reducing peripheral conversion all help even before hormone synthesis is fully controlled.

🎭 Morbidity & Mortality Conference

🎭 M&M — "The Iodine That Fed the Fire"

The case: A 40-year-old woman with Graves' presented in florid thyroid storm — 40.5°C, fast AF, delirious. The team correctly recognised it. But in the rush, the first drug reached for was Lugol's iodine, given before any antithyroid drug, "to shut the thyroid down." Minutes later, to bring the fever down, a nurse was asked for "something for temperature" and handed aspirin. Over the next hour her free T4 climbed further, her temperature rose, the AF degenerated, and she suffered a cardiac arrest. The PTU had still not been given when she was found pulseless. Resuscitation failed.

🧀 The Swiss-cheese holes that lined up:

🧂 IODINE FIRSTIodine given before the antithyroid drug fed new hormone synthesis (Jod–Basedow) instead of blocking release.
💊 ASPIRIN TO COOLAspirin displaced T4 from binding proteins, raising the free (active) fraction and worsening the storm.
🛑 NO ANTITHYROID DRUGPTU — the drug that stops synthesis — was never given in time; the sequence was inverted.
⏱️ NO TIMING DISCIPLINENo one owned the order/interval of the bundle in the chaos.
🌡️ HYPERPYREXIA UNBROKENThe fever, the leading killer, was tackled with the wrong antipyretic.

🛡️ What would have saved her: give the blockade in order — propranolol first, then PTU to block synthesis, and iodine only ≥1 hour after the PTU; cool with paracetamol, never aspirin; and make one person own the sequence and timing. This is exactly the discipline that saved the patient in Bed 4.

⚔️ ONE-LINER RULE"In thyroid storm the drugs are right but the ORDER is the medicine: iodine before the antithyroid drug, or aspirin to cool, turns a survivable crisis into a death."
