The whole management is a structured antidote escalation, started early, not a "resus and hope."

🧭 The three cardiotoxic threads you hold from the first minute

Frame the poisoned heart as three overlapping stories: beta-blocker (brady, hypotension, hypoglycaemia, bronchospasm; propranolol adds sodium-channel blockade + seizures), calcium-channel blocker (brady, vasodilatory + cardiogenic shock, hyperglycaemia), and digoxin (nausea, visual halos, bradyarrhythmias/AV block or bidirectional VT, hyperkalaemia in acute toxicity). One glucose, one potassium, one ECG and the packet triage all three within minutes (see S14).

⚠️ The trap of "she looks stable-ish"Modified-release verapamil and diltiazem can lull you: a patient who is "only mildly slow" at hour 1 can be periarrest at hour 4 as the MR coat keeps dissolving. Anticipate deterioration, secure two large IVs, and get the antidotes to the bedside BEFORE she crashes — the reactive team always arrives late.

👨‍⚕️ Resident Review

The EM resident thinks out loud at 22:46:

🧠 Frame it as "which poison, how much, how fast, which antidote"

"Empty verapamil and metoprolol — so I'm treating a combined CCB/BB overdose, and the high sugar tells me the calcium-channel blocker is driving the shock. She's already refractory to atropine and fluid, so I'm not going to chase them. I want IV calcium and glucagon at the bedside now, I'm preparing high-dose insulin (HIET) because that's the therapy that actually rescues these hearts, and I'm calling toxicology and ICU early and warning the cath lab/ECMO team that this could go to mechanical support."

🎯 Her order of operations

  1. 💉 Two large-bore IVs + central access; ECG, continuous monitoring, defib pads on, arterial line if time allows.
  2. 🦴 IV calcium (calcium gluconate 10% or calcium chloride via central line), repeated/infused — first-line for CCB shock.
  3. 🧫 Glucagon bolus then infusion — especially for the beta-blocker component (bypasses the β-receptor to raise cAMP); have anti-emetics ready.
  4. 💧 Start HIET early: 1 unit/kg insulin bolus then 1 unit/kg/hr, titrated up, with dextrose and potassium monitoring — do not wait for atropine to fail three times.
  5. 📉 Vasopressors (noradrenaline ± adrenaline) for refractory hypotension; consider lipid emulsion and escalate to pacing / VA-ECMO if refractory.
  6. 🧪 Bloods: glucose, potassium, VBG/lactate, digoxin level (if any suspicion), paracetamol/salicylate co-ingestion screen, renal function.
⚔️ ONE-LINER RULE"Refractory CCB/BB shock lives or dies on HIGH-DOSE INSULIN — start it early, don't wait for atropine to fail three times."
🧪 Resident's self-testThe nurse asks: "Her sugar is high anyway — do we still give dextrose with the insulin?"

Yes — HIET means high-dose insulin WITH euglycaemia. You run insulin at doses far above diabetic ranges (1 u/kg/hr and climbing) and give dextrose to keep glucose ~5–8 mmol/L, checking sugars every 15–30 min initially. Even a "high" starting glucose will fall once you unload insulin at these doses. You must also watch potassium — insulin drives K into cells; replace to keep it in the low-normal range, but don't over-correct. The insulin is the treatment; the dextrose and potassium are the safety rails.

S6

🧠 Teaching Points + Interactive Simulation

🔬 The mechanism — WHY the heart stops, WHY the sugar moves (the WHY behind the WHY)

  1. 🚪 Calcium-channel blockers block myocardial and vascular L-type calcium channels → less calcium entry → negative inotropy, chronotropy, dromotropy + vasodilatation. Crucially, the same L-type channels trigger insulin release from pancreatic β-cells — block them and you cause insulin deficiency. The stressed myocardium, which in shock prefers to burn carbohydrate, is now both energy-starved and can't use glucose → hyperglycaemia is the fingerprint.
  2. 📵 Beta-blockers block β-receptors → ↓cAMP → ↓heart rate and contractility, plus hypoglycaemia (blunted glycogenolysis/gluconeogenesis) and bronchospasm. Lipophilic/membrane-active agents (propranolol) add sodium-channel blockade → wide QRS + seizures, behaving like a TCA.
  3. 💊 The rescue logic: calcium forces more charge through the blocked channels; glucagon raises cAMP around the blocked β-receptor; HIET gives the poisoned heart the insulin it lacks, switching the myocardium to carbohydrate fuel and acting as a direct positive inotrope — while dextrose and potassium keep the patient safe.
  4. 🧬 Digoxin poisons the Na⁺/K⁺-ATPase → intracellular Na↑ → via Na/Ca exchange intracellular Ca↑ (some inotropy) but in overdose → bradyarrhythmias, AV block, bidirectional VT, and — because the pump that keeps K inside cells is jammed — acute toxicity causes hyperkalaemia, a marker of severity. The antidote is digoxin-specific antibody.
⚔️ ONE-LINER RULE"CCB overdose runs HIGH sugar; beta-blocker overdose runs LOW — the glucose tells you which poison, and both are rescued by high-dose insulin."

⚖️ Contrast pair — Beta-blocker vs Calcium-channel-blocker overdose

📵 Beta-blocker overdose

  • Low glucose (hypoglycaemia)
  • Bronchospasm; propranolol → wide QRS + seizures (Na-channel) → bicarbonate
  • Signature antidote: GLUCAGON (raises cAMP past the β-receptor)
  • Still give HIET if refractory

🚪 Calcium-channel-blocker overdose

  • High glucose (hyperglycaemia — the fingerprint)
  • Verapamil/diltiazem worst; vasodilatory + cardiogenic shock
  • Signature moves: IV CALCIUM + HIET
  • MR formulations → delayed, prolonged, biphasic crash

💡 A number is a clue, not a diagnosis: the glucose points to the poison, but in a mixed BB/CCB ingestion you treat both threads — calcium, glucagon and insulin are not mutually exclusive.

📋 Orders

🖊️ The order set (22:44)

  • 📈 12-lead ECG now + continuous monitoring, defib pads on; repeat with any change (watch PR/QRS, AV block, junctional rhythm).
  • 💉 Two large-bore IVs + central line; arterial line for beat-to-beat pressure.

S8

👩‍⚕️ Nursing Care

🛏️ Resus-bay priorities — the nurse runs the metabolic safety net

  • 📟 Continuous telemetry, defib pads attached; watch for progressive AV block, junctional escape and asystole.
  • 🍬 Point-of-care glucose every 15–30 min once HIET starts — high-dose insulin can drop the sugar fast; titrate the dextrose to keep euglycaemia.
  • 🧂 Serial potassium — insulin shifts K into cells; replace to keep low-normal, but flag any digoxin picture where the K is high (that changes the whole plan — see S11/S14).
  • 💉 Manage multiple infusions safely: calcium, glucagon, insulin, dextrose, noradrenaline — label lines, avoid calcium/bicarbonate incompatibility, use dedicated lumens.
  • 🤮 Anticipate glucagon-induced vomiting; protect the airway in a drowsy patient; have suction ready.
  • 🧠 Mental-health/safety context: deliberate self-poisoning — ensure a safe environment, remove ligature/medication risks, and arrange psychiatric review once stabilised.
⚔️ ONE-LINER RULE"During HIET the nurse's glucometer and potassium are the resuscitation — a missed sugar or a missed K is how the antidote turns into a new emergency."

S10

📈 ECG / Imaging + Visual Enhancements

🫀 The poisoned-heart ECG — bradycardia, block, and widening

Profound bradycardia with a prolonged PR and, as toxicity deepens, widening QRS (marked with propranolol / sodium-channel blockade). Junctional escape, high-grade AV block and asystole are the feared progression.

🔎 ECG signatures worth knowing cold across the three poisons

PoisonECG cluesMeaning / action
🚪 Calcium-channel blockerSinus brady, PR prolongation, AV block, junctional rhythm; often narrow QRSNegative dromotropy; escalate ladder — calcium, HIET, pressors.
📵 Beta-blockerBrady, AV block; propranolol → wide QRS + terminal R in aVR, seizuresSodium-channel blockade → sodium bicarbonate for the wide QRS.
🧬 DigoxinBrady/AV block, bidirectional VT (pathognomonic), atrial tachycardia with block, "reverse-tick" STLife-threatening dysrhythmia or K⁺>5–6 → digoxin-specific antibody.
💊 TCA (cardiotoxic cousin)Wide QRS, terminal R wave in aVR, tachycardia, right-axisSodium-channel blockade → sodium bicarbonate.
⚔️ ONE-LINER RULE"A widening QRS in an overdose is a sodium-channel scream — reach for bicarbonate; bidirectional VT is digoxin until proven otherwise — reach for the antibody."

🩻 Adjunct imaging

  • Bedside echo (POCUS): assess contractility and filling — distinguishes the pump failure of poisoning, guides fluids vs pressors vs inotropy, and screens for a mechanical mimic.
  • Abdominal X-ray: may show a pharmacobezoar / radiopaque tablet mass in large ingestions, supporting whole-bowel irrigation for MR preparations.

S12

📟 Monitoring

📡 What to watch and why

  • 📈 Continuous telemetry + arterial line — track rate, rhythm, AV conduction and beat-to-beat pressure; defib/pacing to hand.
  • 🍬 Glucose q15–30 min on HIET, titrating dextrose to euglycaemia; a rising insulin requirement can mean rising toxicity.
  • 🧂 Serial potassium — replace as insulin drives it down; a rising K in a digoxin picture is an emergency, not a lab error.
  • 🩸 Lactate and urine output as perfusion endpoints — the goal is tissue perfusion, not a single blood-pressure number.
  • ⏳ Extended observation for MR ingestions — verapamil/diltiazem MR can crash hours later; do not stand down early.
⚔️ ONE-LINER RULE"With modified-release cardiotoxins the monitor's question is never 'is she better now?' but 'will she still be perfused in three hours?' — keep her monitored until the MR curve is truly over."
S13

⚠️ Complications

💀 What can go wrong

  • ⚡ Progressive AV block → asystole / cardiac arrest — the endpoint of untreated brady-toxicity.
  • 💔 Refractory vasodilatory-cardiogenic shock — the crash that HIET and, if needed, ECMO exist to prevent.
  • 🍬 HIET complications: hypoglycaemia and hypokalaemia — iatrogenic and preventable with vigilant monitoring.
  • 🫁 Aspiration / pulmonary oedema — from glucagon vomiting, CNS depression, and large fluid + dextrose volumes.
  • 🧠 Seizures (propranolol, membrane-active agents) and bronchospasm (beta-blockade in asthmatics).
  • 🧬 Digoxin thread: life-threatening hyperkalaemia and dysrhythmia — and the iatrogenic "stone heart" if calcium is given carelessly.
  • 🩹 The system complication: delayed escalation — treating with atropine and fluids and "observing" while the therapeutic window closes.
⚔️ ONE-LINER RULE"The deadliest complication of cardiotoxic overdose is the hour you spent 'observing' — refractory shock is a diagnosis you make early and escalate through, not one you wait to confirm."

🏠 Discharge

📋 Two very different endings — the toxic crash vs the reassuring ingestion

💊 This patient (severe CCB/BB overdose)

  • ICU-level care, extended monitoring for MR biphasic toxicity, weaning of HIET/pressors
  • Psychiatric assessment + safety planning before discharge (deliberate self-harm)
  • Means restriction; social support; follow-up arranged
  • Medical clearance only once truly off vasoactives and past the MR window

🟢 The low-risk / trivial ingestion

  • Small immediate-release ingestion, asymptomatic, normal ECG, normal glucose & K over an adequate observation period
  • MR/verapamil ingestions need longer observation even if well
  • Psychosocial assessment still mandatory in every deliberate overdose
  • Clear safety-net and mental-health follow-up
⚔️ ONE-LINER RULE"A cardiotoxic overdose is never medically clear until the modified-release window has passed AND the psychiatric risk has been addressed — the heart and the mind both get a safe disposition."

🔬 Advanced Topics — Fellowship Level

🧠 Beyond the ladder

  • 💧 The metabolic mechanism of HIET: the poisoned, stressed myocardium switches its preferred fuel from free fatty acids to carbohydrate, but CCB-induced insulin deficiency and cellular resistance starve it of usable glucose. Supraphysiological insulin restores myocardial glucose uptake and calcium handling, acting as a direct positive inotrope and metabolic rescue — which is why HIET outperforms catecholamines in these hearts and is titrated to inotropy, not just glucose.
  • 🧴 Intravenous lipid emulsion — the "lipid sink": a lipid compartment in the blood sequesters lipophilic drug (verapamil, propranolol, local anaesthetics), and may also provide myocardial fatty-acid substrate and a direct cardiotonic effect. Reserved for periarrest/refractory toxicity; interferes with some lab assays and oxygenators.
  • 🫀 VA-ECMO for refractory toxin-induced shock: because most cardiotoxins are eventually metabolised, extracorporeal support is a bridge to recovery — outcomes from refractory poisoning-induced cardiogenic shock are meaningfully better with early ECMO, so the referral must precede the arrest.
  • 📈 TCA (and membrane-active) sodium-channel blockade: fast-sodium-channel blockade produces a wide QRS, terminal R in aVR, hypotension and seizures. Sodium bicarbonate (sodium load + alkalinisation) is the antidote; hypertonic saline and hyperventilation are adjuncts. The same physiology applies to propranolol overdose.
  • 🔵 Methylene blue for vasoplegia: in refractory vasodilatory shock (including some CCB and metformin-associated cases), methylene blue inhibits nitric-oxide-mediated guanylate cyclase, raising systemic vascular resistance as a rescue when catecholamines and HIET are maxed out.
  • 🧬 Digoxin nuance: chronic vs acute toxicity differ (chronic often normokalaemic/hypokalaemic with lower levels but real toxicity); antibody dosing can be empirical in arrest, and free-digoxin levels rise transiently after Fab — interpret post-treatment levels with care, especially in renal failure.

🎭 Morbidity & Mortality Conference

🎭 M&M — "Atropine, A Litre, And Wait"

The case: A 23-year-old woman took a bottle of verapamil MR after a break-up and arrived HR 44, BP 82/50, mildly drowsy. The team, reassured that she was "talking and only a bit slow," gave atropine and a litre of saline and moved her to a monitored corridor bay to "observe." IV calcium was given late and only once; glucagon was drawn but not started; HIET was never begun. Over the next three hours the modified-release tablets kept dissolving — she became increasingly hypotensive, then bradycardic, then arrested in PEA. By the time HIET and ECMO were discussed, CPR was already in progress. Resuscitation failed.

🧀 The Swiss-cheese holes that lined up:

🧠 FALSE REASSURANCE"Talking and only a bit slow" was read as stable — MR verapamil had not finished absorbing.
💧 HIET DELAYEDThe cornerstone therapy was never started until it was too late; hours were lost to atropine and fluid.
🪜 STUCK ON RUNG 1Escalation stalled at supportive care; calcium under-dosed, glucagon undelivered.
⏳ MR UNDER-RESPECTEDNo whole-bowel irrigation, no extended-crash anticipation for a modified-release load.
📞 LATE ESCALATIONTox and ECMO teams were called during the arrest, not before it.

🛡️ What would have saved her: treat refractory brady-hypotension after a BB/CCB ingestion as a poisoned heart from minute one; start high-dose insulin early rather than waiting for atropine to fail; dose calcium and glucagon properly and move up the ladder; respect the modified-release curve with decontamination and prolonged monitoring; and phone toxicology and the ECMO team before the crash. This is exactly the discipline that saved the patient in Bed 10.

⚔️ ONE-LINER RULE"'Atropine, a litre, and wait' has killed more cardiotoxic overdoses than any drug — insulin started at the door buys these patients their lives."
