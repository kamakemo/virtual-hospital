The whole game is a structured, time-boxed pathway with a hard decision on day 3.

🧭 The three things you decide at the bedside first

Before any drug: (1) is this truly severe (apply Truelove & Witts, S5)? (2) is there surgical trouble now (peritonism, distension, toxic megacolon)? and (3) is this infection mimicking a flare (C. difficile, CMV, dysentery)? One examination, one AXR, and a stool sample answer all three within the hour.

⚠️ The trap of "she looks too young to be that sick"ASUC classically hits people in their twenties and thirties. Youth and a normal-looking face buy false reassurance while the colon is failing. Trust the stool count, the observations and the CRP, not the patient looking young and fit.

🎯 Her order of operations

  1. 💧 Resuscitate: IV crystalloid, correct potassium and magnesium (hypokalaemia predisposes to dilatation), transfuse if Hb low.
  2. 💉 IV corticosteroids now — hydrocortisone 100 mg QDS (or methylprednisolone 60 mg/day). The clock to the day-3 decision starts today.
  3. 🩸 VTE prophylaxis — prophylactic LMWH even though she is passing blood; hospitalised IBD is strongly prothrombotic.
  4. 🦠 Exclude infection: stool culture, C. difficile toxin, and consider CMV if she fails steroids; hold antibiotics unless infection or toxic megacolon.
  5. 🩻 AXR today to look for colonic dilatation, mucosal islands and thumbprinting; flexible sigmoidoscopy (limited, unprepped) to confirm and biopsy — NOT a full colonoscopy.
  6. 🔪 Involve surgery on day 1 and stop all antimotility / opioid / anticholinergic / NSAID agents.
⚔️ ONE-LINER RULE"Give VTE prophylaxis in acute severe colitis even while the patient is bleeding — the biggest killer of the young IBD inpatient after their colon is a pulmonary embolus, not the rectal bleeding."
🧪 Resident self-testShe is bleeding per rectum — is it safe to give prophylactic heparin?

Yes. Guidelines (ECCO, BSG) recommend prophylactic-dose LMWH for hospitalised active UC despite rectal bleeding. Active IBD roughly triples VTE risk, and inpatient VTE is a leading cause of death. Prophylactic dosing does not meaningfully worsen colonic bleeding. Do not withhold it because the stool is bloody.

🧠 Teaching Points + Interactive Simulation

🔬 The mechanism — WHY the bleeding, WHY the danger (the WHY behind the WHY)

  1. 🧬 Barrier failure: a dysregulated mucosal immune response to luminal bacteria in a genetically susceptible gut breaks the epithelial barrier of the colon and rectum.
  2. 🩸 Continuous mucosal inflammation: the inflammation is confined to mucosa and submucosa and spreads continuously and proximally from the rectum — no skip lesions. Friable, ulcerated mucosa bleeds on contact and spontaneously → bloody diarrhoea and mucus.
  3. ⚡ Loss of absorptive surface + urgency: inflamed colon cannot reabsorb water and sodium, and a rigid, irritable rectum drives urgency, tenesmus and frequency, including at night.
  4. 💀 Transmural spread → toxic dilatation: in the most severe cases inflammation and nitric-oxide-driven smooth-muscle paralysis extend deeper, the colon dilates, motility fails, and bacterial translocation drives systemic toxicity. This is toxic megacolon — the vicious cycle that ends in perforation.
⚔️ ONE-LINER RULE"UC bleeds because the inflammation lives in the mucosa; UC kills because, untreated, that inflammation goes transmural, the colon dilates, and it perforates. Stop the cycle before it goes deep."

⚖️ Contrast pair — Ulcerative Colitis vs Crohn Disease

🩸 Ulcerative Colitis

  • Continuous from the rectum, proximal — no skip lesions
  • Colon and rectum ONLY (± backwash ileitis)
  • Mucosa/submucosa only — crypt abscesses, goblet-cell depletion, NO granulomas
  • Bloody diarrhoea + urgency; smoking protective; p-ANCA
  • Colectomy is curative of the colonic disease

🌍 Crohn Disease

  • Skip lesions, mouth to anus (terminal ileum classic)
  • Transmural — cobblestoning, non-caseating granulomas
  • Strictures, fistulae, abscesses, perianal disease
  • Often non-bloody; smoking harmful; ASCA
  • Surgery is NOT curative — disease recurs

💡 The single most useful contrast at the bedside: UC is continuous, mucosal, colonic and bloody; Crohn is patchy, transmural, anywhere, and fistulating.

👩‍⚕️ Nursing Care

🛏️ Ward priorities — the nurse runs the safety net

  • 📊 Meticulous stool chart — count every stool and record whether it is bloody. The day-3 decision literally depends on this number; a vague "opened bowels ×several" can cost a colon.
  • 📉 Track the trend, not the moment — rising heart rate, falling BP, rising temperature or a distending abdomen are the earliest signs of toxic megacolon. Escalate early.
  • 💧 Strict fluid balance; the inflamed colon and repeated stools cause large, silent losses of water, sodium and potassium.
  • 💉 Give the LMWH — do not skip VTE prophylaxis because the stool is bloody; challenge anyone who tries to hold it without a clear reason.
  • 🚫 Guard the drug chart: no loperamide, codeine, oral morphine or NSAIDs are to reach this patient.
  • 🧾 Psychological care — she is young, frightened, and hearing the word "colectomy". Honest, calm information and dignity around the commode matter enormously.
⚔️ ONE-LINER RULE"The nurse who counts and describes every single stool is holding the most important dataset on the ward — the day-3 rescue decision is only as good as that stool chart."

🧫 Histology — how the pathologist confirms UC

  • Inflammation confined to mucosa/submucosa (transmural inflammation suggests Crohn).
  • Crypt architectural distortion, crypt abscesses, goblet-cell depletion, and a plasma-cell-rich basal infiltrate.
  • NO non-caseating granulomas (their presence points to Crohn).

📟 Monitoring

📡 What to watch and why

  • 📊 Stool chart (frequency + blood) and daily abdominal examination — the two datasets that decide rescue vs colectomy.
  • 🩸 Daily FBC, CRP and U&E — a CRP that will not fall by day 3, or a rising one, predicts steroid failure.
  • 💓 Observations at least 4-hourly — a climbing heart rate, fever, or falling BP flags toxic megacolon or sepsis.
  • 🩻 Repeat AXR whenever the abdomen distends, pain rises, or bowel sounds disappear — measure the transverse colon.
  • 🗓️ A documented day-3 review applying the Oxford criteria, with the surgeon and IBD team present.
⚔️ ONE-LINER RULE"After you start steroids, the monitor's question changes from 'is she severe?' to 'is she RESPONDING?' — and the answer is written in the stool count and the CRP by the morning of day 3."
S13

⚠️ Complications

💀 What can go wrong

  • 🎈 Toxic megacolon — transverse colon >6 cm + systemic toxicity; the feared emergency, precipitated by hypokalaemia, antimotility agents, opioids and colonoscopy.
  • 💥 Perforation — high mortality; may occur with minimal signs in a steroid-treated abdomen. A rising HR with a quiet, distending belly is ominous.
  • 🩸 Severe haemorrhage — occasionally an indication for urgent colectomy.
  • 🫁 Venous thromboembolism — DVT/PE; a leading cause of death in IBD inpatients, which is exactly why we give prophylaxis despite the bleeding.
  • 🦠 Superinfection — C. difficile and CMV worsen a flare and mimic steroid failure.
  • 🕰️ The system complication: delayed decision — leaving a failing patient on steroids past day 3 is the "complication" that turns a manageable flare into a mortality.
⚔️ ONE-LINER RULE"The deadliest complication of acute severe colitis is not the colon — it is the delay. Toxic megacolon and perforation are what happen when a needed colectomy is postponed."

🗺️ Plan + Procedure Guides with Checklists

🧭 The management plan

  1. 🩸 Diagnosis: acute severe UC (Truelove & Witts positive on all criteria; endoscopy + histology confirm continuous mucosal colitis).
  2. 💉 Days 1–3: IV steroids, resuscitation, VTE prophylaxis, stool chart, infection screen, AXR, surgeon & IBD nurse engaged.
  3. 📏 Day 3 gate (Oxford/Travis): if >8 stools/day, OR 3–8 stools with CRP >45 → escalate: rescue with infliximab or ciclosporin, or proceed to colectomy if unsuitable/failing.
  4. 🔪 Colectomy (subtotal colectomy + end ileostomy) for toxic megacolon, perforation, haemorrhage, or failure of rescue by ~day 4–7 — do not delay.
  5. 💊 Maintenance: 5-ASA for mild–moderate disease, thiopurines, biologics (anti-TNF, vedolizumab, ustekinumab) or JAK inhibitors; plan CRC surveillance colonoscopy.
⚔️ ONE-LINER RULE"A well-timed colectomy is a cure, not a failure. The tragedy is never the stoma — it is the perforation that happened because someone could not say the word 'surgery' in time."

🔬 Advanced Topics — Fellowship Level

🧠 Beyond the pathway

  • 💉 Accelerated / intensified infliximab in ASUC: a low albumin and high inflammatory burden drive rapid drug clearance, so some units use accelerated dosing (e.g. earlier or higher-dose infliximab) in the sickest colons — though robust RCT superiority over standard dosing is still debated.
  • 🧬 Predicting steroid failure early: tools such as the Ho index, admission albumin, CRP, stool frequency and endoscopic ulcer depth help flag on day 1–2 the patients likely to need rescue or surgery.
  • 🔀 Sequential rescue is dangerous: switching from ciclosporin to infliximab (or vice versa) after one fails stacks immunosuppression and infection risk for modest gain — most guidelines favour proceeding to colectomy rather than a second rescue.
  • 💊 Newer agents in acute/steroid-refractory UC: JAK inhibitors (tofacitinib, upadacitinib) act fast and have a role in refractory disease; vedolizumab (gut-selective anti-integrin) and ustekinumab (anti-IL-12/23) broaden maintenance options — VTE/lipid caveats apply to JAK drugs.
  • 👁️ CMV reactivation: in the steroid-refractory colon, biopsy with immunohistochemistry/PCR for CMV; significant tissue CMV may warrant ganciclovir before or alongside further immunosuppression.
  • 🔪 Surgical staging: the emergency operation is a subtotal colectomy with end ileostomy (rectum preserved), later followed by completion proctectomy or ileal pouch–anal anastomosis (IPAA) once recovered — pouchitis and surveillance become the long-term issues.

🎭 Morbidity & Mortality Conference

🎭 M&M — "The Day-3 That Nobody Called"

The case: A 30-year-old man with known UC was admitted with acute severe colitis and correctly started on IV hydrocortisone. On day 3 the stool chart read 9 bloody stools and the CRP had barely moved, but the team felt he was "a little better" and elected to continue steroids. No rescue therapy was started and the surgeons were not called. On day 5 he was more distended; an X-ray was "meant to be done" but the round moved on. On day 7 his abdomen was tense and silent, his heart rate 130 — an AXR showed a 9 cm transverse colon. En route to emergency theatre the colon perforated; he developed faecal peritonitis and septic shock and died in intensive care two days later.

🧀 The Swiss-cheese holes that lined up:

🕰️ NO DAY-3 GATEOxford criteria never applied; "a little better" replaced an objective decision.
💉 RESCUE DELAYEDNeither infliximab nor ciclosporin started when steroids clearly failed.
🔪 SURGEON ABSENTColorectal team not engaged early; colectomy considered far too late.
🩻 NO SERIAL AXRColonic dilatation not tracked; toxic megacolon caught only at 9 cm.
📊 SOFT DATAVague stool documentation let the deterioration hide in plain sight.

🛡️ What would have saved him: treat day 3 as a hard gate and apply the Oxford criteria to the stool count and CRP; start rescue (infliximab or ciclosporin) the same morning steroids fail; involve the surgeons from day 1 and consent early; track the colon with serial AXRs; and never let "he seems a bit better" override an objective failure to respond. A timely colectomy is a cure — a delayed one is a death. This is exactly the discipline that saved the patient in Bed 3.

⚔️ ONE-LINER RULE"'He seems a bit better' has killed more colitis patients than any operation. On day 3 the stool count and the CRP decide — not a hopeful glance at the end of the bed."
