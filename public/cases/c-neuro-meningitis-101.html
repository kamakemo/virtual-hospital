🧭 Bedside meningeal signs — helpful when present, useless when absent

  • 🦵 Kernig sign: with hip flexed to 90°, passive knee extension is resisted/painful (hamstring spasm from meningeal irritation).
  • 🙇 Brudzinski sign: passive neck flexion provokes involuntary hip and knee flexion.
  • 📏 Jolt accentuation: headache worsened by rapid horizontal head-turning — sensitive but not specific.
⚠️ The trap of "no neck stiffness, so it is not meningitis"Kernig and Brudzinski are specific but INSENSITIVE (roughly 5–30% sensitive) — their absence never excludes meningitis. Likewise the classic triad (fever + neck stiffness + altered mental state) is present in only ~40–50% of cases, but almost all patients have at least two of {fever, headache, neck stiffness, altered mental state}. Absence of the full triad is a trap, not reassurance.

🧠 Who needs a CT BEFORE the LP — and why

The fear is cerebral herniation ("coning"): removing CSF below a pressure gradient can precipitate tonsillar herniation if there is a mass lesion or raised ICP. So we CT first only when herniation risk is raised — but crucially, the CT and LP both come after the antibiotic.

🧠 Teaching Points + Interactive Simulation

🔬 The mechanism — WHY the danger (the WHY behind the WHY)

  1. 🦠 Colonisation and invasion: Neisseria meningitidis (or S. pneumoniae) colonises the nasopharynx, breaches the mucosa and enters the blood; it crosses the blood–brain barrier into the subarachnoid space where host defences are weak.
  2. 🔥 Subarachnoid inflammation: bacterial cell-wall components (lipopolysaccharide/endotoxin) trigger a cytokine storm (TNF-α, IL-1). This inflames the meninges → headache, neck stiffness, photophobia and disrupts the BBB.
  3. 🧠 Raised ICP and injury: vasogenic + cytotoxic + interstitial oedema, impaired CSF resorption and vasculitis raise ICP and reduce cerebral perfusion → drowsiness, seizures, focal deficit, coning. This is where dexamethasone dampens the harmful inflammatory response.
  4. 💥 Meningococcal sepsis: endotoxaemia drives capillary leak, vasodilatation and DIC → the non-blanching purpuric rash, shock, and adrenal haemorrhage (Waterhouse–Friderichsen syndrome). The rash is thrombosis in the skin — a window onto the clotting catastrophe inside.
⚔️ ONE-LINER RULE"The petechiae are not 'a rash' — they are microthrombi. A spreading non-blanching rash is DIC declaring itself; it means the antibiotic and the resuscitation are already late, not early."

⚖️ Contrast pair — Meningitis vs Meningococcal Sepsis

🧠 Meningitis (the brain lining)

  • Headache, neck stiffness, photophobia, altered mental state
  • Dominant problem is raised ICP + meningeal inflammation
  • Dexamethasone matters most here (deafness/mortality)

💥 Meningococcal sepsis (the blood)

  • Shock, non-blanching purpuric rash, DIC, multi-organ failure
  • Dominant problem is endotoxin-driven shock + coagulopathy
  • Fluids, vasopressors, ICU; dexamethasone benefit is for meningitis, not septicaemia

💡 The same organism, two overlapping emergencies. Our patient has BOTH — treat the brain and the shock at once.

👩‍⚕️ Nursing Care

🛏️ Resus-bay priorities — the nurse runs the safety net

  • 💉 Get the antibiotic in the room FAST — draw the cultures, prime the ceftriaxone, and challenge any plan that sends her to CT with the drug unsigned. The nurse who says "shall I give the ceftriaxone now?" saves brains.
  • 🩸 Track the rash — mark its edge with a pen and time-stamp it; a spreading purpuric rash signals worsening DIC and shock and must be escalated.
  • 📟 Continuous obs; frequent GCS and pupils — a dropping GCS threatens the airway and changes the LP/CT decision.
  • 💧 Deliver fluid boluses for shock, monitor urine output; watch fluid balance for SIADH (hyponatraemia, concentrated urine, falling output).
  • 🌡️ Antipyretics, dark quiet space (photophobia), analgesia for headache; reassure a frightened family.
  • 🛡️ Droplet precautions until 24 h of effective antibiotics; help identify close contacts for prophylaxis and support public-health notification.
⚔️ ONE-LINER RULE"The nurse who refuses to move a suspected-meningitis patient to CT before the antibiotic is given is the single most effective safety barrier in the department."

📟 Monitoring

📡 What to watch and why

  • 🧠 Serial GCS and pupils — falling conscious level or a blown pupil signals rising ICP/coning; escalate to CT, neuroprotection and critical care.
  • 🩸 Spreading rash + repeat coagulation/platelets/fibrinogen — tracks DIC and the need for blood products.
  • 💧 Fluid balance + sodium — hyponatraemia with concentrated urine = SIADH; a common early complication requiring fluid adjustment.
  • 📉 Lactate, MAP, urine output — the sepsis picture; guide fluids/vasopressors and ICU escalation.
  • ⚡ Seizure watch — cortical irritation/vasculitis can trigger seizures; treat and re-image if new focal signs appear.
  • 👂 Early audiology plan — hearing loss is the commonest sequela; arrange formal testing before discharge.
⚔️ ONE-LINER RULE"After the antibiotic is in, the monitor's question becomes: is the ICP rising (GCS/pupils), is the DIC winning (rash/clotting), and is the sodium drifting (SIADH)? Those three trends drive the next hour."
S13

⚠️ Complications

💀 What can go wrong

  • 💥 Meningococcal septic shock + DIC / Waterhouse–Friderichsen syndrome — capillary leak, purpura fulminans, bilateral adrenal haemorrhage; the fastest killer.
  • 🧠 Raised ICP / cerebral oedema / coning — the reason CT precedes LP in the at-risk patient.
  • 💧 SIADH — hyponatraemia is common; can worsen cerebral oedema and cause seizures.
  • ⚡ Seizures — from cortical irritation, vasculitic infarction or hyponatraemia.
  • 👂 Sensorineural deafness — the commonest long-term sequela (dexamethasone reduces it); also cognitive impairment and focal deficits.
  • 🌊 Hydrocephalus — impaired CSF resorption; may need CSF diversion. Plus subdural empyema, cerebral abscess, ventriculitis, venous sinus thrombosis.
⚔️ ONE-LINER RULE"Meningitis has two killing modes — the brain (coning, oedema, hydrocephalus) and the blood (shock, DIC, adrenal haemorrhage). You must resuscitate both at once; treating one and forgetting the other loses the patient."

🗺️ Plan + Procedure Guides with Checklists

🧭 The management plan

  1. 🦠 Diagnosis: acute bacterial (meningococcal) meningitis with septic shock and early DIC. Cultures + PCR drawn; ceftriaxone + dexamethasone given within minutes.
  2. 💧 Resuscitate the shock in parallel — fluids, vasopressors if needed, ICU; correct coagulopathy with products as guided.
  3. 🧠 CT then LP once stable (CT indicated by falling GCS); MRI later if complications suspected.
  4. 📞 Public-health notification same day; identify close contacts; arrange chemoprophylaxis (± vaccination) for contacts.
  5. 🏥 Admit to ICU/HDU; audiology follow-up; monitor for SIADH, seizures, hydrocephalus; de-escalate antibiotics once organism/sensitivities known.
⚔️ ONE-LINER RULE"The plan is a single sentence: cultures, ceftriaxone, dexamethasone, resuscitate, notify — then image, tap, and protect the contacts. Anything that reorders 'antibiotic' to later is the mistake."

🏠 Discharge

📋 Recovery discharge vs the public-health duty — two parallel worlds

🧠 The patient (recovering meningitis)

  • Complete the antibiotic course; formal audiology before/after discharge (deafness screen)
  • Neuro follow-up for cognition, seizures, focal deficit; safety-net for late hydrocephalus (headache, drowsiness, vomiting)
  • Check/complete meningococcal vaccination as advised
  • Red flags to return: recurrent fever, worsening headache, drowsiness, new rash, seizure

📞 The public-health duty (contacts)

  • Statutory notification to public health the same day
  • Chemoprophylaxis for close contacts (e.g. ciprofloxacin/rifampicin) — household, kissing, halls contacts
  • Vaccination of contacts if a vaccine-preventable serogroup
  • Clear written information for the university/household
⚔️ ONE-LINER RULE"Discharging the patient is only half the job — meningococcal disease has a second patient list: the contacts. Notify public health and arrange prophylaxis the SAME day, before you close the case."

🔬 Advanced Topics — Fellowship Level

🧠 Beyond the pathway

  • 🧬 Multiplex CSF PCR panels (e.g. meningitis/encephalitis film-array) give rapid organism identification — but interpret with the clinical picture and never let a pending panel delay empirical therapy; false negatives/positives occur.
  • 💥 Purpura fulminans & DIC management: aggressive product support, the debated roles of protein C concentrate and the physiology of Waterhouse–Friderichsen adrenal haemorrhage (relative adrenal insufficiency → steroid consideration in refractory shock).
  • 🧠 Refractory raised ICP in meningitis: neuro-critical-care measures, when to consider ICP monitoring, osmotherapy and CSF diversion for hydrocephalus; the delicate LP-vs-herniation calculus.
  • 🛡️ Serogroup epidemiology & vaccines: MenACWY vs MenB (4CMenB) programmes, serogroup shifts, and why vaccination reduces but does not eliminate individual risk; the role of PCR serogrouping in outbreak control.
  • 🧫 Resistant & atypical organisms: penicillin/cephalosporin-resistant pneumococcus (add vancomycin per local resistance/travel), Listeria in the vulnerable, and TB/cryptococcal meningitis in the immunocompromised (lymphocytic, low-glucose CSF).
  • 💊 Steroid nuance: dexamethasone benefit is greatest in pneumococcal meningitis in high-income settings; discontinue if a non-pneumococcal/non-Hib organism is confirmed, and recognise its limited role in pure meningococcal septicaemia.

🎭 Morbidity & Mortality Conference

🎭 M&M — "The Antibiotic That Waited for the Scan"

The case: An 18-year-old student arrived at 20:00 with fever, severe headache, photophobia and a few petechiae. Meningitis was correctly suspected — but the team decided to "do it properly": book an urgent CT, then an LP, and give antibiotics after the tap "so the cultures are not contaminated." The porter was busy; the scanner had a queue. The ceftriaxone, prescribed at 20:20, sat unsigned in the tray. By 21:50 — 90 minutes later — the LP was finally done and the antibiotic given. In that hour and a half she developed a spreading purpuric rash, went into DIC and septic shock, and arrested. She was resuscitated but survived with bilateral below-knee amputations and profound bilateral deafness.

🧀 The Swiss-cheese holes that lined up:

💉 ANTIBIOTIC DELAYEDCeftriaxone withheld until AFTER CT + LP — the single fatal decision.
🧫 CULTURE FALLACYFear of a "contaminated" culture; PCR would have made the diagnosis anyway.
🏥 SYSTEM DELAYScanner queue + porter delay turned "urgent" into 90 minutes.
🩸 RASH IGNOREDSpreading petechiae (early DIC) not escalated as an emergency.
💊 DEXAMETHASONE MISSEDNo steroid before the antibiotic — losing its deafness-sparing benefit.

🛡️ What would have saved her: draw cultures in seconds, then give IV ceftriaxone + dexamethasone immediately — CT and LP come afterwards and PCR still yields the diagnosis; treat a spreading purpuric rash as DIC and resuscitate hard; and never let "we will do the LP first for a clean culture" hold the drug. This is exactly the discipline that saved the patient in Bed 1 — twelve minutes to the needle, not ninety.

⚔️ ONE-LINER RULE"'We will give antibiotics after the LP' has crippled and killed more meningitis patients than any resistant organism. The antibiotic is the resuscitation — the tap is paperwork by comparison."
