🚦 What actually justifies a CTO PCI

A CTO is a 100% (TIMI-0) occlusion estimated to be ≥ 3 months old — recognised by a blunt or tapered proximal cap, bridging collaterals, and a distal vessel filled retrogradely by collaterals. Unlike a STEMI, there is no mortality mandate and no clock. CTO PCI is an elective, symptom- and viability-driven procedure: you open it to relieve angina, improve quality of life, and recruit viable/hibernating myocardium — not to save a life that is not acutely threatened.

⚔️ ONE-LINER RULE"CTO PCI treats the patient, not the angiogram. No symptoms and no viable myocardium behind the occlusion means no procedure — you can not improve a scar, and you can not relieve angina that is not there."

📋 Pre-Procedure Workup & Planning

🧾 The CTO planning bundle — do this BEFORE the wire touches the cap

  • 🧲 Prove viability & ischaemia — MRI/PET/stress imaging: opening a scar helps nobody. Confirm the target territory is alive and ischaemic.
  • 🎞️ Review the prior/diagnostic angiogram frame-by-frame — define the proximal cap (tapered vs blunt), occlusion length, calcification, tortuosity, distal target quality, and the collateral roadmap for a possible retrograde approach.
  • 🔢 Calculate the J-CTO score — it predicts difficulty and time (see S5): blunt stump, calcification, bending > 45°, length > 20 mm, prior failed attempt.
  • 🩸 Plan dual arterial access & anticoagulation — most CTOs need dual injection; retrograde needs two guides (see S3).
  • ☢️💧 Set radiation & contrast budgets up front — long cases: agree a fluoro-time/air-kerma ceiling and a contrast ceiling (roughly ≤ 3.5 × eGFR) before you start, and a pre-declared "walk-away" point.
  • 🩺 Consent honestly — success is not guaranteed; risks include perforation/tamponade, radiation skin injury, contrast nephropathy, donor-vessel injury, equipment loss, and the option of a staged second attempt.
⚔️ ONE-LINER RULE"Plan the CTO on the previous angiogram, not on the table. The operator who has already mapped the cap, the length and the collaterals has half-finished the case before the first wire."

🩸 Vascular Access & Setup

🩸 Why CTO usually means DUAL access & big-bore support

🔀 Dual injection (the CTO golden rule)

  • Two catheters — one in the CTO (donor of antegrade contrast) vessel and one in the collateral-donor vessel
  • Simultaneous injection shows the distal cap, true course and collaterals beyond the occlusion
  • Wiring a CTO on a single injection is wiring blind past the cap

🧭 Guide & support

  • 7–8 Fr guides (radial or femoral) for support and to accommodate microcatheters, ADR kit, retrograde gear
  • Left system: EBU/XB; RCA: AL/JR; consider guide-extension catheters for deep support
  • Retrograde work needs a second guide in the collateral-donor artery
⚔️ ONE-LINER RULE"Dual injection first, wire second. The single most important image in a CTO case is the contralateral injection that shows you the landing zone you are aiming for."

💉 Microcatheters, wires & anticoagulation

  • 🧵 Microcatheter over every wire — it supports the wire, lets you exchange wires, straightens tortuosity and protects the vessel. Over-the-wire is mandatory in CTO.
  • 🪢 Wire escalation family — from polymer-jacketed tapered wires (glide through microchannels) to high-tip-load stiff, tapered penetration wires (drill a blunt/hard cap). Knuckle a polymer wire to travel a long occlusion safely.
  • 🩸 Anticoagulation — different rules from a STEMI: use unfractionated heparin and target a higher ACT ≥ 300–350 s (thrombus forms readily on stagnant CTO/retrograde equipment). Avoid bivalirudin in CTO — you may need to keep an antegrade balloon inflated for a long time and you want reversibility with protamine if you perforate.

S5

🔬 Lesion Assessment — J-CTO, Viability & Donor Physiology

🚧 You do NOT FFR a CTOThe target is 100% occluded — its physiology is a foregone conclusion (no antegrade flow at all). Physiology in a CTO case has a different job: assess the DONOR vessel. Before you run equipment retrograde through collaterals, an unrecognised significant stenosis in the collateral-donor artery can turn a controlled case into global ischaemia and shock. Imaging (IVUS) instead helps you find an ambiguous proximal cap and confirm a true-lumen distal landing.

🔢 The J-CTO score — predicting difficulty & procedural time

The J-CTO (Multicenter CTO Registry of Japan) score assigns one point each; higher scores predict lower guidewire-crossing success within 30 minutes and a longer, harder case.

PointFeatureWhy it hurts
1️⃣Blunt / no tapered stumpNo microchannel entry — the wire has nowhere to start
1️⃣Calcification at the occlusionDeflects and resists penetration wires
1️⃣Bending > 45° within the occlusionWire cannot track the true course; subintimal risk
1️⃣Occlusion length > 20 mmMore vessel to cross blindly; longer dissection planes
1️⃣Prior failed attemptScar, altered anatomy, false lumens from last time
Interpretation: 0 = easy · 1 = intermediate · 2 = difficult · ≥ 3 = very difficult → plan for retrograde/ADR, longer time, and firm radiation/contrast ceilings.
🧮 This case scores J-CTO 3Blunt cap (1) + calcification (1) + occlusion length > 20 mm (1) = 3 → "very difficult." No bending > 45°, no prior attempt. Expect a long case; have the retrograde and ADR trays open from the start.

🎮 Simulation — The Donor-Vessel FFR Dial (before you go retrograde)

Before running gear retrograde through the LAD collaterals, pressure-wire the donor LAD. If the donor is significantly diseased, the whole heart is at risk during the retrograde work. Treat or protect the donor — or reconsider retrograde.

Set the dial to explore the donor-vessel decision.

🎯 Donor FFR ≤ 0.80 → significant donor disease: treat the donor first (or avoid retrograde) — the donor supplies the CTO territory too. > 0.80 → donor safe to borrow as a retrograde conduit.

🖥️ Intravascular imaging in CTO — IVUS earns its keep

🔵 IVUS (the CTO workhorse)

  • Finds an ambiguous / flush proximal cap at a side branch (IVUS-guided puncture)
  • Confirms true vs subintimal position of wire and re-entry
  • Sizes the vessel and guides stent expansion in a long, calcified target

🟠 OCT

  • Higher resolution for stent apposition & edge dissection after crossing
  • Needs a contrast flush — used sparingly in a contrast-conscious CTO case
  • Less useful than IVUS for the ostial/ambiguous-cap problem
⚔️ ONE-LINER RULE"Do not FFR the CTO — pressure-wire the donor. The occlusion is already 100%; the question that changes your plan is whether the vessel you want to borrow can pay the debt."

🎬 Strategy & Decision Point — "Choose Your Path"

J-CTO is 3, the donor LAD is clean, and there is a CC2 septal collateral to the distal RCA. Now the branching core — pick the crossing strategy using the Hybrid algorithm. Correct calls score; wrong calls teach.

🧭 The Hybrid algorithm in one breathAsk four questions of the angiogram: proximal cap (clear vs ambiguous), lesion length (< or > 20 mm), distal target (good vs diseased/bifurcation), and collaterals (interventional or not). Then flow between four techniques and switch early when one stalls: antegrade wire escalation → antegrade dissection re-entry (ADR) → retrograde wiring → retrograde dissection re-entry (reverse CART).

S7

🛠️ Intervention — Step-by-Step Execution

🎈 After crossing, the CTO is prepped and stented like any lesion — the artistry was getting the wire across. Source: Wikimedia Commons (Blausen)

🪜 The Hybrid sequence — each arrow is a decision to switch

  1. 🧵 Antegrade wire escalation (AWE) — microcatheter support, dual injection, escalate from soft polymer/tapered wires to stiff penetration wires to cross the cap into the distal true lumen.
  2. 🪃 Antegrade dissection & re-entry (ADR) — if you enter the subintimal space, do not fight it: use a knuckle wire or the CrossBoss catheter to travel the plane, then re-enter the true lumen distally with the Stingray balloon + Stingray wire. Keep the dissection short — limit it to the occluded segment.
  3. 🔁 Retrograde access — cross a CC2 septal collateral with a soft polymer wire + microcatheter (septal surfing/dilation), reach the distal cap from below.
  4. ↔️ CART / reverse CART — controlled antegrade and retrograde subintimal tracking: overlap antegrade and retrograde dissection planes and connect them (usually an antegrade balloon enlarges the space for the retrograde wire — reverse CART, the workhorse).
  5. 🎣 Externalise & convert — snare/externalise the retrograde wire, then treat antegrade: IVUS-confirm the true lumen, prep the calcified segment, and deploy drug-eluting stents from healthy proximal to healthy distal, post-dilating to full expansion.
⚔️ ONE-LINER RULE"In a CTO you are allowed to leave the true lumen on purpose — but only inside the occlusion, and only if you re-enter promptly. A dissection that extends beyond the cap into a healthy segment is a complication, not a technique."

S10

🏠 Post-Procedure Care, Debrief & Assessment

🩹 After a long CTO case

  • 🩸 Access sites — dual access means double the vigilance: radial occlusion, femoral haematoma/pseudoaneurysm/retroperitoneal bleed. Confirm ACT has fallen before removing large sheaths.
  • 🫗 Watch for delayed tamponade — a wire/collateral perforation can bleed hours later; a post-procedure echo and a low threshold for repeat imaging if hypotensive.
  • 🫘 Renal watch — high contrast load: hydrate, recheck creatinine at 48–72 h for contrast-induced nephropathy.
  • ☢️ Radiation follow-up — document cumulative air kerma; if > 5 Gy, warn the patient about possible skin erythema and arrange dermatology follow-up (deterministic skin injury).

💊 Secondary prevention & antiplatelets

  • DAPT — aspirin + a P2Y₁₂ inhibitor after DES; duration individualised, but a long/complex CTO with overlapping stents and possible subintimal tracking argues for a full standard course.
  • High-intensity statin, blood-pressure and diabetes control, smoking abstinence, cardiac rehab.
  • 🩺 Re-assess symptoms & ischaemia — the endpoint of a CTO PCI is angina relief and quality of life; document the CCS class change at follow-up.
⚔️ ONE-LINER RULE"You opened the artery to give him his life back, not to win the angiogram. Follow up the symptoms, the kidneys and the skin — the CTO success that matters is measured weeks later at the clinic, not on the table."

🔭 Advanced Topics — Fellowship Level

  • 🪃 Dissection/re-entry hardware: CrossBoss (blunt micro-dissection catheter) + Stingray balloon/wire for targeted subintimal re-entry; the "STAR" technique and its limited-antegrade-subintimal-tracking (LAST) variants — with awareness of side-branch loss and higher re-occlusion in extensive subintimal tracking.
  • ↔️ CART vs reverse CART: in CART the retrograde balloon enlarges the subintimal space; in reverse CART (the modern default) the antegrade balloon does, and the retrograde wire crosses into it — IVUS-guided reverse CART fine-tunes balloon sizing and overlap.
  • 🎣 Wire externalisation & snaring: long externalisation wires (e.g., 330 cm) delivered retrograde and snared antegrade to create a rail; guard against donor-vessel/collateral trauma and "cheese-wiring."
  • 🫀 Haemodynamic support in CTO: in a last-remaining-vessel CTO or severe LV dysfunction, consider Impella/mechanical support because retrograde work can transiently ischaemise a large territory.
  • ☢️ Radiation dose management: low frame-rate fluoroscopy, minimal cine, collimation, table/detector positioning, stored fluoro loops, and hard air-kerma alarms — deterministic skin injury begins around 5 Gy and rises steeply above 10–15 Gy.
  • 🖥️ IVUS as a crossing tool: IVUS-guided proximal-cap puncture for flush/ambiguous ostial CTOs, and IVUS-guided re-entry — imaging turns a blind puncture into a targeted one.

🎭 Morbidity & Mortality Conference

🎭 M&M — "The Epicardial Channel That Bled"

The case: A long, difficult RCA CTO. Antegrade had failed; the operator went retrograde. The only usable collateral was an epicardial channel — no good septal option — and it was taken despite the risk. Crossing it, the wire tip caused a small perforation of the epicardial collateral. It was dismissed as a "septal stain" and the case continued. Minutes later the BP fell; the pericardium was filling. The patient was still fully heparinised. By the time the team recognised an epicardial collateral perforation → tamponade, the channel could not be quickly occluded from both ends, protamine was late, and the pericardiocentesis kit was not open. The patient arrested and died on the table.

🧀 The Swiss-cheese holes that lined up:

🫗 EPICARDIAL CHANNEL CHOSENA dangerous epicardial collateral was used when there was no safe septal — the risk was not re-weighed against deferral.
🙈 MISLABELLED THE LEAKAn epicardial perforation was called a harmless "septal stain" — it bleeds into the pericardium, not muscle.
🩸 STILL ANTICOAGULATEDFull heparin left the collateral bleeding freely into the pericardium.
🎯 ONE-END OCCLUSION THINKINGAn epicardial channel must be occluded at BOTH ends; a covered stent cannot seal a collateral.
🫗 KIT NOT READYThe pericardiocentesis set was not open and drainage lost the race to tamponade.

🛡️ What would have saved him: prefer a septal collateral and reconsider deferral when only a hostile epicardial channel exists; the instant contrast stains the pericardium during retrograde work, name it an epicardial perforation, reverse the heparin with protamine, occlude the channel at both ends (coils / fat / thrombin, from either access), and have the pericardiocentesis kit already open to drain the tamponade — surgery on stand-by. This is the drill in S9.

⚔️ ONE-LINER RULE"When a retrograde case turns red, ask first: septal or epicardial? Epicardial means the pericardium is filling now — reverse, embolise both ends, and drain, before you take one more picture."

  4. 🫗 Respect the collaterals — septal over epicardial; an epicardial perforation is a tamponade you occlude at both ends and reverse.
  5. ☢️💧 Steward radiation & contrast — ceilings, a walk-away point, and skin follow-up above ~5 Gy.
