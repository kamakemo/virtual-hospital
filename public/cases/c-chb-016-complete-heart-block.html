🧬 Why THIS patient? — a dying conductor, a drug load, and a slow escape

In complete (third-degree) AV block, the atria and ventricles are electrically divorced: no atrial impulse reaches the ventricle at all. The sinus node keeps firing the atria at its own rate, but the message dies somewhere in the AV node or the His-Purkinje system. The ventricle survives only because a subsidiary pacemaker downstream of the block takes over — an escape rhythm. In Mrs. E.M. the escape is wide and slow (28/min), meaning it arises low in the ventricular conduction tissue rather than the junction. Layered on top of an aging, fibrosing conduction system are three AV-nodal-blocking drugs and a kidney that lets digoxin and potassium accumulate. The substrate (fibrosis) was decades in the making; the drugs and the slow escape are tonight's emergency. She blacks out each time the escape pacemaker pauses — that is a Stokes-Adams attack, and the next pause may not restart.

🔥 Risk factors / causes of complete heart block 🫀 Age-related conduction-system fibrosis (Lev/Lenègre) · 💔 Inferior or anterior MI · 💊 AV-nodal-blocking drugs (beta-blocker, non-DHP CCB, digoxin, amiodarone) · ⚡ Hyperkalemia · 🦟 Lyme carditis / infective endocarditis with aortic-root abscess · 🧬 Cardiac sarcoidosis, amyloid · 👶 Congenital (maternal anti-Ro/SSA) · ❤️ Post valve surgery / TAVI

Mrs. E.M. has shock, recurrent syncope and early pulmonary congestion — UNSTABLE.

🎯 Immediate priorities (her actual order of operations)

  2. 💉 Atropine 0.5 mg IV once while the pacing kit is set up — it may help if any nodal component, but do not expect a wide infranodal escape to answer, and do not delay pacing repeating it.

  5. 🧪 Hunt reversible causes in parallel: a STAT potassium (hyperkalemia!), digoxin level, troponin/ECG for ischemia, and ask about ticks/Lyme. Treat what you find.

🤔 "But she's still conscious between drops — can't I just watch?"

"No. Recurrent Stokes-Adams syncope from a wide, slow escape is a warning shot: the escape pacemaker is unreliable and can fail to asystole at any moment. Watching is how you find her in cardiac arrest. Pads on, atropine drawn up, pacer set, EP called — all in parallel. The fact that she keeps recovering is luck, not stability."

🧪 Resident's self-test (answer before reading on) Someone suggests giving a second, then a third dose of atropine because "the first didn't work — maybe more will." Good idea in this patient?

No. Her escape is wide and therefore infranodal — atropine acts on the AV node, above the level of the block, and predictably won't help. Atropine can even (rarely) worsen things by speeding atrial firing without ventricular response. Give it once while preparing the real treatment, then move to transcutaneous pacing and chronotropic infusions as a bridge to transvenous pacing. Repeating atropine for a wide escape while delaying pacing is the classic fatal error (see the M&M in S13).

The width of the escape is the bedside fluoroscope into the conduction system. Mrs. E.M.'s escape is wide at 28: an infranodal block with a fragile escape. "She is alive on a low ventricular escape — treat it like the candle in the wind it is. 📖 Teaching Points

🔬 The mechanism — WHY the escape rhythm is everything (the WHY behind the WHY)

  1. 🧱 The block: in third-degree AV block, conduction through the AV node / His-Purkinje system fails completely. Not a single atrial impulse reaches the ventricle. The atria and ventricles become electrically divorced.
  2. 👫 Two independent drummers: the sinus node keeps pacing the atria at its rate; a subsidiary pacemaker below the block paces the ventricles at its rate. Because they are independent, the atrial rate is faster than the ventricular rate, and the P–QRS relationship is random — AV dissociation. Cannon A waves and variable S1 are the bedside signs.
  3. 🔋 The escape pacemaker decides life or death: a junctional escape (narrow QRS, 40–60/min) is relatively reliable; a ventricular escape (wide QRS, 20–40/min) is slow and unreliable. The lower the focus, the slower and more fragile the rhythm.
  4. 🫥 Why she faints (Stokes-Adams): whenever the escape pacemaker pauses, the ventricle is briefly silent → no cardiac output → abrupt loss of consciousness with no warning, then sudden recovery when the escape resumes. Each pause is a near-miss; one pause may not restart.
  5. 💥 Why it kills: a wide ventricular escape can fail to asystole without warning. There is no slow decline — the candle simply goes out. That is why a symptomatic wide-complex complete block earns a pacemaker, not observation.
⚔️ ONE-LINER RULE"Pacing resets nothing it just guarantees a beat — give the ventricle a reliable drummer, then go find why its own one failed."

⚖️ Contrast pair #1 — Complete block with NARROW vs WIDE escape

🟢 Narrow (junctional) escape

  • Arises high in the AV junction
  • Rate 40–60/min, relatively reliable
  • Often AV-nodal block (inferior MI, vagal, drugs)
  • Atropine may help
  • May recover; still pace if symptomatic/persistent

🔴 Wide (ventricular) escape (tonight)

  • Arises low in the ventricular Purkinje network
  • Rate 20–40/min, slow and unreliable
  • Infranodal block (anterior MI, advanced fibrosis)
  • Atropine won't help — pace
  • Can fail to asystole — needs urgent + permanent pacing

😈 The trap: seeing "complete heart block" and reflexively reaching for atropine because that's the bradycardia drug. The width of the escape decides whether atropine has any chance at all. A wide escape lives below the node and ignores atropine; the safe move is to pace. The asymmetry of harm — a missed pause is fatal — is why the rule is "get the pads on."

⚖️ Contrast pair #2 — Mobitz I vs Mobitz II vs Third-degree

📈 Mobitz I (Wenckebach)

  • Progressive PR lengthening → a single dropped QRS, then repeats
  • Usually AV-nodal and benign
  • Often just observe if asymptomatic

📉 Mobitz II / Third-degree

  • Mobitz II: constant PR then sudden dropped QRS; infranodal/dangerous
  • Third-degree: complete AV dissociation, independent regular P and R, atrial rate > ventricular
  • Both earn a permanent pacemaker

💡 Why it matters: "Mobitz II and third-degree block earn a pacemaker; Wenckebach usually just earns observation." Mistake a dangerous infranodal block for benign Wenckebach and you send home a patient who will collapse.

🔬 Fellowship Corner — Advanced Topics

  • 🗺️ The His bundle electrogram: the level of block can be localized at EP study — AH-interval prolongation (intranodal, benign, atropine-responsive) vs HV-interval prolongation / split His (infranodal, dangerous, pacing-dependent).
  • 🧬 Congenital complete heart block: classically associated with maternal anti-Ro/SSA antibodies; the escape is often narrow and faster, and patients can be surprisingly stable for years — but still need pacing for symptoms or a slow/wide escape.
  • 🫀 Infiltrative & inflammatory causes: cardiac sarcoidosis (image with cardiac MRI/PET — new AV block in a young/middle-aged patient should trigger a sarcoid hunt), amyloid, endocarditis with an aortic-root abscess eroding into the conduction system (new AV block + fever + murmur = abscess until proven otherwise).
  • 💉 Pacing modes: dual-chamber (DDD) pacing restores AV synchrony; consider conduction-system pacing (His-bundle or left-bundle-branch-area pacing) or biventricular pacing to avoid pacing-induced cardiomyopathy in patients expected to pace a high percentage of beats.
  • 🔌 Iatrogenic / procedural block: AV block after TAVI (right-bundle-branch baseline predicts it), after surgical aortic/mitral valve replacement, or post-AV-node ablation — anticipate it and have pacing ready.
  • 🧪 Bradycardia & Torsades: profound bradycardia from complete block lengthens the QT and can precipitate pause-dependent Torsades — another reason to pace and to watch the QT.

📋 Orders

🖊️ The order set, written as you'd actually write it (08:48)

  • 🛑 STOP bisoprolol, diltiazem and digoxin immediately — flag on chart and whiteboard as AV-nodal-blocking drugs driving the block.
  • 💉 Atropine 0.5 mg IV — single attempt while pacing is set up; do NOT repeat into a wide escape while delaying pacing.
  • 🔌 Transcutaneous pacing — pads on, set rate ~70/min, increase output until electrical AND mechanical capture (palpable pulse matching the pacer); give analgesia/sedation (it is painful).
  • 💊 Chronotropic bridge if pacing delayed/poor capture: isoprenaline infusion, or dopamine 5–20 mcg/kg/min, or adrenaline 2–10 mcg/min titrated to rate/perfusion.
  • 🧪 STAT potassium and venous gas (hyperkalemia!), digoxin level, magnesium, calcium; treat hyperkalemia (calcium gluconate, insulin/dextrose) if present.
  • 🫀 12-lead ECG and serial troponin — exclude inferior/anterior MI as the cause; activate cath lab if STEMI driving the block.
  • 📞 Refer cardiology/EP urgently for transvenous temporary pacing then permanent pacemaker; consider Lyme serology if endemic exposure.
  • 🫁 Oxygen to SpO₂ ≥ 94%, continuous cardiac monitoring, two large-bore IVs, NPO pending procedure.
  • 🔌 Keep defibrillator/pacing pads attached and the pacer charged-ready — anticipate sudden asystole if the escape fails.

👩‍⚕️ Nursing Care

🛏️ Resus/CCU nursing priorities — minute by minute

  • 📟 Continuous telemetry with the pacer/defibrillator at the bedside, pads attached. Keep the machine in PACER mode, set and ready, in case the escape pauses.
  • 🔌 Know capture cold: a pacing spike is not enough — confirm electrical capture (spike → wide QRS) AND mechanical capture (a real pulse matching the rate). The monitor can show "capture" with no pulse.
  • 🩸 BP every 2–5 min (cycle the NIBP, or read the arterial line once placed). A pause in the escape = sudden hypotension/syncope; escalate now, not at the next set of obs.
  • 🧪 Send and chase the STAT potassium and digoxin level; double-check that all AV-nodal-blocking drugs are held — giving the next scheduled dose would be catastrophic.
  • 💤 Prepare analgesia/sedation before transcutaneous pacing — it hurts; assign a team member to the airway.
  • 🧠 Neuro/consciousness checks — a patient who suddenly drops is having a Stokes-Adams attack; check the rhythm and pulse, be ready to pace/CPR.
  • 📋 Document the rhythm strip before and after each intervention, the pacing output and capture threshold, and the time — gold for the EP team.
  • 🔇 Keep the patient warm and calm; reassure her between episodes — repeated frightening blackouts are distressing.
  • 👨‍👩‍👦 Keep the family updated and supported (see S16) — assign a liaison if staffing allows; watching repeated collapses is terrifying.
⚔️ ONE-LINER RULE"The nurse who confirms a real pulse with every paced beat and guards against the next scheduled beta-blocker dose is doing half the work in the room."
📢 Escalate IMMEDIATELY forLoss of pulse / asystole (→ CPR + pace + arrest call) · failure to capture (loss of pulse despite pacing — increase output, reposition pads) · escape rate falling further or longer pauses · new chest pain / ST changes · falling consciousness · a high STAT potassium · any attempt to give a held AV-nodal-blocking drug.

📈 ECG / Imaging

🫀 The presenting rhythm — read it like a detective

Complete (third-degree) AV block: regular P waves march at one rate while a slower, wide ventricular escape marches at its own — the two are completely dissociated. Atrial rate > ventricular rate; both are regular but unrelated. This is AV dissociation due to complete block.

🔎 The complete-heart-block toolkit (memorize the high-yield ones)

FeatureWhy it means complete block
🔀 AV dissociationAtria and ventricles fire independently — no P wave conducts. P waves and QRS each regular, but unrelated.
🐌 Atrial rate > ventricular rateThe sinus node outpaces the slower escape pacemaker — the defining direction of the dissociation in complete block.
📏 Regular R–RThe escape pacemaker is metronomic; the ventricular rhythm is regular even though P waves wander through it.
📐 QRS width localizes the blockNarrow QRS (40–60/min) = junctional escape (higher, more reliable); wide QRS (20–40/min) = ventricular escape (lower, infranodal, unreliable).
🧭 Marching P waves "through" the QRSP waves appear before, on, and after QRS complexes with no fixed PR — they are not driving the ventricle at all.
💥 Cannon A waves clinicallyThe ECG correlate of dissociation is felt at the neck: intermittent giant A waves as the atrium contracts against a shut tricuspid valve.
⚖️ Contrast with Mobitz I/IISome P waves still conduct in second-degree block; in third-degree none do — that's the dividing line.
⚔️ ONE-LINER RULE"Regular P, regular R, atrial faster than ventricular, and no fixed PR — the atria and ventricles have stopped talking. That's complete heart block, and the QRS width tells you how scared to be."

🫀 12-lead, echo & further imaging

  • 📈 12-lead: confirm complete AV dissociation; measure the escape QRS width and axis; look for inferior or anterior MI changes and hyperkalemia clues (peaked T waves, widening QRS).
  • 🩻 Echo: assess LV function, valves (endocarditis/abscess), and any regional wall-motion abnormality suggesting ischemic cause; exclude an aortic-root abscess in the right setting.
  • 🧲 Cardiac MRI / PET (later): if cause unclear or patient young — hunt for cardiac sarcoidosis or other infiltration, which changes management beyond pacing.
⚠️ The ECG's favorite lie in bradycardia"It's just sinus bradycardia / a junctional rhythm, give atropine." March out the P waves: if they are regular and faster than a regular, unrelated ventricular rhythm, it is complete heart block, not simple bradycardia — and a wide escape will not answer atropine. Always look for the dissociated, marching P waves.

💊 Medications

💉 Acute drugs — what, why, and the traps

🚫 The 'never' list in unstable complete heart block❌ Repeated atropine for a wide escape while delaying pacing · ❌ giving the next scheduled beta-blocker / CCB / digoxin dose · ❌ ignoring a STAT potassium of 5.9 · ❌ "observing" a symptomatic wide-complex complete block. When the escape is wide and she is symptomatic, the safest "drug" is a pacing wire.

🏠 Definitive & longer-term therapy (this admission, not just tonight)

  • 📟 Permanent pacemaker for irreversible/symptomatic complete block and Mobitz II — Class I indication. Usually dual-chamber (DDD) to restore AV synchrony.
  • 🔁 Reassess reversibility first: if hyperkalemia/digoxin/drugs/Lyme fully explain it and conduction recovers, a temporary wire may bridge to recovery without a permanent device.
  • 🧬 Treat the underlying cause: antibiotics for Lyme carditis, immunosuppression for sarcoid, revascularization for ischemia.
  • 💙 Review the whole medication list: rationalize antihypertensives, avoid re-stacking AV-nodal blockers, and adjust renally-cleared drugs for her CKD.
S12

📟 Monitoring

📡 What to watch and why

  • 📈 Continuous telemetry with the pacer at the bedside until definitive pacing is in place; print strips of any pauses, escape-rate changes or capture loss.
  • 🔌 Capture surveillance: if pacing, watch for loss of electrical OR mechanical capture (pulse not matching the pacer) — increase output, reposition pads, escalate.
  • 🩸 Invasive arterial line for beat-to-beat BP through pacing and chronotropic infusions; perfusion is the truth, not the number on the monitor.
  • 🧪 Serial potassium and digoxin level — the most modifiable drivers; confirm the hyperkalemia is corrected and the digoxin is falling.
  • 📐 QTc on every 12-lead — profound bradycardia lengthens the QT and risks pause-dependent Torsades; pacing also shortens these pauses.
  • 🫀 Serial troponin / repeat ECG — re-evaluate for evolving MI as the cause of block.
  • 📟 Once a permanent pacemaker is placed: check thresholds, sensing, and percentage paced; watch for lead complications and pacing-induced cardiomyopathy if pacing a high percentage.
⚔️ ONE-LINER RULE"After you secure pacing, the monitor's job changes from 'is she conducting?' to 'is every pacer spike making a real pulse?' — capture is something you feel, not just something you see."
S13

⚠️ Complications

💀 What can go wrong — and the mechanism

  • ⚡ Asystole / cardiac arrest — the great fear; a wide ventricular escape fails without warning and the ventricle goes silent. Treatment pivots to CPR + pacing + the arrest algorithm.
  • 🫥 Recurrent Stokes-Adams syncope — each pause in the escape causes a collapse and risks injury (she fell face-first); the next pause may be terminal.
  • 🧠 Cardiogenic shock / multi-organ hypoperfusion — sustained output of a 28/min heart starves the brain, kidneys and gut.
  • 📐 Pause-dependent Torsades de Pointes — profound bradycardia lengthens the QT and can precipitate polymorphic VT.
  • 🔌 Pacing complications — failure to capture, pad burns/pain (transcutaneous); pneumothorax, lead displacement, RV perforation/tamponade, infection (transvenous/permanent).
  • 🫀 Missed reversible cause — if hyperkalemia, digoxin toxicity, drugs or Lyme aren't corrected, the block persists or a device is implanted unnecessarily.

🎭 Morbidity & Mortality Conference — "Chasing the Node While the Candle Went Out"

The case: A 68-year-old man arrived with an anterior STEMI and new complete heart block, a wide escape at 30, BP 78/44. The team gave atropine 0.5 mg; nothing happened. So they gave it again. And again. And again — four doses over fifteen minutes — while the transvenous pacing wire sat unopened and the pacing pads, though on, were never switched to PACER mode. A colleague flagged a STAT potassium of 6.4 that was never actioned. The escape paused, then stopped. He arrested in asystole; the resuscitation failed. The pre-arrest strip, reviewed at the M&M, showed textbook AV dissociation with a wide escape — a rhythm that was never going to answer atropine.

🧀 The Swiss-cheese holes that lined up:

🧠 WRONG TARGETAtropine was repeated into a wide infranodal escape that lives below the node.
🔌 PACING DELAYEDPads were on but never paced; the transvenous wire stayed in its packet.
🧪 HYPERKALEMIA MISSEDA potassium of 6.4 was flagged and never treated.
🫀 ANTERIOR-MI SETTINGAnterior-MI block is infranodal and ominous — it screams "pace," not "atropine."
🌙 SYSTEMNo senior review, no "wide escape = pace" default, no closed-loop on the abnormal lab.

🛡️ What would have saved him: recognize that a wide escape is infranodal and will not answer atropine; give atropine once and pace immediately (transcutaneous → transvenous); action the hyperkalemia; treat the STEMI; get senior/EP eyes early. This is exactly why the crew with Mrs. E.M. put the pads on early and did not chase the node.

⚔️ ONE-LINER RULE"'Just one more dose of atropine' is the sentence written on the chart of patients who then arrest in asystole. A wide escape needs a wire, not a syringe."

🔀 Differentials

🧩 The bradycardia / AV-dissociation differential

DiagnosisCluesWhy it matters
🔀 Complete (third-degree) AV block (this patient)Independent regular P and R, atrial > ventricular rate, no fixed PR, wide slow escape, Stokes-Adams syncope, cannon A wavesDefault for AV dissociation with a slow escape; unstable + wide escape → pace. Hunt reversible causes.
📈 Mobitz I (Wenckebach)Progressive PR lengthening then a single dropped beat; often AV-nodal/benignUsually just observe if asymptomatic — do NOT confuse with dangerous block.
📉 Mobitz IIConstant PR then sudden dropped QRS; infranodal/dangerousHigh risk of progression to complete block → permanent pacemaker.
🐌 Sinus bradycardia / sinus node diseaseSlow but each P conducts (fixed PR), 1:1 AV relationship; sick sinus may pauseP waves DO conduct — not dissociation. Different management; atropine more likely to help.
🌀 Junctional/ventricular rhythm with isorhythmic AV dissociationVentricular rate ≥ atrial rate; dissociation without true block"AV dissociation" is not the same as complete block; here the ventricle is faster, not blocked.
🧪 Reversible mimics: hyperkalemia, drug toxicity, ischemia, Lyme, hypothyroidismHigh K⁺ (wide QRS, peaked T), digoxin/BB/CCB excess, inferior/anterior MI, EM rash, profound hypothyroidismSpecific reversible causes — correct them and conduction may return, avoiding a permanent device.

💎 Clinical Pearls

🔋 The escape is everythingIn complete block the patient is alive only because of an escape rhythm — and a wide, slow escape is a candle in the wind.
🔌 Wide escape = paceAtropine is a message to the AV node; an infranodal (wide-complex) escape won't read it — get the pacing pads on instead.
📟 Mobitz II & III = pacemakerMobitz II and third-degree block earn a pacemaker; Wenckebach usually just earns observation.
🫀 Anterior vs inferior MINew complete block in ANTERIOR MI is a catastrophe (big infarct, infranodal); in INFERIOR MI it's often vagal and transient.
🔀 Diagnose at the neckCannon A waves + variable S1 + slow regular pulse with marching P waves = AV dissociation = complete block.
🧪 Empty the reversible pocketsStop AV-nodal drugs, check potassium, digoxin, Lyme and ischemia before committing to a permanent device.
🐌 Narrow vs wide escapeNarrow junctional escape (40–60) is relatively reliable and atropine may help; wide ventricular escape (20–40) is unreliable — pace.
🫥 Stokes-Adams is a warningSudden blackout with no aura and quick recovery from a fragile escape — the next pause may not restart.
📏 2nd vs 3rd degreeIn second-degree block SOME P waves conduct; in third-degree NONE do. Look for a fixed PR — its absence with atrial > ventricular = complete block.
🫀 Capture is felt, not seenConfirm mechanical capture — a real pulse matching the pacer — not just a spike on the monitor.
⚡ Don't repeat atropineRepeated atropine for a wide escape while delaying pacing is the classic fatal error — give it once and pace.
🩻 Block is a symptomAlways ask "why now?" — drugs, hyperkalemia, ischemia, Lyme, fibrosis. Pace the patient and treat the cause.
