🧭 The immediately lethal causes of sudden dyspnoea + shock

Your reflex differential for "sudden breathlessness + collapse + shock" is the obstructive/vascular killers: massive PE, tension pneumothorax, cardiac tamponade, and acute MI with cardiogenic shock (± anaphylaxis, aortic catastrophe). One focused exam, a bedside ultrasound and a 12-lead triage all of them in minutes (see S14). Here, clear lungs + distended neck veins + a swollen leg screams PE.

⚠️ The trap of "she's just anxious / hyperventilating"Anxiety and hyperventilation do NOT cause sustained hypoxia (SpO₂ 86%), hypotension, a raised JVP, or a unilateral swollen leg. Labelling a crashing PE as "panic attack" is one of the most lethal cognitive errors in emergency medicine — the fix is to let the vital signs and the leg lead you, not the affect.

👨‍⚕️ Resident Review

The EM resident thinks out loud at 14:09:

🧠 Frame it as "is this HIGH-RISK PE?" — a haemodynamic question

"Everything says PE — sudden dyspnoea, syncope, hypoxia, a swollen leg, oestrogen and immobility. But the decision that matters right now isn't 'PE yes/no,' it's 'is she high-risk?' She's hypotensive with signs of RV strain — that's massive (high-risk) PE, which changes the algorithm from 'anticoagulate and image' to 'reperfuse.' I want a bedside echo now, heparin started, and the thrombolysis conversation started in parallel — I do NOT want to send a shocked patient down a corridor to CT alone."

🎯 Her order of operations

  1. 🫀 Bedside echo (POCUS) now — dilated hypokinetic RV, McConnell sign, septal flattening confirm the diagnosis without leaving Resus.
  2. 💉 Therapeutic anticoagulation immediately — IV unfractionated heparin (titratable, reversible, bridges to reperfusion).
  3. 💧 CAUTIOUS fluids — a single small bolus (≤ 250–500 mL) if pre-load-responsive; then STOP — over-filling distends and worsens the failing RV.
  4. 💊 Vasopressor (noradrenaline) for shock to support the coronary perfusion of the RV.
  5. 🩻 CTPA only if she can be stabilised; if peri-arrest, the bedside echo IS the decision-making study — do not delay lysis for the scanner.
⚔️ ONE-LINER RULE"In a crashing patient, don't send the RV to CT to die in the doughnut. A dilated RV on bedside echo in shock is enough — reperfuse, don't wait for the perfect picture."
🧪 Resident's self-testThe BP is 84/56 — she reaches for a litre of fluid wide open "to fill her up." Right move?

No — this is the classic trap. The failing, dilated RV is already over-stretched; flooding it pushes it further up (down) its Starling curve, bows the septum further into the LV, and drops cardiac output more. Give a small bolus (≤ 250–500 mL) only if there is pre-load responsiveness, then move quickly to vasopressors and reperfusion. "Don't drown the right heart."

🧠 Teaching Points + Interactive Simulation

🔬 The mechanism — WHY the shock, WHY the spiral (the WHY behind the WHY)

  1. 🧱 Obstruction: thrombus (usually from a leg DVT) lodges in the pulmonary arteries, mechanically blocking flow and triggering hypoxic vasoconstriction and mediator release.
  2. 📈 Pulmonary vascular resistance soars: the cross-sectional area of the pulmonary bed falls → acute rise in PVR → the thin-walled RV must suddenly generate high pressure it was never built for → RV pressure overload.
  3. 🎈 RV dilatation → ventricular interdependence: the overloaded RV dilates and the interventricular septum bows into the LV. The shared pericardial space means a big RV squashes the LV → impaired LV filling → falling stroke volume and BP.
  4. 💀 The death spiral: hypotension ↓ coronary perfusion to a wall that is now doing more work → RV ischaemia → the RV fails further → output falls further → PEA arrest. Each step feeds the next.
⚔️ ONE-LINER RULE"Massive PE kills through the RIGHT heart: obstruction → RV overload → septal bowing → the LV can't fill → shock → RV ischaemia → arrest. Break the spiral by unloading the RV (reperfusion) and perfusing it (pressors)."

⚖️ Contrast pair — Massive vs Submassive PE

🔴 Massive (high-risk) PE

  • Sustained hypotension (SBP < 90) / pressor need / arrest
  • RV strain present; obstructive shock
  • Reperfuse: systemic thrombolysis (± catheter/surgical) + heparin
  • Our patient

🟠 Submassive (intermediate) PE

  • Normotensive but RV dysfunction &/or raised troponin/BNP
  • At risk of deteriorating
  • Anticoagulate + monitor closely; consider lysis only if they decompensate (PEITHO caution)

💡 The dividing line is the blood pressure, not the CT clot burden — a modest-looking clot in a poor-reserve patient can be "massive," and a big clot in a compensated patient can be "submassive."

📋 Orders

🖊️ The order set (14:08)

  • 🫀 Bedside echo (POCUS) now — look for dilated hypokinetic RV, McConnell sign, septal flattening, RV:LV > 1; screen for tamponade/pneumothorax.
  • 💉 IV unfractionated heparin (bolus + infusion) immediately unless contraindicated — titratable and reversible if lysis follows.
  • 🩸 hs-troponin, BNP/NT-proBNP, lactate, CBC, U&E, coag (baseline pre-lysis), group & save, ABG.
  • 💧 Cautious fluid: ≤ 250–500 mL crystalloid ONCE if pre-load responsive — then reassess; avoid over-filling the RV.
  • 💊 Noradrenaline infusion for shock (SBP target ≈ ≥ 90 / MAP ≥ 65); consider dobutamine for RV inotropy if low-output persists.
  • 🫁 High-flow oxygen — she is hypoxic (SpO₂ 86%); target ≥ 94%.
  • 🩻 CTPA if she can be stabilised; if peri-arrest, treat on bedside echo and DO NOT delay reperfusion for the scanner.
  • 📞 Prepare systemic thrombolysis (alteplase) and activate the PE-response team; screen contraindications now.
⚔️ ONE-LINER RULE"Heparin buys time, pressors buy pressure, but only REPERFUSION removes the obstruction. In massive PE, start all three lines of thinking at once — anticoagulate, support, and reperfuse."

👩‍⚕️ Nursing Care

🛏️ Resus-bay priorities — the nurse runs the safety net

  • 📟 Continuous telemetry + defib pads on — massive PE can drop into PEA arrest without warning; be ready.
  • 🩸 Arterial line / frequent NIBP — titrate noradrenaline to the pressure; flag any fall in BP or rise in HR immediately.
  • 💧 Guard the fluids — question any "wide-open litre"; the failing RV is over-filled already. Small boluses only, on the doctor's call.
  • 💉 Draw the pre-lysis bloods and coag NOW and check the thrombolysis contraindication checklist with the team — every minute saved matters.
  • 🧾 Keep the patient calm and flat-ish but not fully supine if it worsens breathlessness; explain each step — terror drives tachycardia and oxygen demand.
  • 🚑 Ready the transfer/cath-lab bag with resus drugs if catheter-directed therapy or embolectomy is chosen; never move a crashing PE unescorted.
⚔️ ONE-LINER RULE"In massive PE the nurse's most important reflex is the hand that STOPS the fluid and reaches for the pressor — you cannot fill your way out of a failing right ventricle."

📈 ECG / Imaging + Visual Enhancements

🫀 The ECG of acute RV strain — read it as a stressed right heart

The commonest ECG in PE is sinus tachycardia. The classic (but insensitive) S1Q3T3 and anterior T-wave inversion (V1–V4) signal acute RIGHT-heart strain — supportive, never required to act.

🩻 Adjunct imaging

  • CXR: often normal or non-specific; may show Hampton's hump or Westermark's sign — mostly it helps EXCLUDE mimics (pneumothorax, consolidation) that explain a clear/abnormal chest.
  • Lower-limb venous Doppler: a confirmed proximal DVT in the swollen leg supports treating for PE when CT is not feasible.

S12

📟 Monitoring

📡 What to watch and why

  • 📈 Continuous telemetry + arterial line — massive PE can arrest suddenly; beat-to-beat BP guides pressor titration.
  • 🫁 SpO₂ and respiratory rate — worsening hypoxia or a rising RR signals decompensation.
  • 🩸 Serial lactate — a falling lactate after reperfusion/pressors means the shock is resolving; a rising one means the RV is losing.
  • 🫀 Repeat bedside echo — track RV size/function and septal position; improving RV:LV after lysis is the win.
  • 🩹 Post-lysis bleeding watch — neuro obs for intracranial haemorrhage, puncture sites, Hb — the price of reperfusion is bleeding risk.
⚔️ ONE-LINER RULE"After you commit to reperfusion, the monitor's question changes from 'is the RV failing?' to 'is it recovering — and is she bleeding?' The answers are the lactate, the echo, and the neuro obs."
S13

⚠️ Complications

💀 What can go wrong in the ED phase

  • ⚡ PEA / cardiac arrest — the RV loses; consider empirical thrombolysis during arrest if PE is the presumed cause, and prolonged CPR (50–60+ min) after lysis.
  • 💔 Refractory obstructive shock — despite heparin/pressors; escalate to lysis, catheter-directed therapy, embolectomy, or ECMO.
  • 🩸 Thrombolysis haemorrhage — intracranial (the feared one), GI, retroperitoneal; the main downside seen in PEITHO.
  • 💧 Iatrogenic RV overfilling — the "wide-open litre" that tips a compensating RV into shock.
  • 🫁 Recurrent embolism — further clot from an untreated leg DVT; anticoagulate, consider IVC filter only if anticoagulation is truly contraindicated.
  • 🩹 The system complication: the missed diagnosis — labelling it "anxiety," "pneumonia," or "reflux" and losing the reperfusion window.
⚔️ ONE-LINER RULE"The two deadliest complications of massive PE are opposite mistakes: doing too little (missing it / fluids only) and doing lysis blind to a contraindication. Both are avoided by a fast, deliberate reperfusion decision."

🗺️ Plan + Procedure Guides with Checklists

🧭 The management plan

  1. 🫁 Diagnosis: high-risk (massive) PE — hypotension + RV strain on bedside echo, obvious DVT and provoking factors. Confirmed on CTPA once briefly stabilised.
  2. 💉 Reperfuse + anticoagulate: IV heparin immediately; systemic thrombolysis (alteplase) as no contraindication → escalate to catheter-directed / embolectomy if lysis fails or is contraindicated.
  3. 💊 Support: cautious fluid (≤ 250–500 mL once), noradrenaline for shock, high-flow O₂; PE-response team activated.
  4. 🏥 Admit to ICU/HDU for post-lysis monitoring; then long-term anticoagulation and a thrombophilia / provoking-factor review (stop the pill, treat the DVT).
⚔️ ONE-LINER RULE"Massive PE is not 'a big DVT' — it is obstructive shock with a treatable obstruction. The plan is reperfuse, support, anticoagulate, and hunt the cause — in that urgency, not that leisure."

🏠 Discharge

📋 (Post-recovery) discharge & the genuinely low-risk PE — two different worlds

🫁 This patient (survived massive PE)

  • Therapeutic anticoagulation ≥ 3 months (often longer / indefinite if unprovoked or thrombophilia)
  • Stop the oestrogen pill; thrombophilia + provoking-factor review; treat the DVT
  • Red flags: recurrent breathlessness, chest pain, syncope, leg swelling → 999
  • Bleeding precautions post-lysis; haematology / follow-up echo for CTEPH surveillance

🟢 The genuinely low-risk PE

  • sPESI = 0, normotensive, no RV strain, normal biomarkers
  • Consider early/outpatient management on a DOAC with shared decision-making
  • Written safety-net + rapid anticoagulation clinic follow-up
⚔️ ONE-LINER RULE"Not every PE needs a hospital bed, and not every PE is 'small' — the disposition follows the RISK (BP, RV, sPESI), never the word 'clot.' Match the intensity of care to the physiology."

🧠 Beyond the pathway

  • 🩺 Chronic thromboembolic pulmonary hypertension (CTEPH): a minority of PE survivors develop organised, non-resolving clot → progressive pulmonary hypertension. Screen persistent breathlessness with echo/V-Q; potentially curable by pulmonary endarterectomy, or treated with balloon pulmonary angioplasty / riociguat.
  • 🧵 Catheter-directed therapy (CDT) & EKOS: catheter-directed (± ultrasound-assisted, EKOS) thrombolysis delivers a lower lytic dose locally — an option in intermediate-high or massive PE when systemic lysis is risky or has failed; also mechanical/aspiration thrombectomy devices.
  • 🫀 ECMO (VA): veno-arterial ECMO as a bridge in refractory obstructive shock or peri-arrest massive PE — buys time for lysis to work or for surgical embolectomy.
  • 🧬 Thrombophilia work-up: factor V Leiden, prothrombin G20210A, protein C/S and antithrombin deficiency, antiphospholipid syndrome; timing matters (test off anticoagulation/acute phase). Guides duration of anticoagulation and family counselling.
  • ⚖️ Sub-massive risk stratification: the intermediate group is split by troponin, BNP/NT-proBNP and RV dysfunction (echo or CT RV:LV > 1.0). "Intermediate-HIGH" (positive biomarker AND RV dysfunction) is the group to watch closely and rescue-lyse if they tip.
  • 🤰 Special populations: pregnancy-adapted diagnostic pathways (YEARS, modified Wells, bilateral leg Dopplers first), and massive PE in pregnancy where lysis is relatively — not absolutely — contraindicated when life-threatening.

🎭 M&M — "The Panic Attack That Wasn't"

The case: A 28-year-old woman on the combined pill presented to a busy ED with sudden breathlessness and a feeling of impending doom two days after a long flight. She was tachypnoeic and tachycardic; her SpO₂ read 89% but was charted as "?probe artefact." She was labelled "panic attack / hyperventilation," given reassurance and a paper bag, and left on a corridor trolley to "settle." A junior briefly wondered about her swollen calf. No bedside echo was performed and a CTPA was booked as "routine." Forty minutes later she was found in PEA arrest; post-mortem showed a saddle pulmonary embolus. A retrospective bedside echo clip taken during the arrest showed a grossly dilated RV with McConnell's sign — the diagnosis had been one probe-touch away the whole time.

🧀 The Swiss-cheese holes that lined up:

🧠 ANCHORING"Panic attack" label stuck; a young woman's distress was read as psychological, not physiological.
🫁 HYPOXIA DISMISSEDSpO₂ 89% explained away as "probe artefact" instead of believed.
🦵 CLUE IGNOREDUnilateral swollen calf + pill + flight never assembled into a VTE story.
🩻 CT OVER ECHOA "routine" CTPA was awaited while a 30-second bedside echo would have shown the failing RV.
🏥 SYSTEMOvercrowding → corridor care → unmonitored, undressed, no defib to hand.

🛡️ What would have saved her: believe the sats; treat sudden dyspnoea + syncope + a swollen leg as PE, not panic; put a probe on the chest in Resus rather than waiting for CT; keep a hypotensive/hypoxic patient monitored with defib to hand; and reperfuse a shocked right heart without delay. This is exactly the discipline that saved the patient in Bed 3.

⚔️ ONE-LINER RULE"'Panic attack' has killed more young PEs than any clot ever will. Hypoxia, syncope and a swollen leg are physiology, not psychology — put the probe on and look at the right heart."
