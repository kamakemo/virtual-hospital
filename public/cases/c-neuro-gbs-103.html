📜 History — the antecedent illness is the whole story

  • 🦠 Diarrhoeal illness ~2 weeks ago (bloody, self-limiting) — the classic Campylobacter jejuni trigger. Two-thirds of GBS follows an infection by 1–3 weeks.
  • 🦶 Ascending pattern: paraesthesiae then weakness starting distally in the legs and climbing — symmetrical, over days (not minutes, not months).
  • 🔇 Areflexia: reflexes lost early and out of proportion to weakness — a red-flag examination finding.
  • 🗣️ New bulbar hints (quiet/nasal voice) and autonomic lability (tachycardia, hypertension) — both mark a patient who can deteriorate fast.
⚔️ ONE-LINER RULE"Symmetrical ascending weakness with AREFLEXIA days after an infection is Guillain-Barré until proven otherwise — and the danger is not the legs, it is the diaphragm."
🔥 Why acute neurology is the highest-risk place to under-call thisGBS looks unimpressive at first — the patient talks, sats look fine, ABG is normal. Yet up to 20–30% need mechanical ventilation, and respiratory failure can arrive within hours. The whole game is serial forced vital capacity and a low threshold to move to a monitored bed — not a reassuring glance.

🧭 The bedside tests that cost nothing

Single-breath count (count aloud on one breath — <15–20 suggests reduced vital capacity), FVC on a handheld spirometer, and watching for paradoxical abdominal breathing (the diaphragm giving way). A patient who cannot lift the head off the pillow has weak neck flexors — the neck flexors and the diaphragm fail together.

⚠️ The trap of "the sats are fine"Neuromuscular respiratory failure is a ventilation (pump) failure, not an oxygenation failure. SpO₂ and PaO₂ stay normal until the patient is nearly exhausted; a rising PaCO₂ is a pre-terminal sign. Do NOT wait for desaturation or a bad gas — act on the falling FVC.

🎯 Her order of operations

  1. 🫁 Serial FVC + single-breath count — now, then 4-hourly (more often if trending down); apply the 20/30/40 rule (S6).
  2. 🛏️ Monitored / HDU bed, cardiac monitor for autonomic arrhythmia, ICU + anaesthetics pre-alerted.
  3. 🧪 LP (albuminocytological dissociation) and nerve conduction studies to confirm demyelination — but treatment does not wait for them.
  4. 💉 Start IVIG 0.4 g/kg/day × 5 days OR plasma exchange once GBS is the working diagnosis and disability is significant/progressing.
  5. 🩸 VTE prophylaxis (immobile patient), autonomic monitoring, swallow assessment, pain relief.
⚔️ ONE-LINER RULE"Treat the breathing before you finish the diagnosis. IVIG or plasma exchange, a monitored bed and a serial FVC come BEFORE the LP result — the diaphragm does not wait for the lab."
🧪 Resident's self-testHe asks whether a course of high-dose IV steroids would speed recovery, "as in an MS relapse." Give them?

No. Unlike CNS inflammatory disease (MS relapse, where steroids help), corticosteroids do NOT benefit GBS — randomised trials (and the Cochrane review) show no improvement, and oral steroids may even slow recovery. The proven treatments are IVIG or plasma exchange; they are equally effective and are NOT combined.

🧠 Teaching Points + Interactive Simulation

🔬 The mechanism — WHY the nerves fail (the WHY behind the WHY)

  1. 🦠 The trigger: an infection — classically Campylobacter jejuni (also CMV, EBV, Mycoplasma, Zika, and post-vaccination rarely) — presents antigens to the immune system.
  2. 🎭 Molecular mimicry: microbial surface sugars (lipo-oligosaccharides) resemble gangliosides on peripheral nerve. Antibodies raised against the bug cross-react with the patient's own nerve.
  3. ⚡ Autoimmune attack: antibody + complement strip myelin from peripheral nerves and roots (AIDP), or in axonal variants attack the axon at the nodes of Ranvier (AMAN). Demyelination slows or blocks conduction.
  4. 🦵 The ascending pattern: the longest nerves (to the feet) are affected first and recover last — hence weakness climbs from the feet upward, symmetrically, and reflexes (which need fast, intact conduction along the whole arc) are lost early → areflexia.
  5. 🫁 The life-threat: when demyelination reaches the phrenic nerves and bulbar muscles, the pump fails — ventilatory (not oxygenation) failure. Autonomic fibres are hit too → labile BP, arrhythmia.
⚔️ ONE-LINER RULE"GBS is your own antibodies mistaking your nerves for the bug that made you ill — a case of mistaken identity that climbs from the feet toward the diaphragm."

⚖️ Contrast pair — Demyelinating (AIDP) vs Axonal (AMAN)

🧬 AIDP (demyelinating)

  • Commonest form in Europe/North America
  • Antibody + complement attack MYELIN; conduction slowing/block on NCS
  • Often good recovery as myelin repairs

⚙️ AMAN (acute motor axonal)

  • Commoner in Asia; strongly linked to Campylobacter
  • Attack on the AXON at nodes; anti-GM1/GD1a antibodies
  • Can be more severe / slower recovery

💡 A raised anti-ganglioside antibody points to a variant, but the diagnosis is clinical — do not wait for antibody results to treat.

📋 Orders

🖊️ The order set (14:35)

  • 🫁 Bedside FVC + single-breath count now, then 4-hourly (hourly if trending down or symptomatic); document each value and the trend.
  • 🛏️ Admit to HDU/ICU-monitored bed; continuous cardiac monitoring for autonomic arrhythmia; ICU + anaesthetics alerted.
  • 🧪 Lumbar puncture (CSF protein, cells); nerve conduction studies; bloods incl. FBC, U&E, LFT, glucose, HbA1c, B12, TFT, HIV, anti-ganglioside antibodies (send, do not wait).
  • 💉 Start IVIG 0.4 g/kg/day × 5 days (total 2 g/kg) or plasma exchange (~5 exchanges over ~2 weeks) — one, not both.
  • 🩸 VTE prophylaxis (LMWH + mechanical) — this is an immobile patient at high clot risk.
  • 🍽️ Swallow assessment before oral intake (bulbar weakness → aspiration risk); analgesia for neuropathic pain (often severe).
  • 🚫 No corticosteroids for the GBS itself; avoid drugs that worsen autonomic instability.
  • 📞 If FVC approaches 20 mL/kg / <1 L, bulbar failure, or fatigue → elective controlled intubation, do not wait for an ABG.
⚔️ ONE-LINER RULE"A handheld spirometer and a monitored bed cost almost nothing; the expensive mistake is the FVC you never measured because 'he was still talking.'"
S8

👩‍⚕️ Nursing Care

🛏️ Monitored-bed priorities — the nurse runs the safety net

  • 🫁 Measure and chart the FVC every 4 hours (or as prescribed) — the nurse who spots the downward trend catches respiratory failure before it happens. Escalate on any fall.
  • 📟 Continuous cardiac monitoring — autonomic instability causes tachy/brady-arrhythmia, asystole on suctioning, and wild BP swings; treat swings cautiously (they are often transient).
  • 🗣️ Guard the airway: assess cough and swallow, keep suction to hand, keep nil-by-mouth until swallow cleared — a weak cough cannot protect the lungs.
  • 🩸 VTE prophylaxis checked and given; reposition to prevent pressure sores in a paralysed patient; eye and mouth care if facial weakness.
  • 😖 Neuropathic pain is common and under-treated — score it and escalate analgesia; the patient may be terrified and unable to move but fully aware.
  • 🧾 Keep the patient oriented and reassured — GBS patients are alert and frightened; communication is care.
⚔️ ONE-LINER RULE"In GBS the nurse who re-checks the FVC and the swallow on every shift is the most sensitive ventilator-predictor in the hospital."

🩻 The Miller Fisher variant — recognise the triad

  • Ophthalmoplegia (eye-movement paralysis), ataxia, and areflexia — with anti-GQ1b antibodies.
  • Often descending/cranial rather than ascending; overlaps with Bickerstaff brainstem encephalitis (add drowsiness/upgoing plantars).

S12

📟 Monitoring

📡 What to watch and why

  • 🫁 Serial FVC + single-breath count (the master signal) — 4-hourly, hourly if falling; act on the trend toward 20 mL/kg / <1 L, not on the sats.
  • 📟 Continuous cardiac monitor — autonomic dysfunction causes arrhythmia (including asystole on suctioning), labile BP; treat sustained, not transient, changes.
  • 🗣️ Bulbar function — cough, swallow, secretions; NBM and suction ready; aspiration is a real killer.
  • 🩸 BP each 1–4 h; watch sodium (SIADH); daily VTE-risk review in the immobile patient.
  • 📈 Progression watch — GBS worsens for up to ~2–4 weeks before the plateau; a patient improving one day can still deteriorate the next.
⚔️ ONE-LINER RULE"After you say 'GBS,' the monitor's question changes from 'is he weak?' to 'is the diaphragm about to fail and is the heart about to swing?' — and the answers are the FVC trend and the rhythm strip."
S13

⚠️ Complications

💀 What can go wrong

  • 🫁 Respiratory failure — the commonest killer; 20–30% need ventilation. Ventilatory (pump) failure, warned by FVC not by sats.
  • 💓 Autonomic instability — arrhythmia (including fatal asystole/brady on suction), labile BP, ileus, urinary retention. A leading cause of death.
  • 🗣️ Aspiration from bulbar weakness — a weak cough cannot clear secretions.
  • 🩸 VTE / PE — immobility; and pressure sores, contractures.
  • 😖 Severe neuropathic pain and, in ventilated patients, prolonged ICU stay, ICU-acquired weakness, and psychological distress (fully alert, "locked in").
  • 🚑 The system complication: under-triage — the "mild GBS" on an unmonitored ward whose FVC was never measured.
⚔️ ONE-LINER RULE"GBS kills two ways: the diaphragm that fails and the heart that swings. Watch the FVC AND the rhythm — a patient can arrest from an autonomic asystole while breathing perfectly."

🧭 The disposition plan

  1. 🧠 Diagnosis: GBS (AIDP) — ascending areflexic weakness, CSF dissociation, demyelinating NCS, Campylobacter trigger. High risk for ventilation.

🏠 Discharge

📋 Recovery & rehab — a long road, mostly forward

🧠 This patient (severe GBS)

  • Multidisciplinary neuro-rehab: physio, OT, SALT; graded return of function over months
  • Most improve substantially; ~80% walk independently by a year, but fatigue/pain/residual deficit are common
  • Watch for recurrence/treatment-related fluctuation; a minority evolve to CIDP if relapsing beyond 8 weeks
  • VTE, pressure, mood and pain follow-up

🟢 Milder GBS discharge

  • Only after a clear plateau and demonstrably stable/improving FVC and swallow
  • Written safety-net: return immediately for new breathlessness, swallowing trouble, or fresh weakness
  • Neurology follow-up; driving and work advice per recovery
⚔️ ONE-LINER RULE"Never discharge a GBS patient on the UP-slope of their disease — send them home only after a documented plateau with a stable FVC and a safe swallow, plus a written 'come back if you cannot breathe' net."

🔬 Advanced Topics — Fellowship Level

🧠 Beyond the bedside

  • 🧬 The variant spectrum: AIDP, AMAN, AMSAN, Miller Fisher (anti-GQ1b), pharyngeal-cervical-brachial, paraparetic and pure-sensory forms, and Bickerstaff brainstem encephalitis — a single autoimmune process with many faces.
  • 📊 Prognostic scores: EGRIS (Erasmus GBS Respiratory Insufficiency Score) predicts mechanical ventilation; mEGOS predicts walking outcome — tools to escalate proactively rather than reactively.
  • 🔁 Treatment-related fluctuation vs CIDP: a secondary deterioration after initial improvement may need a repeat course; deterioration beyond ~8 weeks or ≥3 relapses suggests acute-onset CIDP (which, unlike GBS, DOES respond to steroids).
  • 🧪 Second dose / second modality: routine second IVIG course in poor responders is not clearly beneficial (SID-GBS); switching or combining PLEX+IVIG is not supported — the PSGBS trial showed no additive benefit.
  • 💉 Emerging therapy: complement inhibition (eculizumab) is under study for severe GBS to limit the antibody-complement attack on nerve.
  • 🦠 Public-health links: Campylobacter and, in outbreaks, Zika virus; understanding molecular mimicry underpins vaccine-safety surveillance (GBS is a monitored, very rare adverse event of some vaccines).

🎭 Morbidity & Mortality Conference

🎭 M&M — "The FVC Nobody Measured"

The case: A 29-year-old woman presented with two days of ascending numbness and mild leg weakness after a diarrhoeal illness. She was alert, chatty, sats 97%, and "looked well." Diagnosed with "probable mild GBS," she was admitted to a busy general medical ward for "observation." Her reflexes were absent and her voice had softened, but no forced vital capacity was ever measured and no monitored bed was arranged. Overnight she grew quiet and breathed shallowly; the observation chart recorded "sleeping." At the 06:00 round she was found in respiratory arrest — a silent neuromuscular ventilatory failure. Resuscitation achieved return of circulation but she suffered a hypoxic brain injury.

🧀 The Swiss-cheese holes that lined up:

🧠 FALSE REASSURANCE"Still talking, sats 97%" treated as safe — but sats fall LATE in pump failure.
🫁 FVC NEVER MEASUREDThe one early-warning number was never obtained or trended.
🛏️ WRONG BEDUnmonitored general ward instead of HDU/ICU for a progressing GBS.
🔁 NO SERIAL EXAMAreflexia + softening voice = bulbar/respiratory risk, not observed for.
😴 'SLEEPING' TRAPHypoventilation and rising CO₂ misread as restful sleep overnight.

🛡️ What would have saved her: measure a bedside FVC in EVERY suspected GBS and repeat it 4-hourly; treat areflexia + bulbar signs + rapid progression as high-risk and take a monitored bed; never trust a normal SpO₂ or ABG to exclude neuromuscular respiratory failure; and intubate electively on the falling FVC trend. This is exactly the discipline that saved the patient in Bed 3.

⚔️ ONE-LINER RULE"'He was still talking' has killed more GBS patients than the disease itself. The FVC you did not measure is the arrest you did not prevent."
