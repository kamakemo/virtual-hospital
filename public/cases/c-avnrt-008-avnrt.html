🧬 Why THIS patient? — dual AV-nodal physiology

AVNRT exists because of a quirk almost everyone is born with but few notice: the AV node has two functional inputs — a fast pathway (conducts quickly, but with a long refractory period) and a slow pathway (conducts slowly, but recovers quickly). In normal rhythm, an impulse races down the fast pathway and you never know the slow one exists. The setup for re-entry: a well-timed premature atrial beat arrives while the fast pathway is still refractory, so it is forced down the slow pathway. By the time it reaches the bottom, the fast pathway has recovered — and the impulse turns around and conducts retrogradely up the fast pathway, back to the top, then down the slow pathway again. A self-sustaining loop is born, spinning entirely within and around the AV node at ~188 laps a minute. The substrate (dual pathways) has been there her whole life. Tonight a single ectopic beat — caffeine, stress — found the perfect timing and lit it.

🔥 Triggers / risk factors for AVNRT ☕ Caffeine · 🍷 Alcohol · 😰 Emotional stress & catecholamine surges · 🏃 Exercise (especially the recovery phase) · 🤰 Pregnancy · 💊 Stimulants/sympathomimetics · 🚺 Young, female predominance (≈ 2:1) · 🧬 A well-timed premature atrial beat — the universal spark.

🧭 The single most important question: STABLE or UNSTABLE?

Every tachyarrhythmia forks on one decision. Look for the adverse features (the "4 S's"): Shock (SBP < 90, poor perfusion), Syncope/presyncope, Severe ischemia (chest pain, ST changes), Severe heart failure (pulmonary edema). Ms. A.K. has mild presyncope and a borderline pressure but is conscious, perfusing and STABLE-leaning. That means we climb the chemistry/vagal ladder — but we keep cardioversion in reserve and re-check her constantly, because borderline patients can tip.

⚡ The decision: STABLE-leaning → VAGAL FIRST, then ADENOSINEA stable regular narrow-complex SVT gets the gentle ladder: (1) a properly performed modified Valsalva, (2) IV adenosine, (3) a rate-controlling drug if needed. Electricity (synchronized cardioversion) is held in reserve for the patient who becomes unstable. Don't reach for the defibrillator on a chatting 24-year-old — but never be more than one deterioration away from it.

👨‍⚕️ Resident Review

The cardiology resident arrives at 14:34. Her reasoning, out loud:

🧠 Pattern recognition first, then physiology

"Narrow. Regular. Fast — 188. Young, structurally normal heart, recurrent paroxysmal episodes she breaks by bearing down. That is AVNRT — the commonest regular paroxysmal SVT. The narrow QRS tells me the ventricles are still being activated through the normal His-Purkinje system, so the circuit is above or within the AV node. This is the mirror image of our VT patient down the hall: there, scar + wide + regular = a ventricular circuit you shock. Here, normal heart + narrow + regular = a nodal circuit you can interrupt with a vagal maneuver or a drug that briefly blocks the AV node."

🎯 Immediate priorities (her actual order of operations)

  1. 💨 Modified Valsalva, done properly: 15 seconds of forced exhalation against resistance (blow into a 10 mL syringe) while semi-recumbent, then immediately lie flat and have the legs raised to 45° for 15 s (the REVERT maneuver). This boosts venous return and the reflex vagal surge — roughly tripling conversion vs a standard Valsalva.
  2. 💉 If vagal fails → adenosine 6 mg rapid IV push + immediate saline flush, via a large proximal (antecubital) cannula, with continuous ECG running and the print button ready. Then 12 mg, then 12 mg if needed.
  3. 🗣️ Warn the patient before the push: "You'll feel a few seconds of chest tightness, flushing, breathlessness, maybe a sense of impending doom — it passes in seconds. That's the medicine working." Forewarned is half-treated.
  4. 🛡️ Have defib pads on and the resuscitation trolley ready — not because adenosine usually causes trouble, but because the whole point of the drug is a transient complete AV block, and you respect that.
  5. 🧯 Check the contraindications out loud: asthma/COPD (bronchospasm), recent heart transplant and dipyridamole (potentiate — reduce dose), and confirm the rhythm is regular and not pre-excited AF before any AV-nodal blocker.
  6. 📋 Capture the conversion on ECG — the moment adenosine blocks the node is diagnostic gold (reveals flutter waves, an atrial tachycardia, or the abrupt break of re-entry).
⚔️ ONE-LINER RULE"Vagal first, adenosine second, cardioversion if unstable — and a rate-controlling drug only once you're certain it's a regular SVT and not pre-excited AF."

🤔 "Why not just give verapamil first — it works for SVT?"

"Verapamil does terminate AVNRT, but it's second-line behind vagal and adenosine for a reason: it's longer-acting, it drops the blood pressure, and — crucially — if I'm even slightly wrong and this is actually pre-excited AF (an irregular rhythm masquerading), an AV-nodal blocker can shunt conduction down the accessory pathway and precipitate VF. Adenosine's half-life is ~10 seconds, so its block is over almost before it begins; verapamil's effect lingers. Start with the reversible, then the short-acting, then the long-acting — and confirm regularity before any of the AV-nodal blockers."

🧪 Resident's self-test (answer before reading on) A junior wants to give adenosine through a small cannula in the back of the hand, slowly, "to be gentle." Good idea?

No — that's how adenosine fails. Its half-life is ~10 seconds; pushed slowly or through a distal vein, it's metabolized before it ever reaches the AV node and does nothing — leading teams to wrongly conclude "adenosine didn't work" and escalate. Give it fast, through a large proximal (antecubital) line, with an immediate saline flush chasing it (the two-syringe or three-way-tap technique). Speed and a flush are the whole trick.

🎓 Consultant Round

The on-call consultant electrophysiologist, by phone at 14:40, then at the bedside:

🗂️ Name the rhythm precisely — it changes the long-term plan

🔴 AVNRT (this patient)

  • Re-entry within/around the AV node using dual (slow & fast) pathways
  • P wave usually buried in the QRS or just after it (pseudo-r' in V1, pseudo-S in inferior leads)
  • Commonest regular paroxysmal SVT; young, often female, normal heart
  • Acute: vagal → adenosine. Definitive: slow-pathway ablation

🔵 AVRT (orthodromic)

  • Re-entry through the AV node down and an accessory pathway back up — the circuit needs the whole atrium & ventricle
  • P wave clearly after the QRS (longer RP); look for delta waves/WPW in sinus rhythm
  • Risk of pre-excited AF → never block the node blindly
  • Definitive: ablate the accessory pathway
⚔️ ONE-LINER RULE"AVNRT hides its P wave inside the QRS; AVRT shows its P wave after the QRS. Both stop when you block the AV node — but only AVRT carries an accessory pathway you must respect."

🔎 The RP-interval trick — how the consultant tells the three apart

"Look at where the retrograde P sits relative to the QRS — the RP interval. In typical (slow-fast) AVNRT the P is buried in or immediately after the QRS (very short RP — pseudo-r'/pseudo-S). In AVRT the P comes a little later (short RP, but distinct, after the QRS). In atrial tachycardia the P is far from the QRS (long RP) and the rhythm may persist with AV block when you give adenosine — the atria keep firing even though the node is blocked. That last point is the elegant diagnostic value of adenosine: it doesn't just treat, it unmasks."

🧭 Consultant's three commandments for the next ten minutes

  1. 💨 "Do the vagal maneuver properly — the REVERT (modified Valsalva with leg-raise) before anyone touches a syringe. It's free, it's safe, and it works in nearly half of cases when done right. A lazy breath-hold is not a treatment."
  2. 💉 "If you give adenosine, give it like you mean it: 6 mg fast through a big line with a flush, run the ECG strip, warn her first, pads on. If 6 fails, 12, then 12. Reduce the dose with dipyridamole or a transplanted heart; avoid it in real asthma."
  3. 🗺️ "Then think beyond tonight: this is her umpteenth episode. A slow-pathway catheter ablation is >95% curative with ~1% risk of needing a pacemaker. Offer it — it can take a recurrent, life-disrupting arrhythmia and make it disappear for good."
☠️ The consultant's war memory (more in S13 M&M)"The dangerous error with these isn't usually AVNRT itself — it's the lookalike. I watched a team push adenosine into an irregular wide tachycardia they'd called 'SVT.' It was pre-excited AF. The transient nodal block shunted everything down the accessory pathway, the ventricular rate exploded, and it degenerated into VF. Adenosine is a deliberate six-second cardiac arrest — only aim it at a regular rhythm you're sure of, with the defibrillator at your elbow."

📖 Teaching Points

🔬 The mechanism — WHY dual pathways make AVNRT (the WHY behind the WHY)

  1. 🛤️ The substrate: the AV node has two functional pathways. The fast pathway conducts quickly but has a long refractory period. The slow pathway conducts slowly but has a short refractory period. Most people have this and never know.
  2. ⏱️ The trigger: a premature atrial beat arrives at a critical moment — early enough that the fast pathway is still refractory (it blocks there), but the slow pathway has recovered and accepts it.
  3. 🔁 Re-entry is born: the impulse crawls down the slow pathway; by the time it reaches the bottom, the fast pathway has recovered, so it conducts retrogradely up the fast pathway to the top, then back down the slow pathway. A wavefront chasing its own tail in a tiny loop around the AV node. Each lap drives the atria and ventricles almost simultaneously → ~150–250/min.
  4. 👻 Why the P wave vanishes: because atria and ventricles are activated at nearly the same instant, the retrograde P wave is buried inside the QRS — or peeks out as a pseudo-r' in V1 and a pseudo-S in the inferior leads (II, III, aVF). Those tiny deflections are retrograde atrial activation, not part of the QRS.
  5. 💥 Why it's usually well-tolerated (but not always): a normal heart copes with 188 for a while, but short diastole means reduced filling, lower output, lightheadedness, and — if prolonged or in a borderline patient — hypotension and presyncope. Block the AV node for one beat and the loop has nowhere to go: it terminates instantly.
⚔️ ONE-LINER RULE"AVNRT is a wavefront trapped in a loop through the AV node. Anything that blocks the node for a single beat — a vagal surge or a slug of adenosine — collapses the loop and the tachycardia stops dead."

⚖️ Contrast pair #1 — AVNRT vs AVRT vs Atrial Tachycardia (the RP-interval family)

🌀 AVNRT (assume this first)

  • Re-entry within/around the AV node (dual pathways)
  • Very short RP — P buried in QRS / pseudo-r' (V1), pseudo-S (inferior)
  • Young, normal heart, sudden on/off
  • Terminates with vagal/adenosine; cured by slow-pathway ablation

🔵 AVRT & Atrial Tachycardia

  • AVRT: uses an accessory pathway — P clearly after QRS; hunt for WPW/delta waves; ablate the pathway
  • Atrial tachycardia: long RP, P far from QRS; continues despite AV block with adenosine (atria keep firing)
  • Adenosine is diagnostic and therapeutic — watch what happens at the moment of block

😈 The trap: all three are regular narrow tachycardias that can look alike at a glance. The discriminators are the RP interval, the presence of WPW on a resting ECG, and the response to adenosine — re-entry (AVNRT/AVRT) breaks abruptly; atrial tachycardia and flutter persist with transient block, unmasking their atrial activity.

⚖️ Contrast pair #2 — Narrow-complex vs Wide-complex tachycardia

🌀 Narrow-complex (this patient)

  • QRS < 120 ms — ventricles via normal His-Purkinje
  • Supraventricular origin: AVNRT, AVRT, atrial tach, flutter, AF
  • Regular + narrow → treat as SVT: vagal/adenosine
  • Generally well-tolerated; reversible block ends re-entry

⚡ Wide-complex

  • QRS ≥ 120 ms — abnormal ventricular activation
  • Treat a wide-AND-regular tachycardia as VT until proven otherwise, especially with structural heart disease
  • Never give AV-nodal blockers blindly to wide tachycardia
  • Unstable → synchronized cardioversion (see the VT case)

💡 Why it matters: the narrow QRS here is what makes the gentle SVT ladder safe. The instant a tachycardia is wide and regular, the default flips to "treat as VT," and AV-nodal blockers become dangerous. Width changes the whole algorithm.

🔬 Fellowship Corner — Advanced Topics

  • 🔁 Typical vs atypical AVNRT: "slow-fast" (typical, ~90%, very short RP, P buried) vs "fast-slow" or "slow-slow" (atypical, long RP, P well after QRS) — the atypical forms mimic atrial tachycardia and can be tricky to map.
  • 🗺️ Slow-pathway modification: ablation targets the slow-pathway region in the inferoseptal right atrium near the coronary sinus os; endpoint is loss of sustained AVNRT with at most a single AV-nodal echo. The risk to avoid is the compact node → complete heart block.
  • 🧬 The AVNRT–AVRT mechanistic divide: AVNRT is a small intranodal loop; AVRT requires a macro-reentrant circuit through an accessory pathway — which is why a resting ECG in sinus rhythm (looking for pre-excitation/delta waves) is mandatory before ablation.
  • 💊 The 'pill-in-the-pocket' strategy: for infrequent, well-tolerated episodes, a single oral dose of diltiazem + propranolol (or flecainide in selected patients with normal hearts) taken at onset can abort attacks without daily medication.
  • 🤰 AVNRT in pregnancy: vagal maneuvers first; adenosine is considered safe and is the drug of choice; metoprolol/verapamil as alternatives; cardioversion is safe if unstable. Flecainide/sotalol for prophylaxis if needed.
  • 🧪 The pseudo-r'/pseudo-S sign: compare the tachycardia ECG to the patient's sinus-rhythm ECG — a new small terminal deflection (r' in V1, S in inferior leads) that disappears after conversion is retrograde atrial activation and is fairly specific for typical AVNRT.

📋 Orders

🖊️ The order set, written as you'd actually write it (14:35)

  • 💨 Modified Valsalva (REVERT) FIRST — forced exhalation 15 s against resistance, then immediate supine position with passive leg-raise; repeat once if needed.
  • 💉 Adenosine 6 mg rapid IV push + immediate 20 mL saline flush via a large antecubital cannula, continuous ECG recording; if no conversion → 12 mg, then 12 mg. Reduce dose if on dipyridamole or heart transplant; avoid in active asthma.
  • 🛡️ Defibrillator pads applied, resuscitation trolley at bedside before adenosine — anticipate the transient pause and counsel the patient.
  • 🚫 Confirm the rhythm is REGULAR and not pre-excited AF before any AV-nodal blocker — flag on chart.
  • 💊 If adenosine fails or AVNRT recurs: non-DHP calcium-channel blocker (verapamil 5–10 mg IV slowly or diltiazem) or a beta-blocker — only once a regular SVT is confirmed and BP tolerates it.
  • ⚡ If she becomes UNSTABLE: synchronized DC cardioversion (sedate if time allows) — low energy usually suffices for organized SVT.
  • 🫀 12-lead ECG before, during (capture the moment of conversion) and after; basic bloods: U&E, Mg²⁺, TSH (screen for thyrotoxicosis as a driver), pregnancy test.
  • 📞 Refer cardiology/EP for slow-pathway ablation discussion; advise on triggers (caffeine, alcohol) and the self-administered modified Valsalva for future episodes.
  • 🫁 Continuous monitoring, second IV, reassure — anxiety feeds the sympathetic drive that sustains the rhythm.

👩‍⚕️ Nursing Care

🛏️ ED / EP nursing priorities — minute by minute

  • 📟 Continuous telemetry with a printed rhythm strip running across every intervention. The strip capturing the moment of conversion is diagnostic gold for the EP team.
  • 💉 Set up adenosine to be given FAST: draw the 6 mg, draw a 20 mL flush, connect a three-way tap to a large proximal cannula. The drug fails if it is given slowly or through a tiny distal vein — your set-up makes or breaks the treatment.
  • 🗣️ Coach the patient through the modified Valsalva and warn her about the adenosine sensation beforehand — a calm, prepared patient tolerates both far better.
  • 🩸 BP every few minutes — borderline pressures can drop; watch for presyncope or any adverse feature that would tip her toward cardioversion.
  • 🛡️ Defib pads on and trolley ready before adenosine. Trouble is rare, but the drug deliberately blocks the AV node, so you respect it.
  • 🔇 Calm the room: anxiety pours out catecholamines that feed the re-entry circuit and make termination harder. A composed bedside is genuinely therapeutic.
  • 📋 Document the rhythm before/after each maneuver and drug, the dose, the time, and tolerance — and keep a copy of the sinus-rhythm ECG to compare P-wave morphology.
  • 🧠 Re-check consciousness and pulse character — a patient who stops talking or whose pulse changes needs immediate medical review.
  • 👨‍👩‍👦 Keep the family/companion informed (see S16) — a fit young person with a racing heart is frightening to watch.
⚔️ ONE-LINER RULE"The nurse who sets adenosine up to be pushed FAST with a flush, and coaches the modified Valsalva properly, does most of the antiarrhythmic work in the room."
📢 Escalate IMMEDIATELY forNew hypotension with shock · syncope or worsening presyncope · chest pain or ischemic ST changes · pulmonary edema · an irregular rhythm appearing (think pre-excited AF — stop AV-nodal blockers) · any deterioration suggesting the patient is now UNSTABLE and needs cardioversion.

Resting (sinus) 12-leadNo delta wave / pre-excitation—Crucial: no WPW pattern argues against AVRT and supports AVNRT; it also reassures before any AV-nodal blocker.
⚔️ ONE-LINER RULE"In AVNRT the bloods are usually boring — and that's the point. A normal-heart, normal-electrolyte, regular narrow tachycardia is a wiring problem, not a chemistry problem. The ECG, not the lab, makes this diagnosis."
🧠 Why the SINUS-rhythm ECG matters as much as the tachycardia ECGAlways obtain a 12-lead once she's back in sinus rhythm. Two reasons: (1) compare it to the tachycardia ECG — a terminal pseudo-r' (V1) or pseudo-S (inferior leads) that vanishes after conversion confirms it was retrograde atrial activation, i.e. typical AVNRT; (2) look for a delta wave / short PR of WPW — if present, the rhythm may have been AVRT and the accessory pathway changes the long-term ablation target and the danger of pre-excited AF.

📈 ECG / Imaging

🫀 The presenting rhythm — read it like a detective

Regular, narrow, monomorphic tachycardia at ~188 with a small terminal pseudo-r' (the retrograde P buried in the QRS). At the arrow, adenosine blocks the AV node and the re-entry loop terminates abruptly into sinus rhythm — the signature of nodal-dependent SVT.

🔎 The AVNRT recognition toolkit (memorize the high-yield ones)

FeatureWhy it means AVNRT
📏 Narrow QRS, regular< 120 ms and metronome-regular — ventricles via the normal His-Purkinje system, so the circuit is supraventricular/nodal.
👻 No visible P wavesThe retrograde P is buried in the QRS because atria and ventricles fire almost simultaneously — the hallmark of typical (slow-fast) AVNRT.
🔼 Pseudo-r' in V1A small terminal positive deflection in V1 that wasn't there in sinus rhythm — retrograde atrial activation peeking out of the QRS.
🔽 Pseudo-S in II, III, aVFA small terminal negative deflection in the inferior leads — the same retrograde P, seen from below.
⚡ Sudden onset & offsetRe-entry starts and stops in a single beat — paroxysmal "on/off like a switch." Contrast with sinus tach, which warms up and cools down.

⚔️ ONE-LINER RULE"A new pseudo-r' in V1 or pseudo-S inferiorly that vanishes the moment the tachycardia breaks is a retrograde P wave — and near-proof of typical AVNRT."

🫀 Post-conversion 12-lead & echo

  • 📈 Post-conversion 12-lead: sinus rhythm ~72/min, normal axis, normal PR, no delta wave, and — compared to the tachycardia strip — the V1 pseudo-r' and inferior pseudo-S have disappeared, confirming they were retrograde atrial activation (typical AVNRT).
  • 🩻 Echo: structurally normal heart, normal chambers, normal valves, EF 62%. This is expected and reassuring — AVNRT is a wiring problem on a healthy heart.
  • 🧪 Ambulatory monitoring (later): if the diagnosis is ever in doubt, a Holter/event recorder can capture onset (a premature atrial beat initiating the run) — useful before ablation.
⚠️ The ECG's favorite trap in narrow-complex tachycardia"It's regular and narrow, so it must be AVNRT." Not always — atrial flutter with 2:1 block sits at almost exactly 150/min and looks deceptively regular. Adenosine (or a careful look for flutter waves at 300/min) unmasks it: the ventricular rate slows with AV block while sawtooth flutter waves march on. Always interrogate the response to nodal block.

When unsure and unstable, the safest "drug" is synchronized electricity.

🏠 Definitive & preventive therapy (beyond breaking tonight's episode)

  • 🗺️ Slow-pathway catheter ablation — the definitive cure: >95% success, ~1% risk of AV block/pacemaker. For recurrent, symptomatic AVNRT this beats lifelong tablets.
  • 💊 Chronic oral AV-nodal blockers (beta-blocker or non-DHP CCB) — for patients who decline or defer ablation, to reduce episode frequency.
  • 💼 'Pill-in-the-pocket' — a single oral dose at onset for infrequent, well-tolerated attacks.
  • ☕ Trigger modification & self-Valsalva training — reduce caffeine/alcohol, manage stress, and teach the modified Valsalva so she can often abort attacks herself.

📟 Monitoring

📡 What to watch and why

  • 📈 Continuous telemetry through the maneuvers and drug pushes; print a strip across every intervention — the conversion strip is diagnostic gold for the EP team.
  • 🩸 Frequent BP — borderline pressures can drop; a falling BP or new presyncope means re-evaluate stability and consider cardioversion.
  • 📐 Watch the post-adenosine rhythm — expect a brief pause/AV block; rarely a few seconds of bradycardia, ectopy or even transient AF before sinus resumes. Be ready, but don't over-react to the expected pause.
  • 🫀 Compare the tachycardia and sinus 12-leads — disappearance of the pseudo-r'/pseudo-S confirms AVNRT; a delta wave changes the plan.
  • 😰 Symptom + anxiety monitoring — recurrence is often heralded by the patient feeling it "switch on" again; her own report is a sensitive monitor.
  • 🔁 Watch for recurrence — AVNRT can restart, especially if the catecholamine state (anxiety, pain, caffeine) persists. Reassurance and trigger removal are part of monitoring.
  • 🔋 If referred for ablation: the EP study itself is the definitive "monitor" — inducing and mapping the circuit to confirm the slow-pathway target.
⚔️ ONE-LINER RULE"After you break AVNRT, the monitor's job changes from 'can I terminate it?' to 'is it coming back?' — and the two things that answer that are the rhythm strip and whether the trigger is still present."
S13

⚠️ Complications

💀 What can go wrong — and the mechanism

  • 🌀 Misdiagnosing pre-excited AF as 'SVT' — the most dangerous error. Giving an AV-nodal blocker to an irregular wide tachycardia shunts conduction down the accessory pathway → very rapid ventricular rates → VF. Always confirm regularity first.
  • 📉 Hemodynamic deterioration — a borderline patient can tip into shock or presyncope if the rate is sustained, mandating a switch to cardioversion.
  • ⏸️ Adenosine effects — transient flushing, chest tightness, dyspnea, sense of doom, and a brief pause; rarely transient AF, bradycardia or bronchospasm (asthmatics). Almost always self-limiting because of the ~10 s half-life.
  • 🩺 Verapamil hypotension — second-line CCB can drop the pressure (vasodilation + negative inotropy), especially if the rhythm was actually VT — hence "confirm SVT first."
  • 🗺️ Ablation complications — slow-pathway ablation carries a small (~1%) risk of complete AV block requiring a pacemaker (the compact node is nearby), plus the usual access-site/effusion risks.
  • 🔁 Recurrence — without trigger control or definitive ablation, AVNRT comes back; it's benign but can be life-disrupting.

🎭 Morbidity & Mortality Conference — "The Adenosine That Became a Defibrillation"

The case: A 29-year-old man arrived with palpitations and a fast tachycardia the night team labeled "SVT." They reached for adenosine — but the rhythm was actually pre-excited atrial fibrillation (irregular, varying QRS width, a delta wave on an old ECG nobody pulled). The transient AV-nodal block from adenosine pushed conduction entirely down the accessory pathway; the ventricular rate accelerated to over 280 and degenerated into VF. He arrested and needed prolonged defibrillation. In a parallel near-miss the same week, a different team gave adenosine through a hand vein slowly, watched it "fail," and escalated to IV verapamil for what was in fact a regular AVNRT — the verapamil dropped the BP dangerously before the rhythm finally broke. Two errors, one lesson.

🧀 The Swiss-cheese holes that lined up:

🌀 REGULARITY NOT CHECKEDIrregular wide tachycardia = pre-excited AF, not simple SVT — AV-nodal block is lethal there.
📈 OLD ECG NOT PULLEDA documented delta wave (WPW) would have changed everything.
💉 GIVEN WRONGSlow push through a distal vein → adenosine metabolized before it works → false 'failure.'
💊 PREMATURE ESCALATIONJumping to verapamil for a 'failed' adenosine that was never given properly.
🌙 SYSTEMNight fatigue, no senior review, no "confirm regular + check for pre-excitation" default.

🛡️ What would have saved them: before any AV-nodal blocker, confirm the rhythm is regular and look for pre-excitation (irregular + varying QRS width = pre-excited AF → procainamide or cardiovert, never block the node); give adenosine FAST through a large proximal line with a flush, defibrillator ready; don't call adenosine a 'failure' until it's been given correctly; climb the ladder in order. This is exactly why your team confirmed a regular narrow rhythm and a clean sinus ECG before pushing tonight.

⚔️ ONE-LINER RULE"'It's just SVT' is the sentence written before an AV-nodal blocker turns pre-excited AF into VF. Confirm REGULAR and rule out pre-excitation before you ever block the node."

🏠 Discharge

📋 Discharge plan & safety net

  • 💊 Medications: usually none needed acutely after conversion; consider a low-dose beta-blocker or non-DHP CCB if episodes are frequent and she's deferring ablation. Provide a clear written plan.
  • 💨 Self-management: taught and given a written guide to the modified Valsalva (blow into a syringe, then lie flat with legs raised) so she can often abort future attacks at home.
  • 🗺️ EP follow-up for elective slow-pathway ablation booked before she leaves; cardiology clinic appointment confirmed.
  • ☕ Trigger plan: reduce caffeine and alcohol (especially during exams), manage stress and sleep, stay hydrated; explain that these fire the premature beats that start AVNRT.
  • 🚩 Red flags → call emergency services / return: palpitations with chest pain, breathlessness, fainting or severe lightheadedness, or an episode that won't break with her Valsalva within a reasonable time.
  • 📚 Education: AVNRT explained in plain language (a tiny short-circuit in the heart's wiring, not a sign of a damaged heart), reassurance about its generally benign nature, and what to expect from an ablation.
⚔️ ONE-LINER RULE"Discharge after AVNRT isn't 'rhythm fixed, go home' — it's a patient who can break her own attacks, knows her triggers, knows when to return, and has a referral that could cure her for good."
