🧭 The three exertional-syncope killers to sort at the bedside

For "exertional collapse in an older adult" the reflex differential is fixed LV outflow obstruction (severe aortic stenosis / HCM), arrhythmia (complete heart block, VT — AS causes both via calcium into the conducting system), and massive PE. The murmur, the pulse character and the ECG separate them in minutes (see S14). A true exertional syncope is never "just a vasovagal."

⚠️ The trap that defines this caseThe instinct on seeing "elderly + chest tightness + BP 104/86 + crackles" is to treat "angina and mild heart failure": GTN, an ACE inhibitor, a good dose of furosemide. In a fixed obstruction every one of those drops preload or afterload, and the heart cannot increase flow across the narrow valve to compensate — blood pressure and coronary perfusion fall off a cliff. Recognise the physiology first; the "obvious" treatment is the danger.

  2. 🫀 Urgent bedside + formal echo — the test that grades the valve (peak velocity, mean gradient, valve area) and the LV.
  3. 🚫 Explicitly withhold vasodilators — no GTN, no ACE inhibitor, no aggressive diuresis; sign the chart so no one else gives them.
  4. 🩸 Cautious IV fluids if under-filled (the hypertrophied LV is preload-dependent); phenylephrine drawn up and ready for hypotension.
  5. 📞 Refer cardiology now — symptomatic severe AS is a Class I indication for valve replacement (AVR or TAVI); plan the route to definitive therapy.
⚔️ ONE-LINER RULE"Keep the AS patient FULL and TIGHT — preload and afterload are the only things keeping blood across that valve; phenylephrine, not nitrates."
🧪 Resident's self-testThe BP is 104/86 and he has crackles — a colleague suggests 40 mg IV furosemide and a GTN infusion "to offload the failure." Do it?

No — not reflexively. In severe AS the congestion comes from a stiff, pressure-overloaded LV, and stroke volume is fixed by the valve. GTN and heavy diuresis slash preload/afterload; because flow across the valve can't rise to compensate, cardiac output and coronary perfusion plummet and he can arrest. Give only gentle, carefully titrated diuresis if truly congested, avoid nitrates, keep him full and tight, and get the valve fixed. The real fix for the failing AS heart is the valve, not the loop diuretic.

🧠 Teaching Points + Interactive Simulation

🔬 The mechanism — WHY the syncope, WHY the danger (the WHY behind the WHY)

  1. 🚪 Fixed obstruction: a calcified, narrowed aortic valve creates a fixed resistance to LV outflow. Stroke volume becomes flow-limited by the valve — the heart cannot simply pump more when the body demands it.
  2. 💪 Pressure-overload hypertrophy: to generate the pressure needed to eject through the stenosis, the LV hypertrophies concentrically. It becomes thick, stiff and preload-dependent — it needs a full ventricle and atrial kick to fill.
  3. ⚡ Supply–demand vulnerability (the angina): the thick muscle has high oxygen demand, while its raised wall stress and high LV pressure compress the sub-endocardial coronary flow that occurs in diastole. Angina can occur even with normal coronary arteries.
  4. 😵 Exertional syncope: exercise vasodilates the periphery, but the fixed valve cannot raise cardiac output to match → systemic and cerebral perfusion pressure fall → collapse (arrhythmia from calcium into the conducting tissue adds to the risk).
  5. 💀 The ED trap: anything that drops preload or afterload — GTN/nitrates, ACE inhibitors, over-diuresis, spinal/sedation-induced vasodilatation — lowers the pressure driving flow across the valve. Output and coronary perfusion crash, the thick heart goes ischaemic, and it can spiral to arrest.
⚔️ ONE-LINER RULE"A fixed valve can't compensate: whatever you take away from preload or afterload, the stenosis won't give back in flow — so keep the AS patient full and tight."

⚖️ Contrast pair — Fixed (AS) vs Dynamic (HCM) obstruction

🚪 Aortic stenosis — FIXED obstruction

  • Obstruction is a rigid narrowed valve; severity doesn't change beat-to-beat
  • Worsened by dropping afterload/preload (vasodilators, diuresis) → less flow across the valve
  • Manage: keep FULL and TIGHT; phenylephrine if hypotensive; fix the valve

🌀 HCM — DYNAMIC (LVOT) obstruction

  • Obstruction is muscular, worsened by a small, vigorous, empty ventricle
  • Worsened by decreased preload/afterload AND increased contractility — physiology overlaps AS, murmur behaviour differs (louder with Valsalva/standing)
  • Manage: fill it, slow it, avoid inotropes/vasodilators

💡 Both hate an empty, vasodilated circulation — but AS is a valve problem and HCM a muscle problem. Get the murmur manoeuvres right and you'll tell them apart.

📋 Orders

🖊️ The order set (17:48)

  • 📈 12-lead ECG now + continuous cardiac monitoring with defib pads on (screen for LVH/strain, AV block, VT).
  • 🫀 Urgent echocardiogram — bedside now, formal transthoracic study to grade the valve (peak velocity, mean gradient, aortic valve area) and LV function.
  • 🧪 Bloods: FBC, U&E, troponin (expect mild elevation from demand ischaemia — NOT a coronary occlusion to stent), BNP/NT-proBNP, glucose, coag, group & save (pre-procedure).
  • 🚫 DO NOT give GTN / nitrates / ACE inhibitors; no aggressive diuresis — flag on the drug chart; these drop preload/afterload across a fixed valve.
  • 💉 Phenylephrine drawn up and labelled — pure alpha-agonist, the pressor of choice if he becomes hypotensive (restores coronary/systemic perfusion pressure without dropping the gradient).
  • 🩸 Cautious IV fluids if under-filled; the hypertrophied LV is preload-dependent. Small aliquots, reassess.
  • 🫁 O₂ only if SpO₂ < 90%; keep him calm and supine-ish as tolerated; analgesia titrated carefully (avoid sudden vasodilatation).
  • 📞 Refer cardiology urgently — symptomatic severe AS; plan the Heart-Team route to AVR/TAVI, with balloon aortic valvuloplasty as a bridge if he decompensates.
⚔️ ONE-LINER RULE"In severe AS the most important orders are the ones you cross OUT: no GTN, no ACE inhibitor, no big diuretic dose — keep him full and tight and get the valve fixed."
S8

👩‍⚕️ Nursing Care

🛏️ Resus-bay priorities — the nurse guards the "do-no-harm" rule

  • 📟 Continuous telemetry, defib pads attached; watch for AV block, VT and AF — the stiff LV depends on the atrial kick and diastolic filling time.
  • 🚫 Be the last checkpoint on vasodilators — if anyone reaches for a GTN spray/infusion, an ACE inhibitor or a big furosemide bolus, pause and query it. In severe AS these can crash the pressure in seconds.
  • 🩸 Blood pressure every 5 min during any intervention; report a falling systolic immediately — hypotension in AS is a pre-arrest sign. Keep phenylephrine and the pressure ready.
  • 💧 Cannulate ×2; give prescribed fluids cautiously and reassess — the preload-dependent hypertrophied ventricle needs a full tank, but overload worsens congestion.
  • 🧾 Keep him calm, flat-to-semi-recumbent as tolerated, and minimise straining/Valsalva — sudden preload swings are poorly tolerated.
  • 😵 Fall/collapse precautions: assisted mobilisation only, bed low, call bell in reach — exertional syncope means the next faint could be at the toilet.
⚔️ ONE-LINER RULE"In aortic stenosis the nurse who questions the GTN spray saves the life — the most dangerous drug in the room is the one that 'obviously' treats the chest pain."

S12

📟 Monitoring

📡 What to watch and why

  • 📈 Continuous telemetry with defib to hand — VT/VF peaks in the first hours; ST-segment monitoring catches re-occlusion.
  • 🧪 Serial troponin to define the trajectory; repeat 12-leads with every symptom change.
  • 🩸 BP each 5–15 min — hypotension may mean RV involvement, over-nitration, or evolving shock.
  • 🫁 SpO₂ (target ≥ 94% only if hypoxic), respiratory rate for evolving LV failure.
  • 🧮 Recompute risk (GRACE) once troponin returns — GRACE > 140 pushes toward early (< 24 h) invasive management.
⚔️ ONE-LINER RULE"After you decide 'ACS,' the monitor's question changes from 'is he ischaemic?' to 'is he about to arrest or re-occlude?' — and the answers are the rhythm and the recurring pain."

⚠️ Complications

💀 What can go wrong in the ED phase

  • ⚡ VT / VF / cardiac arrest — commonest early killer; why pads stay on.
  • 🫀 Evolution to STEMI / re-occlusion — the "NSTEMI" that declares itself on the 4th ECG.
  • 💔 Acute LV failure / cardiogenic shock — large territory at risk.
  • 🔊 Mechanical (later, days 3–7): papillary muscle rupture (acute MR), VSD, free-wall rupture — a new murmur + crash.
  • 🧠 Bleeding from antithrombotics; contrast nephropathy around PCI.
  • 🩹 The system complication: premature discharge — the biggest "complication" of all in the ED.
⚔️ ONE-LINER RULE"The deadliest ED complication of ACS isn't in the patient — it's the decision to send them home. Treat disposition as a procedure with its own checklist."

🏠 Discharge

📋 (Post-PCI) discharge & the low-risk chest-pain discharge — two different worlds

🫀 This patient (confirmed MI)

  • DAPT 12 months, high-intensity statin, ACEi/ARB, beta-blocker, ± MRA if EF < 40%
  • Cardiac rehab, smoking cessation, diabetes & BP control
  • Red flags: recurrent chest pain, breathlessness, syncope → 999
  • Cardiology + GP follow-up; no driving per local rules

🟢 The genuinely low-risk chest pain

  • HEART 0–3 + negative serial hs-troponin + non-ischaemic ECG
  • Shared decision-making, written safety-net, timely outpatient testing if needed
  • Explicit "come back immediately if…" advice

🔬 Advanced Topics — Fellowship Level

🧠 Beyond the pathway

  • 🩻 The "Occlusion MI (OMI)" paradigm: the STEMI/NSTEMI dichotomy misses acute coronary occlusion without ST-elevation criteria. OMI-vs-NOMI thinking (subtle occlusion patterns, hyperacute T waves, modified-Sgarbossa, AI-ECG) identifies patients who benefit from emergent reperfusion despite "non-STEMI" labels.
  • 🧪 hs-troponin nuance: sex-specific 99th-percentile cut-offs; the value of very low single values (< limit of detection) for immediate rule-out; assay-specific 0/1-h and 0/2-h absolute delta thresholds (ESC 2023).
  • 🫀 MINOCA (MI with non-obstructive coronaries): work up plaque disruption (intravascular imaging — OCT/IVUS), coronary spasm (provocation), spontaneous coronary artery dissection (SCAD — think young women, peripartum), microvascular disease, and cardiac MRI to distinguish true MI from myocarditis/Takotsubo.
  • 🧬 Antiplatelet timing debate: routine pre-treatment with P2Y₁₂ before angiography in NSTE-ACS is no longer recommended (no ischaemic benefit, more bleeding — ISAR-REACT 5 favoured prasugrel over ticagrelor with a deferred-loading strategy).
  • 🖥️ Coronary CT angiography in low–intermediate-risk ED chest pain: high NPV for ruling out CAD (ROMICAT-II, PROMISE), useful when troponin/ECG are equivocal and pre-test probability is modest.
  • 🩸 Cardiogenic-shock ACS: culprit-only PCI (CULPRIT-SHOCK), early revascularisation (SHOCK), judicious use of mechanical support.

🎭 Morbidity & Mortality Conference

🎭 M&M — "The Antacid That Killed"

The case: A 61-year-old diabetic man came to a crowded ED at 02:00 with "burning" epigastric/chest discomfort and one vomit. Triaged as "dyspepsia." He was given an antacid "GI cocktail," felt "a bit better," and — with the department full — was moved to the corridor to await review. The single ECG taken at triage showed tall R waves and ST depression in V1–V3; no one recorded posterior leads and no one repeated it. Ninety minutes later he was found unresponsive in the corridor chair — a posterior STEMI that had extended, then arrested in VF. Resuscitation failed.

🧀 The Swiss-cheese holes that lined up:

🧠 ANCHORING"Dyspepsia" label stuck; the GI cocktail "response" falsely reassured.
📈 ECG NOT READPosterior STEMI pattern (ST↓ V1–V3) unrecognised; posterior leads never done.
🔁 NO SERIAL ECGOne snapshot, never repeated despite ongoing symptoms.
🏥 SYSTEMOvercrowding → corridor care → no monitoring, no defib to hand.
🩺 RISK IGNOREDDiabetic with atypical presentation treated as low-risk.

🛡️ What would have saved him: treat every diabetic with chest/epigastric pain as high-risk; read ST depression in V1–V3 as a possible posterior STEMI and record V7–V9; repeat the ECG on any ongoing symptom; keep suspected ACS monitored with defib to hand — never in an unmonitored corridor; and never let a "GI cocktail response" close the door. This is exactly the discipline that saved the patient in Bed 1.

⚔️ ONE-LINER RULE"'Responded to antacid' has killed more patients than it has ever diagnosed. Symptom relief is not a diagnosis — the ECG and the troponin are."
