🚦 Recognising the emergency — the perforation triad

Coronary perforation is rare (roughly 0.2–0.6% of PCI) but disproportionately lethal — it is the leading procedural cause of acute cardiac tamponade in the lab. Recognition is visual and haemodynamic: contrast where it should not be (the vessel silhouette suddenly "leaks"), a falling blood pressure, and often a paradoxical bradycardia (vagal surge plus failing cardiac output). The higher-risk substrates are exactly the case you are in: elderly, female, small/tortuous or heavily calcified vessels, and aggressive lesion modification (oversized balloons, atherectomy, high-pressure post-dilation, distal wire migration).

⚔️ ONE-LINER RULE"Contrast outside the vessel plus a falling pressure is a perforation until proven otherwise. Your hands INFLATE — they do not withdraw."

📋 Pre-Procedure Workup & Planning

🧠 Rewind — the perforation was seeded before it happenedMost perforations are foreseeable. The pre-procedure plan is where you decide the calcium-modification strategy, wire choice, and balloon sizing that will either respect the vessel or tear it. A calcified undilatable lesion demands preparation — not brute force.

🧾 The pre-PCI risk assessment for perforation

  • 🪨 Calcium & vessel substrate — dense circumferential calcium, small caliber, tortuosity, and CTO work all raise perforation risk. Plan atherectomy or lithotripsy for undilatable calcium rather than oversizing a balloon into a rigid wall.
  • 🧵 Wire strategy — polymer-jacketed and stiff/hydrophilic wires migrate distally and are the classic cause of distal wire (Ellis III / "cavity spilling") perforations; keep the wire tip in view and out of tiny distal branches.
  • 📏 Balloon/stent sizing — size 1:1 to the true reference; an oversized balloon in calcium is the textbook mid-vessel Ellis II–III rupture.
  • 🫘 Renal & bleeding status — CKD and anticoagulation both matter: they do not stop you treating, but they shape contrast load and the reversal plan if you perforate.
  • 🖥️ Baseline echo — know whether there is a pre-existing effusion; a "new" stripe is easier to call against a clean baseline.
⚔️ ONE-LINER RULE"Prepare calcium before you dilate it. An oversized balloon in a rigid, calcified wall does not open the artery — it splits it."

🩸 Vascular Access & Setup

🖐️ Access matters more once you are bleeding

🖐️ Radial (default)

  • Fewer access-site bleeds — but a 6 Fr radial guide LIMITS your bail-out options
  • A large covered stent (Graftmaster is bulky) may need 7–8 Fr or a guide-extension / ping-pong technique
  • Know your covered-stent profile BEFORE you need it

🦵 Femoral (bail-out / support)

  • Bigger sheath = easier covered-stent delivery and dual-catheter ("block-and-deliver") technique
  • Fast large-bore access if you must escalate to mechanical support
  • Also the route for an intra-aortic balloon pump if shock deepens
⚔️ ONE-LINER RULE"The moment you perforate, your access size becomes destiny. If your covered stent will not pass a 6 Fr guide, you needed to know that before the leak — not during it."

💉 Anticoagulation & the reversal you may suddenly need

  • 🩸 Heparin (UFH): titrated to ACT ~250–300 s for PCI — fully anticoagulated blood pours through a perforation. It is reversible with protamine (roughly 1 mg per 100 U of heparin given), which is why heparin is the friend of the perforator.
  • 🧪 Bivalirudin: a direct thrombin inhibitor with a short half-life and NO specific antidote — you cannot fully reverse it, only wait it out (and consider stopping the infusion, giving fluids/blood). This is the trade-off to remember when a bleed starts.
  • 🧭 Guide & equipment ready: keep the balloon that just caused the leak — you may re-inflate it to tamponade. Have a covered stent, coils, and the pericardiocentesis tray reachable, not in a store cupboard.

🔬 Lesion Assessment — Physiology & Imaging

🫀 The lesion that led here — and why imaging mattersThis was a calcified mid-LAD stenosis. Intravascular imaging (IVUS/OCT) up front tells you whether calcium needs modification, sizes the vessel so you do NOT oversize a balloon, and — after a leak — helps localise and characterise the perforation. In non-culprit or ambiguous lesions, physiology (FFR ≤ 0.80 / iFR ≤ 0.89) decides whether to treat at all — and every lesion you decline to treat is a perforation you can never cause.

📏 FFR / iFR — treat only what is ischaemic

  • FFR (fractional flow reserve) — ratio of flow across a stenosis to normal during hyperaemia (adenosine). FFR ≤ 0.80 = ischaemic → treat. Above 0.80 → defer and treat medically.
  • iFR (instantaneous wave-free ratio) — a resting index, no adenosine. iFR ≤ 0.89 = significant.
  • 🛡️ The safety angle: deferring a non-ischaemic lesion removes all its procedural risk — including perforation. The safest complication is the one you never set up.

🖥️ Intravascular imaging — IVUS vs OCT

🔵 IVUS

  • Ultrasound — deep penetration, sizes the vessel, quantifies calcium arc & plaque burden
  • Best for sizing to avoid oversizing; localises perforation and residual leak; no contrast flush needed (useful when you are trying to STOP contrast)

🟠 OCT

  • Light — ultra-high resolution; superb for edge dissection, apposition, calcium thickness
  • Needs a contrast flush — relatively contraindicated when actively perforating (do NOT flush into a leak)
⚔️ ONE-LINER RULE"Size to the vessel, not to your ambition. Imaging that prevents an oversized balloon prevents the perforation — and after a leak, IVUS localises it without flushing more contrast into the hole."

🛠️ Intervention — Step-by-Step Execution

🎈 The same balloon that can tear a vessel is your first tool to tamponade the leak; a covered stent seals it. Source: Wikimedia Commons (Blausen)

🪜 The perforation rescue sequence — each step is a decision

  1. 🎈 Balloon tamponade NOW — re-inflate a balloon (the one already there, or a slightly oversized one) across / just proximal to the leak at low pressure. Keep it up for 5–10 minutes. Do NOT remove the wire or balloon.
  2. 🧪 Reverse anticoagulation — give protamine to reverse heparin (aim ACT back toward normal). If on bivalirudin, stop the infusion (no antidote) and support with fluids/blood.
  3. 🎯 Grade & localise — deflate briefly under angiography (or use IVUS without flushing) to define Ellis grade and vessel size.
  4. 🩹 Definitive seal by location: large proximal/mid vessel Ellis III → covered stent (PK Papyrus is lower-profile/6 Fr-friendly; Graftmaster is a bulkier PTFE sandwich needing 7–8 Fr); distal/collateral → coil, fat, or thrombin embolization; consider a "block-and-deliver" dual-catheter technique to keep tamponade while delivering the covered stent.
  5. 🫗 Drain the tamponade — echo-guided pericardiocentesis if there is haemodynamic compromise; autotransfuse the aspirated blood; keep surgery on stand-by.
⚔️ ONE-LINER RULE"Balloon first, reverse the heparin, seal by location, drain the sac — in that order, every time. The order is the whole algorithm."

🎬 Complication Drill — "It Is Not Over Yet"

❤️ Stability: ●●●○○⏱️ Elapsed: 0 s⚡ Path XP: 0
You have controlled the leak, but 5 minutes later the BP crashes again to 60/40, HR 38, the JVP is up, there is pulsus paradoxus, and echo shows a GROWING pericardial stripe with right-atrial and right-ventricular diastolic collapse. What is happening, and what do you do FIRST?
⚔️ ONE-LINER RULE"Tamponade is obstructive shock — the treatment is to DRAIN, not to diurese. A diuretic or a vasodilator drops the preload the failing heart is desperately using to fill, and it will arrest."
🔁 The tamponade vicious cycleBlood in the sac raises pericardial pressure → compresses the thin-walled right heart in diastole → impaired filling → falling stroke volume → falling cardiac output and coronary perfusion → the heart pumps even less → pressure rises further. It is a self-amplifying spiral, and only a few hundred millilitres accumulating rapidly can be fatal, because the stiff pericardium has no time to stretch.

🏠 Post-Procedure Care, Debrief & Assessment

🏥 After a sealed perforation — the next 24 hours are the risk window

  • 🫗 Serial echo — at 1 h, 4–6 h and before discharge from the unit; a re-accumulating effusion is a re-bleed until proven otherwise. Keep the pericardial drain in until output is minimal.
  • 📟 Monitored bed / CCU — continuous haemodynamics; treat delayed hypotension as recurrent tamponade first.
  • 💊 Antithrombotic balancing act — you reversed heparin to stop the bleed, but a freshly placed covered stent is highly thrombogenic and needs DAPT. Restart/continue antiplatelets once the bleeding is controlled; individualise timing — this tension is the essence of post-perforation care.
  • 🔪 Keep surgery briefed until stability is proven; do not discharge from monitoring on the day of a large perforation.
⚔️ ONE-LINER RULE"A covered stent is a thrombogenic patch that still needs DAPT — the same patient you just stopped bleeding now must not clot the stent. Reverse to survive the bleed, then re-cover to survive the stent."

🔭 Advanced Topics — Fellowship Level

  • 🥪 Covered-stent techniques: the Graftmaster is a PTFE "stent sandwich" (bulky, needs 7–8 Fr, less deliverable in tortuosity); PK Papyrus is single-layer, lower-profile, 6 Fr-compatible. Block-and-deliver / dual-catheter (ping-pong) keeps a tamponade balloon inflated through one guide while the covered stent is delivered through a second — no interruption in haemostasis.
  • 🌀 Distal embolization materials: microcoils, subcutaneous autologous fat, thrombin, gelfoam, and microspheres — for distal wire/collateral perforations where a covered stent cannot reach; deliver via microcatheter as distal as safe.
  • 🕳️ Ellis III cavity-spilling (III-CS): a perforation draining into a cardiac chamber or coronary sinus may NOT tamponade (it decompresses internally) but can cause a left-to-right shunt or coronary steal — a distinct management problem.
  • 🪨 Atherectomy-related perforation: rotational/orbital atherectomy and laser can perforate directly — burr/crown into a tortuous or subintimal position; meticulous wire position and vessel selection are prevention.
  • 🫀 Refractory obstructive shock: if drainage does not restore output (ongoing bleed, RV failure, concomitant MI), escalate to IABP, Impella, or VA-ECMO and emergency surgery.
  • 🔪 Surgical bail-out: when the leak cannot be sealed percutaneously — direct repair, vessel ligation, evacuation of clot, ± CABG if the sealing sacrifices a large territory.

🎭 Morbidity & Mortality Conference

🎭 M&M — "The Distal Leak That Bled in Recovery"

The case: During a CTO PCI, a stiff polymer-jacketed wire was advanced into a small distal branch. A faint distal stain was noted on one cine and dismissed as "just a little staining, Ellis I." The final angiogram looked clean, the patient was stable, and she was sent to recovery still fully anticoagulated. Forty minutes later she became restless and hypotensive; the nurse called for a fluid bolus. By the time an echo was done she had a large effusion with RV collapse — a delayed distal wire perforation that had bled slowly the entire time. She arrested in recovery. Reversal, pericardiocentesis and a covered stent came too late.

🧀 The Swiss-cheese holes that lined up:

🧵 WIRE TOO DISTALA stiff/polymer wire pushed into a tiny branch — the classic distal perforation setup.
👁️ MISGRADED & DISMISSEDFrank staining called "Ellis I" and ignored — a distal leak can bleed slowly for a long time.
🩸 LEFT ANTICOAGULATEDHeparin never reversed — the slow leak was kept open by the anticoagulation.
🖥️ NO EXIT ECHONo confirmatory echo before leaving the lab; the baseline effusion was never checked.
⏳ DELAYED RECOGNITIONRecovery hypotension treated as "fluid-responsive" instead of tamponade; echo done far too late.

🛡️ What would have saved her: keep the wire tip in view and out of tiny distal branches; treat ANY distal stain as a potential slow leak, not a nuisance; reverse anticoagulation and embolize/observe before leaving; do a completion echo before the patient leaves the lab; and treat unexplained recovery hypotension as tamponade until an echo says otherwise. A distal perforation is quiet — until it is not.

⚔️ ONE-LINER RULE"There is no such thing as 'just a little staining' in a fully anticoagulated patient. Reverse, watch, echo before you leave — a distal wire leak kills slowly and silently."
