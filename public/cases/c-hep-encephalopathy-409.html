🧭 The three things that turn "just HE" into a resuscitation

Before you settle on encephalopathy, actively exclude the reversible killers that hide behind confusion in liver disease: hypoglycaemia (bedside glucose), a bleed (PR, Hb, haemodynamics — variceal until proven otherwise), and sepsis / SBP (temp, ascitic tap). A focal neurological sign or a head-injury story adds a fourth: intracranial bleed (these patients are coagulopathic and fall).

⚠️ The trap of "he is a known encephalopath"A prior label of HE does not immunise against a subdural, a hypo, or meningitis. The more times someone is admitted as "his liver again," the more likely the one time it is something else gets missed. Re-earn the diagnosis each admission.

🎯 Her order of operations

  2. 💧 Diagnostic ascitic tap (cell count + culture) BEFORE antibiotics if feasible — SBP is present in a meaningful minority of hospitalised cirrhotics with HE and is easy to miss.
  3. 🧪 Full bloods: FBC, U&E (renal failure, Na⁺, K⁺), LFTs, glucose, CRP, cultures, and an ammonia only to support (not grade).
  4. 💊 Lactulose now, titrated to 2–3 soft stools/day; add enemas if he cannot take oral / is too drowsy.
  5. 🚫 Stop the zopiclone and all sedatives/opioids; correct electrolytes; avoid over-diuresis; give thiamine (cover Wernicke).
⚔️ ONE-LINER RULE"Treat the trigger and the encephalopathy melts. Give lactulose but chase the precipitant — the lactulose without the SBP-tap and the PR is half a treatment."
🧪 Resident's self-testThe night SHO suggests midazolam "to settle his agitation" so imaging is easier. Agree?

No — this can be lethal. A cirrhotic clears benzodiazepines poorly; sedation deepens the encephalopathy, obtunds him further, and risks losing the airway. Agitation in HE is treated by treating the HE and its precipitant, optimising the environment, and — if the airway is threatened — a controlled anaesthetic intubation, NOT a ward benzo. Sedatives are a documented precipitant, not a treatment.

🧠 Teaching Points + Interactive Simulation

🔬 The mechanism — WHY the brain fails (the WHY behind the WHY)

  1. 🦠 Gut makes ammonia: colonic bacteria and enterocyte glutaminase generate NH₃/NH₄⁺ from dietary protein, blood, and urea. Constipation and a GI bleed (a huge protein load of blood in the gut) both crank up production.
  2. 🫁 The failing / shunted liver cannot clear it: normally the liver converts ammonia to urea (urea cycle) and glutamine. In cirrhosis, hepatocyte failure PLUS portosystemic shunting (spontaneous shunts or TIPS) let ammonia-rich portal blood bypass the liver straight into the systemic circulation. This is the core of the WHY.
  3. 🧠 Ammonia crosses into the brain: astrocytes are the only brain cells that detoxify ammonia — they combine it with glutamate to make glutamine. Glutamine is an osmolyte; it draws water in, causing astrocyte swelling → low-grade cerebral oedema and dysfunction. Add oxidative stress, altered neurotransmission (GABA-ergic tone, inflammation), and you get the clinical syndrome.
  4. 🔁 The vicious cycle: a precipitant (bleed/sepsis/constipation/dehydration) → more ammonia and/or worse clearance → more astrocyte swelling → deeper coma → poorer airway and gut function → aspiration/AKI → even more ammonia. Break the cycle by removing the precipitant AND lowering gut ammonia.
⚔️ ONE-LINER RULE"Ammonia is GUT-made and LIVER-cleared; HE is what happens when the gut makes too much and the shunted, failing liver clears too little — so we treat at BOTH ends: less made (lactulose/rifaximin) and less produced by the trigger (stop the bleed/sepsis/constipation)."

⚖️ Contrast pair — the two levers of lactulose

💩 Lactulose: the cathartic lever

  • Non-absorbable disaccharide → osmotic laxative
  • Clears the nitrogen load (and any blood) OUT of the gut fast
  • Target: 2–3 soft stools/day — under-dosing does nothing, over-dosing dehydrates and can WORSEN HE

🧫 Lactulose: the acidifying lever

  • Colonic bacteria ferment it → acidifies lumen (↓pH)
  • Traps NH₃ as non-absorbable NH₄⁺ ("ion trapping"); shifts flora away from ammonia producers
  • Rifaximin adds a gut-selective antibiotic that lowers ammonia-producing bacteria for recurrence

💡 Lactulose is not "just a laxative" — it is a two-mechanism ammonia-lowering drug. But it only works if it is titrated to stool output, which is exactly what the sim below trains.

🎮 Simulation — The Lactulose Titration Dial

Lactulose is dosed to a target of 2–3 soft stools per day, not to a fixed millilitre. Drag the dose and watch how too little and too much both fail the patient.

Set the dial to explore under-dosing vs the target vs over-dosing.

🎯 Zero stools = the gut keeps making ammonia and HE persists. 2–3 soft stools/day = the sweet spot. More than 4–5 watery stools = dehydration, hypokalaemia and AKI, which PRECIPITATE worse HE — the treatment becomes the trigger.

📋 Orders

🖊️ The order set (22:55)

  • 💊 Lactulose 30 mL PO/NG QDS, then titrate to 2–3 soft stools/day; add phosphate/lactulose enemas if too drowsy to swallow safely.
  • 🩸 Bloods: FBC, U&E (Na⁺, K⁺, creatinine — AKI/HRS), LFTs, glucose, magnesium, phosphate, CRP, coag, group & crossmatch; venous ammonia (supportive only).
  • 🧫 Diagnostic ascitic tap NOW: cell count (PMN ≥ 250/mm³ = SBP), culture in blood-culture bottles at the bedside, albumin/protein.
  • 🦠 Septic screen: blood cultures, urine, CXR; low threshold to start empirical antibiotics for SBP/sepsis after the tap.
  • 🩸 Treat the suspected GI bleed: IV access ×2, fluids/blood to target, terlipressin + prophylactic antibiotics for suspected variceal bleed, urgent endoscopy; hold non-essential antihypertensives.
  • 🚫 STOP: zopiclone and all sedatives/opioids; hold/decrease diuretics if dehydrated or AKI; avoid nephrotoxins.
  • 💉 Thiamine (Pabrinex) to cover Wernicke; correct K⁺/Mg²⁺; maintain glucose.
  • 🍽️ Do NOT protein-restrict — early enteral feeding, 1.2–1.5 g/kg/day protein; NG feed if unsafe swallow.
⚔️ ONE-LINER RULE"The order set for HE is a two-column sheet: LOWER the ammonia (lactulose ± rifaximin) and REMOVE the precipitant (tap, PR, cultures, stop sedatives). Skip the second column and the first column fails."
S8

👩‍⚕️ Nursing Care

🛏️ Ward priorities — the nurse runs the safety net

  • 📟 Neuro obs / GCS hourly with grade tracking; a falling GCS toward III–IV is an airway emergency — escalate early, do not "watch and wait."
  • 💩 Chart every stool — lactulose is titrated to output. Too few = under-treated HE; too many watery = dehydration/AKI. The stool chart IS the drug chart here.
  • 🥄 Assess swallow before oral meds/food; a drowsy patient aspirates lactulose and diet. Nurse head-up, have suction ready, request SALT/NG if unsafe.
  • 🩸 Watch for ongoing bleeding: fresh melaena, haematemesis, rising HR, falling BP — the bleed feeds the encephalopathy.
  • 💧 Strict fluid balance; daily weights; flag over-diuresis (postural drop, rising creatinine, low urine output → HRS risk).
  • 🚫 Challenge any sedative prescribed to "settle" him — benzodiazepines/opioids deepen HE. Advocate for treating the cause, not sedating the sign.
⚔️ ONE-LINER RULE"The stool chart is the ammonia gauge and the GCS trend is the airway alarm. The nurse who reports ‘only one hard stool all shift’ or ‘GCS dropped to 8’ changes the whole plan."

🩻 Imaging / Labs + Visual Enhancements

👋 Asterixis — the sign you can "see" on a tremor trace

Asterixis is a held posture broken by sudden brief lapses of tone ("negative myoclonus") — a flapping tremor. It is a sign of grades I–III; it disappears in grade IV coma because the patient can no longer sustain the posture. Not specific to HE (also uraemia, CO₂ narcosis).

S12

📟 Monitoring

📡 What to watch and why

  • 🧠 GCS / West Haven grade hourly — the trend decides escalation. Grade III→IV means call anaesthetics for airway protection; do not sedate to control agitation.
  • 💩 Stool chart — titrate lactulose to 2–3 soft stools/day. No stool = under-treated; profuse watery = over-treated → dehydration/AKI/hypokalaemia → worse HE.
  • 🩸 Bleeding watch — serial Hb, obvious melaena/haematemesis, HR/BP; the bleed and the HE feed each other.
  • 🧪 U&E + electrolytes daily/twice-daily — Na⁺ (correct slowly), K⁺ (keep normal), creatinine (AKI/HRS), glucose.
  • 🌡️ Sepsis surveillance — cirrhotics mount blunted fevers; a soft rise or new confusion may be the only sign of recurrent infection.
⚔️ ONE-LINER RULE"Once you have decided ‘precipitated HE,’ the monitor's question changes from ‘what tipped him?’ to ‘is his airway about to go and is he still bleeding?’ — and the answers are the GCS trend and the stool/Hb charts."
S13

⚠️ Complications

💀 What can go wrong

  • 🫁 Aspiration pneumonia — the drowsy, flapping patient given oral lactulose/food with an unsafe swallow; a leading avoidable killer here.
  • 🧠 Progression to grade IV coma with loss of airway — needs ICU and intubation; do not let a benzo get you there.
  • 🩸 Ongoing / rebleeding varices — hypovolaemia, more blood-protein in the gut, deeper HE, AKI.
  • 💧 Hepatorenal syndrome / AKI — from the bleed, over-diuresis, SBP; worsens ammonia handling and prognosis.
  • 🧂 Iatrogenic: over-aggressive lactulose → dehydration/hypokalaemia/hypernatraemia (which worsen HE); too-rapid Na⁺ correction → osmotic demyelination.
  • 🎭 The system complication: premature "it is just his liver" labelling — the biggest complication of all, and the one that misses the bleed and the SBP.
⚔️ ONE-LINER RULE"The deadliest complication of HE is not the ammonia — it is the aspiration you allowed by feeding a grade-III patient and the precipitant you never looked for. Protect the airway, hunt the trigger."

🗺️ Plan + Procedure Guides with Checklists

🧭 The management plan

  1. 🧠 Diagnosis: overt HE (West Haven II–III) precipitated by SBP + variceal GI bleed, aggravated by constipation, hypokalaemia, hyponatraemia and new zopiclone. Multiple triggers, all being treated.
  2. 💊 Lower the ammonia: lactulose titrated to 2–3 soft stools/day (+ enemas while drowsy); add rifaximin given recurrent HE.
  3. 🔎 Remove the precipitants: antibiotics + IV albumin for SBP; terlipressin + antibiotics + urgent endoscopy for the bleed; replace K⁺/correct Na⁺ slowly; stop zopiclone; thiamine; treat AKI.
  4. 🫁 Protect the airway: nil-by-mouth until swallow safe / NG feed; if GCS falls toward grade IV → ICU + anaesthetics for intubation. Feed 1.2–1.5 g/kg/day protein — no restriction.
  5. 🏥 Disposition: monitored bed/HDU; hepatology + endoscopy; secondary prophylaxis (lactulose + rifaximin) and adherence/alcohol support on recovery; consider shunt work-up if recurrent.
⚔️ ONE-LINER RULE"HE is treated on two fronts at once: empty and acidify the gut, AND extinguish the precipitant. Doing only the first is why the ‘lactulose-only’ patient bounces back — or does not wake up."

🏠 Discharge

📋 Preventing the revolving door — two worlds of after-care

🧠 Secondary prevention of HE

  • Lactulose daily, self-titrated to 2–3 soft stools/day — with clear written instructions
  • Rifaximin 550 mg BD added after a recurrent episode (this patient qualifies)
  • Normal-protein diet (1.2–1.5 g/kg/day), avoid fasting, favour vegetable/dairy protein and a late-evening snack
  • Avoid sedatives/opioids; treat constipation promptly; recognise early confusion and reverse triggers at home

🫁 The whole cirrhotic package

  • Variceal secondary prophylaxis (non-selective beta-blocker ± banding programme)
  • SBP prophylaxis where indicated; diuretics for ascites reviewed and titrated
  • Alcohol abstinence support; nutrition; vaccination; HCC surveillance
  • Transplant assessment — an overt HE episode flags decompensation and prognosis
⚔️ ONE-LINER RULE"Discharge on lactulose ‘titrated to 2–3 soft stools,’ NOT a fixed dose — the fixed dose either does nothing or dehydrates. Add rifaximin for recurrence, keep the protein IN, and refer for transplant assessment."

💎 Clinical Pearls

🔎 Hunt the precipitantHE is a diagnosis of the trigger. Infection/SBP, GI bleed, constipation, dehydration/diuretics, electrolytes, sedatives, renal failure — find it, treat it.
🧠 Grade it clinicallyWest Haven 0–IV at the bedside. Asterixis in I–III, gone in IV coma. Ammonia supports but never grades.
🩸 Do the PR & the tapMelaena and SBP are the two most-missed precipitants. A tap (PMN ≥ 250) and a PR change the whole plan.
💩 Titrate to stoolsLactulose is dosed to 2–3 soft stools/day, not a fixed millilitre — too little fails, too much dehydrates and worsens HE.
🧫 Rifaximin for recurrenceAdd rifaximin 550 mg BD to lactulose for recurrent HE (Bass 2010) — not instead of it.
🍽️ Keep the protein INDo NOT protein-restrict. Feed 1.2–1.5 g/kg/day; over-restriction worsens muscle loss and outcomes.
🚫 No sedativesBenzodiazepines/opioids deepen HE and cost the airway. Treat the cause; never sedate a cirrhotic to "settle."
🧂 Fix the potassiumHypokalaemia drives renal ammoniagenesis and worsens HE — replace K⁺, correct Na⁺ slowly, check glucose.

🔬 Advanced Topics — Fellowship Level

🧠 Beyond the bundle

  • 🧬 Minimal / covert HE (MHE): normal bedside exam but impaired psychometrics (e.g. PHES, Stroop/EncephalApp, critical flicker frequency). It impairs driving and predicts overt HE — worth detecting and treating in selected patients.
  • 🩻 Portosystemic shunts & TIPS: HE occurs in a significant minority after TIPS; large spontaneous shunts can drive recurrent/refractory HE and may be amenable to shunt embolisation. Balance shunt reduction against rebleeding/ascites risk.
  • 💊 Beyond lactulose/rifaximin: L-ornithine L-aspartate (LOLA) supports ammonia detoxification (urea cycle/glutamine synthesis) and has RCT support; oral branched-chain amino acids improve HE and nutrition; albumin/plasma-exchange and faecal microbiota transplantation are areas of active study.
  • 🔁 Ammonia is not the whole story: systemic inflammation, infection, sarcopenia, manganese deposition, altered GABA-ergic neurotransmission and gut dysbiosis all modulate HE — which is why a "normal" ammonia never excludes it and why treating infection matters so much.
  • 🧪 Refractory HE work-up: if it is not improving, re-hunt occult infection, occult bleeding, a missed shunt, hyponatraemia, and consider non-convulsive status epilepticus (EEG) and structural mimics (CT/MRI).
  • 🫁 Prognosis & transplant: an episode of overt HE is a decompensation event with poor medium-term survival — it should trigger MELD-based transplant assessment, not just another lactulose script.

🎭 Morbidity & Mortality Conference

🎭 M&M — "Just Encephalopathy"

The case: A 62-year-old man with alcohol-related cirrhosis was brought in at 02:30, drowsy and confused. Triage wrote "known encephalopath — for lactulose." He was given lactulose and, when he became restless, a dose of lorazepam "to settle him" so the busy night team could review later. No PR was performed, no ascitic tap was done, and he was protein-restricted and left on the ward. Overnight he passed large volumes of melaena and spiked a fever; his abdomen was tense. By morning he was in grade IV coma. A PR and tap — finally done at the crash call — revealed a variceal GI bleed AND spontaneous bacterial peritonitis, the two precipitants nobody had looked for. He aspirated, arrested, and could not be resuscitated. The benzodiazepine had deepened the coma and taken his airway.

🧀 The Swiss-cheese holes that lined up:

🧠 ANCHORING"Known encephalopath" label stuck; the episode was treated as routine, not investigated.
🩸 NO PR / NO TAPThe GI bleed and the SBP — the actual triggers — were never sought.
💊 SEDATIONA benzodiazepine given to "settle" him deepened HE and lost the airway.
🍽️ WRONG DIETProtein-restricted against evidence — worsening catabolism, no benefit.
🫁 AIRWAYA grade-deepening patient left unmonitored on the ward until he aspirated.

🛡️ What would have saved him: treat every "known encephalopath" as a precipitant hunt — PR, ascitic tap, cultures, glucose and electrolytes on arrival; treat the SBP and the variceal bleed; NEVER sedate a cirrhotic to settle agitation (protect the airway with anaesthetics if needed); keep the protein IN; titrate lactulose to stools; and monitor the GCS trend closely. This is exactly the discipline that saved the patient in Bed 7.

⚔️ ONE-LINER RULE"‘Just encephalopathy’ has killed more cirrhotics than the encephalopathy itself. The label is not a diagnosis — the PR, the tap, and the precipitant you find are."
