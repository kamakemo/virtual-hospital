She says: "I cannot stop going… it is just water now, and my tummy is swollen and sore." A commode chart shows twelve watery stools since midnight. This is the exact moment C. difficile colitis is mislabelled "just a bug" — and the exact moment it starts sliding toward toxic megacolon.

  • 💊 Omeprazole (proton-pump inhibitor) started this admission — gastric acid suppression is an independent risk factor.

⚔️ ONE-LINER RULE"New diarrhoea in an inpatient who has had antibiotics is C. difficile until proven otherwise — do not blame the laxatives, the feed, or 'a bug going round' until you have sent a stool toxin."
🔥 Why the ward is where C. difficile thrivesC. difficile is the commonest cause of healthcare-associated infective diarrhoea. Its spores survive on surfaces for months and resist alcohol hand gel, so a single missed case seeds the whole bay. Roughly 1 in 5 treated patients relapse, and severe disease still carries an inpatient mortality of several percent. The whole game is early recognition, the right drug, and rigorous infection control — not reassurance.

🧭 The three questions that grade the danger at the bedside

Before any result comes back, three bedside questions triage severity: Is she septic? (fever, tachycardia, hypotension, confusion) · Is her abdomen benign or distending? (peritonism, tympany, absent bowel sounds → surgical territory) · Is she dry and shutting down her kidneys? (postural drop, poor urine output, rising creatinine). Any "yes" moves her from non-severe toward severe or fulminant disease (see S5 and S13).

⚠️ The trap of "she is opening her bowels, so nothing is obstructed"Toxic megacolon can co-exist with diarrhoea, and the transition is easy to miss on a busy ward. Serial abdominal examination + a low threshold for an abdominal X-ray (transverse colon > 6 cm = megacolon) is the safety net — not a single reassuring look.

👨‍⚕️ Resident Review

The medical resident thinks out loud at 09:52:

🧠 Frame it as "which severity band?", not just "does she have it?"

"High pre-test probability: recent co-amoxiclav, a PPI, elderly inpatient, profuse watery diarrhoea. My job is not only to confirm C. difficile — it is to grade the severity now and act before the result lands. She already has a WCC above 15 and an acute kidney injury, so she is severe. I will stop the co-amoxiclav and the PPI where I safely can, send a stool GDH plus toxin, isolate her, resuscitate with fluids, and start oral vancomycin. I will not give loperamide, and I will not wait for the lab to start treating a septic patient."

🎯 Her order of operations

  1. 🛑 Stop the offending antibiotic (co-amoxiclav) and review the PPI and any other non-essential drugs.
  2. 🧪 Send stool for the two-step test (GDH screen → toxin EIA / PCR); send only the loose stool, never a formed sample.
  3. 🧫 Bloods: FBC, U&E/creatinine, CRP, albumin, lactate, VBG — these define the severity band.
  4. 💊 Start oral vancomycin 125 mg QDS empirically in a septic, high-probability patient rather than waiting.
  5. 🚪 Isolate (side room), contact precautions, gloves and apron, soap-and-water hand hygiene; alert infection control.
  6. 💧 IV fluid resuscitation, correct electrolytes, and a low threshold for an abdominal X-ray if the abdomen distends.
⚔️ ONE-LINER RULE"Stop the culprit antibiotic, start oral vancomycin, isolate with soap and water, and resuscitate the fluids — four moves you can make before a single test returns."
🧪 Resident's self-testThe pharmacist suggests oral metronidazole because "that is what we always used." Is that the right first-line drug here?

No. Metronidazole has been demoted. Current guidance (NICE, IDSA/SHEA, ESCMID) makes oral vancomycin or fidaxomicin first-line for all initial episodes, severe or not. Metronidazole is reserved for non-severe disease only when neither of those is available, because it is less effective and repeated courses risk cumulative neurotoxicity. This woman is severe — she needs oral vancomycin (or fidaxomicin), not metronidazole.

🧠 Teaching Points + Interactive Simulation

🔬 The mechanism — WHY the antibiotics cause the colitis (the WHY behind the WHY)

  1. 🌿 Colonisation resistance lost: a healthy microbiome fills every niche and keeps C. difficile in check. A broad-spectrum antibiotic flattens the commensal flora, opening the door.
  2. 🌱 Spore germination: C. difficile survives as acid- and alcohol-resistant spores. With competitors gone (and bile-acid signalling altered), spores germinate into toxin-producing vegetative bacteria.
  3. ☠️ Toxins A and B: the pathogenic strains release toxin A (enterotoxin) and toxin B (cytotoxin), which glucosylate Rho GTPases → disrupt the actin cytoskeleton → tight junctions fail, cells die, and a neutrophil-rich inflammatory storm follows.
  4. 🟡 Pseudomembranes: sloughed epithelium, fibrin, mucus and neutrophils form the classic yellow-white plaques on the colonic mucosa — pseudomembranous colitis. Fluid pours into the lumen → profuse watery diarrhoea, protein loss and hypoalbuminaemia.
  5. 💀 The vicious cycle: transmural inflammation → colonic smooth-muscle paralysis → ileus and dilatation (toxic megacolon) → stasis lets toxin accumulate → more damage → risk of perforation and peritonitis.
⚔️ ONE-LINER RULE"C. difficile is a disease of a MISSING microbiome, not just a present bug. That is why the cure is to stop the antibiotic that emptied the garden — and why replacing the flora (FMT) beats yet another antibiotic when it keeps coming back."

⚖️ Contrast pair — Colonisation vs Infection

🦠 Active C. difficile infection

  • Diarrhoea (≥ 3 loose stools/day) PLUS a positive toxin
  • Systemic upset: fever, raised WCC, AKI
  • Treat: stop culprit antibiotic + oral vancomycin/fidaxomicin
  • Our patient

🤝 Asymptomatic colonisation

  • Positive GDH/PCR but NO diarrhoea, or formed stool
  • Toxin often negative — the organism is present but not causing disease
  • Do NOT treat — antibiotics here just harm and select resistance
  • Never test or treat a formed stool

💡 A positive PCR on a formed stool is not a diagnosis — it is a colonised patient. Testing has to be gated by the clinical picture, or you will treat people who do not have the disease and miss the ones who do.

📋 Orders

🖊️ The order set (09:55)

  • 🛑 STOP co-amoxiclav immediately; review and stop the PPI and any other non-essential agent; if an antibiotic is still truly needed, switch to the narrowest option.
  • 🧪 Stool sample for C. difficile — two-step: GDH (or PCR) screen then toxin EIA; only send loose/liquid stool.
  • 🧫 Bloods: FBC, U&E/creatinine, CRP, albumin, lactate, VBG, magnesium/potassium, blood cultures if septic.
  • 💊 Oral vancomycin 125 mg QDS (first-line); fidaxomicin 200 mg BD is an equal first-line alternative, especially if recurrence risk is high.
  • 💧 IV crystalloid resuscitation, replace K⁺/Mg²⁺, strict fluid-balance and stool chart.
  • 🚫 No antimotility agents (loperamide, codeine, opioids) — they promote toxin retention and toxic megacolon.
  • 🚪 Side-room isolation, contact precautions, gloves + apron, soap-and-water hand hygiene; dedicated equipment; alert infection prevention & control.
  • 🩻 Abdominal X-ray now (baseline colon diameter) and repeat if the abdomen distends; early surgical review if fulminant features appear.
⚔️ ONE-LINER RULE"The order set has one line that is easy to forget and lethal to omit: STOP the culprit antibiotic. Adding vancomycin while the co-amoxiclav runs is treating with the handbrake on."
S8

👩‍⚕️ Nursing Care

🛏️ Bedside priorities — the nurse runs the containment and the safety net

  • 🚪 Isolate in a side room the moment C. difficile is suspected — do not wait for the toxin result. Cohort only if no side room is available.
  • 🧼 Soap-and-water hand hygiene after every contact; alcohol gel does NOT kill spores. Gloves and apron for every entry; dedicated commode, thermometer and equipment.
  • 💩 Bristol Stool Chart + frequency chart — accurate typing (type 6–7) and counting is how the team tracks response and spots the ominous slowing of diarrhoea.
  • 🎈 Watch the abdomen — measure girth if distending, report new tenderness, tympany or absent bowel sounds urgently (evolving megacolon).
  • 💧 Strict fluid balance, monitor for dehydration and AKI, encourage oral intake, protect the skin (barrier cream) against corrosive stool.
  • 🧴 Chlorine-based (hypochlorite) cleaning of the room and surfaces daily and at discharge — spores need a sporicidal, not a routine wipe.
⚔️ ONE-LINER RULE"Soap and water, gloves and apron, a side room and bleach — the nurse at the door of the bay is the person who decides whether this is one case or an outbreak."

🔬 Stool / Imaging / Endoscopy + Visual Enhancements

🩻 The abdominal X-ray — read the colon diameter like a fuel gauge

The dilated transverse colon measures ≈ 8 cm (normal < 6 cm), with loss of haustra and thumbprinting from mucosal oedema. On a plain film the diagnosis of toxic megacolon lives in a single measured diameter.

🔎 The imaging & endoscopy picture in severe C. difficile

ModalityLook forMeaning
🩻 Plain abdominal X-rayTransverse colon > 6 cm, loss of haustra, thumbprinting, air–fluid levelsToxic megacolon / ileus — a surgical warning sign; repeat with clinical change.
💻 CT abdomen/pelvisDiffuse colonic wall thickening, "accordion" sign, pericolic fat stranding, ascites, free airBest for severity, complications and perforation; free air = emergency laparotomy.
🔬 Flexible sigmoidoscopy (limited, gentle)Yellow-white raised pseudomembranes on erythematous, friable mucosaDiagnostic when needed (e.g. ileus with no stool to test); AVOID full colonoscopy — perforation risk.

⚔️ ONE-LINER RULE"A colon wider than 6 cm on the film is a colon talking to the surgeons. Do not full-colonoscope a toxic megacolon — a gentle sigmoidoscopy at most, and only if it changes the plan."
⚠️ Pseudomembranes are the fingerprint, not the screening toolThe classic endoscopic pseudomembranes are pathognomonic, but endoscopy is invasive and risky in a fragile, dilated colon. Reserve it for when stool testing cannot be done or the diagnosis is genuinely in doubt — the toxin assay and the abdominal film do most of the work.

💊 Medications

💉 The C. difficile drug map — what, why, and the traps

DrugRoleMechanism / caution
Oral vancomycin 125 mg QDS ×10 daysFirst-line, all initial episodes (incl. severe)Glycopeptide, acts within the lumen (not absorbed) → high colonic levels. Use 500 mg QDS in fulminant disease.
Fidaxomicin 200 mg BD ×10 daysEqual first-line; preferred if high recurrence riskNarrow-spectrum macrocyclic; spares the microbiome → fewer recurrences than vancomycin.
Metronidazole (oral 400 mg TDS)Demoted — non-severe only, when vanc/fidaxomicin unavailableLess effective; cumulative neuropathy with repeated courses. IV route reserved for fulminant/ileus (adjunct only).
IV metronidazole (add-on)Fulminant disease WITH oral vancomycinReaches inflamed colon via blood when ileus limits luminal delivery; never the sole agent.
Bezlotoxumab (single IV infusion)Adjunct to reduce recurrence in high-risk patientsHuman monoclonal vs toxin B; give with (not instead of) antibiotics; caution in heart failure.

⚔️ ONE-LINER RULE"Oral vancomycin OR fidaxomicin first for everyone; metronidazole is the reserve, not the default; and in fulminant disease it is high-dose oral vancomycin PLUS IV metronidazole — because a paralysed colon cannot deliver an oral drug to where it is needed."
🚫 The 'never in acute C. difficile' list❌ loperamide / codeine / any antimotility · ❌ metronidazole as first-line for a fresh severe episode · ❌ full colonoscopy of a toxic megacolon · ❌ continuing the culprit antibiotic. Slowing the gut traps the toxin and precipitates megacolon.
S12

📟 Monitoring

📡 What to watch and why

  • 🌡️ Full NEWS2 observations 4-hourly (more if deteriorating) — fever, tachycardia and hypotension track sepsis and fluid loss.
  • 🧫 Daily WCC, creatinine, albumin, lactate, CRP — a rising WCC or lactate, or a falling albumin/BP, signals failure of medical therapy.
  • 🎈 Serial abdominal examination — new distension, tenderness, tympany or a fall in stool frequency with a sicker patient = evolving megacolon → repeat the abdominal film.
  • 💩 Stool chart — expect improvement (fewer, firmer stools) over 3–5 days; failure to improve by day 3–5 prompts review of dose/agent and imaging.
  • 💧 Strict fluid balance and electrolytes — profuse diarrhoea drives hypokalaemia and AKI.
  • 🔪 Trigger for surgical review: megacolon, perforation, rising lactate, shock, or clinical deterioration despite maximal medical therapy.
⚔️ ONE-LINER RULE"Once you have started treatment, the monitor's question changes from 'is this C. difficile?' to 'is this colon responding or is it about to perforate?' — and the answers are the white count, the lactate, and the abdomen."
S13

⚠️ Complications

💀 What can go wrong

  • 🎈 Toxic megacolon — colonic dilatation > 6 cm + systemic toxicity; the feared, life-threatening complication.
  • 🛑 Ileus — the "diarrhoea that stops"; toxin is retained, not cleared. A trap that looks like recovery.
  • 💥 Perforation & faecal peritonitis — free air on imaging; a surgical emergency with high mortality.
  • 🩸 Septic shock & multi-organ failure — hypotension, lactataemia, AKI.
  • 🔁 Recurrence — ~20–25% relapse after a first episode, higher after each subsequent one (see S20).
  • 🧂 Dehydration, hypokalaemia, hypoalbuminaemia — from massive fluid and protein loss.
  • 🏥 The system complication: onward transmission — an unrecognised or un-isolated case becomes a ward outbreak.
⚔️ ONE-LINER RULE"The deadliest turn in C. difficile is the colon that goes quiet. Falling stool output with a rising white count and a distending abdomen is toxic megacolon until the film says otherwise — call the surgeons early, not late."

🗺️ Plan + Procedure Guides with Checklists

🧭 The management plan

  1. 🦠 Diagnosis: severe C. difficile colitis (GDH+/toxin+, WCC ≥ 15, AKI, fever) with early signs of ileus — watch for fulminant conversion.
  2. 🛑 Stop the culprit co-amoxiclav and the PPI; oral vancomycin 125 mg QDS (or fidaxomicin); no antimotility drugs.
  3. 💧 Resuscitate: IV fluids, correct K⁺/Mg²⁺, monitor renal function and lactate.
  4. 🚪 Isolate, contact precautions, soap-and-water hygiene, chlorine cleaning, inform IPC.
  5. 🔪 Escalate to surgery if fulminant: high-dose oral vancomycin + IV metronidazole, and colectomy for megacolon/perforation/deterioration.
  6. 🔁 Plan for recurrence risk: consider fidaxomicin, bezlotoxumab, and FMT pathways if it comes back.

🏠 Discharge

📋 Two different discharges — the recovered case vs the recurrence-prone patient

🦠 This patient (recovered severe episode)

  • Complete the 10-day oral vancomycin course; do NOT re-test stool "for cure"
  • Antibiotic stewardship: avoid unnecessary future antibiotics and PPIs
  • Safety-net: return urgently if diarrhoea, fever or abdominal pain recur (recurrence ~20%)
  • GP letter flags the C. difficile history for any future prescriber

🔁 The recurrent / high-risk patient

  • First recurrence → fidaxomicin (or vancomycin taper/pulse)
  • Consider bezlotoxumab with the antibiotic to cut further relapse
  • Multiply-recurrent → refer for faecal microbiota transplant
  • Review and minimise all recurrence drivers (age, PPI, ongoing antibiotics)
⚔️ ONE-LINER RULE"A safe C. difficile discharge finishes the course, avoids the next needless antibiotic, and hands over a written safety-net and a recurrence plan — and it never wastes a repeat stool test to 'confirm cure.'"

💎 Clinical Pearls

🛑 Stop the culprit firstThe most powerful treatment is stopping the offending antibiotic — start vancomycin, but stop co-amoxiclav/clindamycin/cephalosporin/quinolone.
💊 Vanc or fidaxomicin, not metronidazoleOral vancomycin OR fidaxomicin is first-line for every episode; metronidazole is a demoted reserve.
🧴 Soap and water, not gelSpores resist alcohol — wash hands with soap and water and clean with chlorine.
🧪 Test loose stool onlyNever test formed stool and never re-test for cure — the toxin/PCR stays positive for weeks.
🧫 Five severity numbersWCC, creatinine, temperature, albumin, lactate — any red flag = severe.
🎈 The quiet colon killsDiarrhoea that stops with a sicker patient = ileus/toxic megacolon, not recovery.
🚫 No loperamideAntimotility agents trap toxin and precipitate megacolon — never in acute C. difficile.
🔁 Recurrence has answersFidaxomicin, bezlotoxumab and FMT — replace the missing microbiome when it keeps returning.

🔬 Advanced Topics — Fellowship Level

🧠 Beyond the pathway

  • 🧬 The hypervirulent NAP1/BI/027 strain: a fluoroquinolone-associated ribotype with binary toxin and a tcdC deletion causing toxin over-production — linked to more severe disease, more megacolon, and outbreaks. Ribotyping guides infection-control epidemiology.
  • 💩 Faecal microbiota transplant mechanics: restoring colonisation resistance (bile-acid metabolism, niche competition) cures > 85% of recurrent disease. Delivery via colonoscopy, NG tube, enema or oral encapsulated preparations; newer defined live biotherapeutics (e.g. fidaxomicin-sparing microbiome products such as SER-109 / RBX2660-type agents) are now licensed to prevent recurrence.
  • 🛡️ Bezlotoxumab nuance: anti-toxin-B monoclonal reduces recurrence but does not treat the acute episode; watch for heart-failure exacerbation in susceptible patients.
  • 🔁 Recurrence risk-stratification: age > 65, ongoing non-C.diff antibiotics, PPI use, immunosuppression, prior recurrence and severe index disease drive relapse — the group where fidaxomicin ± bezlotoxumab pays off.
  • 💊 Vancomycin taper/pulse and "chaser" strategies: extended tapering regimens, and a vancomycin/fidaxomicin course to cover a subsequent unavoidable systemic antibiotic, reduce recurrence.
  • 🧫 Diagnostic stewardship: PCR-only testing over-diagnoses colonisation; a two-step GDH→toxin (or PCR + toxin) algorithm plus "test only unformed stool, only when clinically indicated" reduces false labelling and unnecessary treatment.
  • 🔪 Surgical options in fulminant disease: subtotal colectomy with end ileostomy is standard; the colon-sparing diverting loop ileostomy with antegrade vancomycin lavage is an alternative in selected patients.

🎭 Morbidity & Mortality Conference

🎭 M&M — "The Loperamide and the Lost Colon"

The case: A 76-year-old man on a surgical ward developed profuse diarrhoea after a week of cephalosporins and a PPI. The team was reassured when, overnight, his diarrhoea "settled" — and a night doctor, wanting him to rest, prescribed loperamide. Nobody isolated him; staff used the alcohol gel at the door because the bay was busy. By morning his abdomen was tense and silent, his stool output near zero, and his white count had jumped to 28. The abdominal film showed a 9 cm toxic megacolon. He went to theatre for an emergency subtotal colectomy and spent two weeks in intensive care — and over the following fortnight three more patients in the same bay developed C. difficile.

🧀 The Swiss-cheese holes that lined up:

🚫 ANTIMOTILITYLoperamide slowed a toxin-laden colon → toxin retention → toxic megacolon.
🎈 "SETTLING" MISREADFalling diarrhoea with a sicker patient was ileus, mistaken for recovery.
🧴 ALCOHOL GELGel does not kill spores; no soap-and-water, no chlorine cleaning → onward spread.
🚪 NO ISOLATIONNever side-roomed → the bay became an outbreak of four cases.
🛑 CULPRIT CONTINUEDThe cephalosporin and PPI ran on while the gut flora stayed flattened.

🛡️ What would have saved him (and the bay): never give antimotility drugs in acute C. difficile; read a falling stool output with a rising white count and a distending abdomen as toxic megacolon, not recovery; isolate on suspicion with soap-and-water hygiene and chlorine cleaning; stop the culprit antibiotic and PPI; start oral vancomycin early; and image the abdomen the moment it distends. This is exactly the discipline that saved the patient in Bed 6.

⚔️ ONE-LINER RULE"'The diarrhoea settled' has killed more colons than it has ever cured. In C. difficile a quiet gut can mean a paralysing one — and a squirt of alcohol gel is how one case becomes an outbreak."
