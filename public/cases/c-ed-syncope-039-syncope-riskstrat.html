🔥 Why syncope is the ED's quiet trapSyncope is ~1–3% of ED visits. Most are benign reflex faints — but a small, dangerous minority are the first (and sometimes only) warning of a lethal arrhythmia or structural heart disease. The whole task is risk stratification: reliably separating the benign faint from the sentinel event, using the story, the ECG, and a validated tool — not gestalt "he looks well."

🧭 Define it precisely first — is this even syncope?

Syncope = transient loss of consciousness from global cerebral hypoperfusion, with rapid onset, short duration, and spontaneous complete recovery. Confirm all four features; then separate it from its mimics: seizure, hypoglycaemia, intoxication, psychogenic pseudo-syncope, and "collapse without LOC" (mechanical fall, vertigo, drop attack). Getting the category right is half the diagnosis.

⚠️ The trap of "he looks well now"By definition every syncope patient has completely recovered — looking well is the natural history of the condition, not evidence of safety. The dangerous ones look identical to the benign ones between events. Risk lives in the circumstances, the ECG and the history, never in the well-looking patient in front of you.

🎯 Her order of operations

  1. 📈 12-lead ECG, reviewed line by line for red flags (QT, pre-excitation, Brugada, LVH/HCM, AV block, arrhythmia, ischaemia).
  2. 📟 Continuous cardiac monitoring while in the department — the arrhythmia may recur in front of you.
  3. 🧮 Canadian Syncope Risk Score to place him in a 30-day serious-outcome band (S5).
  4. 🩺 Lying & standing BP, focused cardiac exam (murmurs, Valsalva), and a targeted history for structural/inherited disease.
  5. 🚫 No routine CT head or EEG — low yield in uncomplicated syncope; reserve for focal neurology, head injury on anticoagulation, or true seizure features.
⚔️ ONE-LINER RULE"No prodrome + palpitations + family history of sudden death = admit and monitor, don't reassure. The reassuring young face is the trap."
🧪 Resident's self-testThe patient is well and the CT scanner is free — should she order a CT head "just to be safe"?

No. In uncomplicated syncope with no focal neurology and no head-injury red flags, routine CT head changes management in well under 5% and mostly finds nothing relevant — it is low-yield and irradiating. A CT head almost never explains a faint. The high-yield test is the ECG, followed by monitored risk stratification. Reserve neuroimaging for focal deficit, persistent altered consciousness, seizure, or significant head trauma (esp. on anticoagulation).

🧠 Teaching Points + Interactive Simulation

🔬 The mechanism — WHY consciousness is lost (the WHY behind the WHY)

  1. 🧠 Common final pathway: a transient drop in global cerebral perfusion below the threshold needed for consciousness (~6–8 seconds of arrest, or a fall in systolic BP to ~50–60 mmHg). Reperfusion → rapid, complete recovery. That is the fingerprint of syncope.
  2. 🔄 Reflex (neurally-mediated): an inappropriate autonomic reflex → vasodilatation (vasodepressor) and/or bradycardia (cardioinhibitory). Vasovagal (emotion, pain, prolonged standing), situational (cough, micturition, defecation), carotid sinus. Benign — but can injure.
  3. 🧍 Orthostatic: failure to defend BP on standing — volume depletion, drugs (antihypertensives, diuretics, alpha-blockers), autonomic failure. Reproduced by a lying/standing BP drop.
  4. 🫀 Cardiac — the dangerous one: a sudden fall in cardiac output. Arrhythmic (VT/VF, long QT, Brugada, WPW, complete heart block, sick sinus) or structural/obstructive (HCM outflow obstruction, severe aortic stenosis, massive PE, tamponade, dissection). This is the group with real mortality.
⚔️ ONE-LINER RULE"Reflex and orthostatic syncope inconvenience; cardiac syncope kills. The entire ED workup exists to find the small cardiac group hiding in a crowd of benign faints."

⚖️ Contrast pair — Cardiac vs Vasovagal syncope

🫀 Cardiac syncope (dangerous)

  • Trigger: exertion or occurs supine
  • No/short prodrome — sudden "off"; palpitations first
  • Structural heart disease / low EF; abnormal ECG
  • Family history of sudden death / channelopathy
  • → Admit & monitor. Can kill.

🥵 Vasovagal syncope (benign)

  • Trigger: emotion, pain, prolonged standing, heat
  • Prodrome of warmth, nausea, tunnel vision
  • Upright at onset; slow recovery with fatigue
  • Structurally normal heart, normal ECG
  • → Reassure & educate. Benign.

💡 And the other classic pair — syncope vs seizure: seizure suggests post-ictal confusion, lateral tongue-biting and incontinence; but note brief myoclonic jerks (convulsive syncope) occur in true faints, so jerks alone do not make it a seizure.

📋 Orders

🖊️ The order set (16:50)

  • 📈 12-lead ECG now, reviewed for every red flag; repeat if symptoms recur; add rhythm strip.
  • 📟 Continuous cardiac monitoring (telemetry) while in the department — catch the recurring arrhythmia live.
  • 🩺 Lying & standing BP (supine, then at 1 & 3 min standing) to test for orthostatic drop.
  • 🧪 Targeted bloods: capillary glucose (exclude hypoglycaemia), FBC (anaemia/GI bleed as a cause), U&E; troponin & β-hCG / D-dimer only if the story points that way (not routine).
  • 🧮 Canadian Syncope Risk Score documented with disposition rationale.
  • 🫀 Bedside echo / formal echo if structural disease suspected (murmur, abnormal ECG, exertional syncope).
  • 🚫 No routine CT head / EEG / carotid Dopplers — order only if focal neurology, seizure, or head-injury red flags.
  • 📞 Early cardiology referral for exertional syncope + abnormal ECG; discuss admission vs monitored observation.
⚔️ ONE-LINER RULE"An ECG and a lying-standing BP cost pennies and change the whole risk category; a reflex CT head costs money, radiation and false leads — and almost never explains a faint."
S8

👩‍⚕️ Nursing Care

🛏️ The nurse runs the safety net

  • 📟 Put him on the monitor and keep him on it — a well-looking young man who "wants to go" is exactly who slips off telemetry and arrests unwitnessed.
  • 📈 Capture a 12-lead early and label the time; grab a rhythm strip during any palpitations — the transient arrhythmia is the diagnosis.
  • 🩺 Perform the lying & standing BP carefully (supine 5 min, then 1 & 3 min standing) and record symptoms with the numbers.
  • 🍬 Check a bedside glucose and a set of obs; reassess if he feels faint again — and lie him flat with legs raised if pre-syncopal.
  • 🧾 Do not facilitate self-discharge of a high-risk syncope; escalate to the treating clinician and document the conversation and capacity.
  • 😌 Keep him informed — "you look well, but the way you collapsed needs a heart check first" — engagement prevents the walk-out.
⚔️ ONE-LINER RULE"In syncope, the nurse who keeps the well-looking young athlete on the monitor is preventing the unwitnessed arrest nobody would have seen coming."

🩻 Adjunct imaging

  • Echocardiography: confirms HCM (septal thickness, LVOT gradient, SAM), aortic stenosis, RV/LV impairment, ARVC features — mandatory when the ECG or exam is abnormal or syncope is exertional.
  • Ambulatory / prolonged monitoring: Holter, external loop or an implantable loop recorder (ILR) for infrequent unexplained syncope with suspected arrhythmia.

💊 Medications

💉 Syncope is a diagnostic problem — the "drugs" are mostly about what to STOP

ConsiderationRoleMechanism / caution
Review culprit drugsEvery syncopeAntihypertensives, diuretics, alpha-blockers, nitrates → orthostatic syncope; QT-prolonging drugs (antipsychotics, macrolides, some antiemetics) → torsades. The best "treatment" is often deprescribing.
Correct electrolytesIf long QT / arrhythmiaKeep K⁺ and Mg²⁺ replete; IV magnesium is first-line for torsades.
Beta-blockersSelected LQTS, catecholaminergic VT (CPVT), some HCMBlunt catecholamine-driven arrhythmia — but specialist-directed; wrong in Brugada/brady syncope.
Avoid QT-prolongersKnown/suspected LQTSGive patients a "drugs to avoid" list (see CredibleMeds); a single wrong prescription can trigger torsades.

🚫 Never do this❌ Don't start a QT-prolonging antiemetic in a long-QT patient · ❌ don't treat exertional syncope with reassurance and a beta-blocker without imaging · ❌ don't discharge a high-risk cardiac syncope on any drug in place of monitoring and cardiology review.
S12

📟 Monitoring

📡 What to watch and why

  • 📟 Continuous telemetry in the ED — the whole point of admitting/observing high-risk syncope is to capture the arrhythmia that caused it if it recurs.
  • 📈 Repeat the 12-lead / grab a rhythm strip with any palpitations, dizziness or recurrence — the transient rhythm is the diagnosis.
  • 🩺 Recheck lying/standing BP if orthostatic mechanism suspected, especially after fluids or drug changes.
  • 🧭 Escalate monitoring intensity to the pre-test probability: telemetry inpatient → Holter → external loop → implantable loop recorder for infrequent but suspicious events.
  • 📋 Re-score / reassess disposition as new data arrive (troponin, echo, cardiology opinion) rather than locking in the first impression.
⚔️ ONE-LINER RULE"Once you suspect cardiac syncope, the monitor's job is to be watching the second it happens again — because next time the rhythm may not self-terminate."
S13

⚠️ Complications

💀 What can go wrong

  • ⚡ Sudden cardiac death — the recurrence of a malignant arrhythmia (VT/VF) that does not self-terminate: the outcome the whole workup exists to prevent.
  • 🩹 Trauma from the collapse — head injury, fractures, dental/facial injury; unheralded syncope (no prodrome) carries the worst injury risk.
  • 🚗 Syncope at the wheel / at height — recurrence while driving or operating machinery harms the patient and others; hence driving restrictions.
  • 🫀 Progression of the underlying disease — untreated AS, HCM, or high-grade block declaring catastrophically.
  • 🩻 Iatrogenic harm from over-testing — CT radiation, incidentalomas, false-positive EEGs sending you down neurological rabbit holes.
  • 🏥 The system complication: premature or self-discharge of a high-risk patient — the deadliest and most preventable of all.
⚔️ ONE-LINER RULE"The worst complication of syncope is the one that happens after discharge, to a patient you called 'a simple faint.' Treat disposition as the highest-stakes decision in the case."

🧭 The disposition plan

  1. 🫀 Assessment: high-risk syncope — exertional, no prodrome, palpitations, family history of sudden death, abnormal ECG (LVH voltage + repolarisation change ± long QT). CSRS medium–high.

🔬 Advanced Topics — Fellowship Level

🧠 Beyond the pathway

  • 🧬 Channelopathies on the ECG: Long QT (LQTS) — genotype–phenotype (LQT1 exertional/swimming, LQT2 auditory/emotional, LQT3 at rest); Brugada — spontaneous vs drug-induced type 1, fever-provoked VF, sodium-channel testing; CPVT — normal resting ECG but exercise/emotion-induced bidirectional VT; short QT. Syncope in these patients is a sentinel for VF.
  • 🧱 Cardiomyopathies: HCM (dynamic LVOT obstruction, SAM, exertional syncope, ICD risk score) and ARVC (fibro-fatty RV replacement, epsilon waves, exercise-induced VT) — the classic young-athlete sudden-death substrates.
  • 🫁 Structural causes hiding as "syncope": severe aortic stenosis (exertional syncope triad with angina/dyspnoea), massive pulmonary embolism (syncope with RV strain/hypotension), aortic dissection and tamponade — always ask whether the faint is the tip of a catastrophe.
  • ⚙️ Mechanisms, precisely: distinguishing reflex (cardioinhibitory vs vasodepressor vs mixed), orthostatic (neurogenic vs volume/drug), and cardiac (arrhythmic vs obstructive) — because the mechanism dictates the therapy.
  • 🛌 Tilt-table testing: a tool for suspected reflex syncope or to demonstrate a susceptibility, not a rule-out — a negative tilt never excludes cardiac syncope, and a positive tilt can mislead in a patient who actually has an arrhythmia.
  • 📼 Implantable loop recorders (ILR): the highest-yield strategy for infrequent, unexplained syncope with suspected arrhythmia — long monitoring windows capture the rare culprit rhythm that Holters miss.
  • 🚗 Driving & occupation: apply the relevant regulator's syncope rules (e.g. DVLA/DMV categories for single vs recurrent, reflex vs cardiac, treated vs untreated, private vs vocational licence) and document the advice — a medico-legal essential.

🎭 Morbidity & Mortality Conference

🎭 M&M — "The Vasovagal That Wasn't"

The case: A 19-year-old semi-professional footballer collapsed during a match, out for a few seconds, then "came round quickly" and felt fine. In a busy ED he was seen briefly, told it was a vasovagal faint from "exertion and dehydration," and sent home without an ECG — reassured, told to drink more and rest. His teammates mentioned a relative who had "died young of a heart problem"; it wasn't recorded. Two days later, at the next training session, he collapsed again and could not be resuscitated. Autopsy showed hypertrophic cardiomyopathy. The resting ECG he never had that day would have shown the LVH voltages and deep repolarisation changes.

🧀 The Swiss-cheese holes that lined up:

🧠 ANCHORING"Young and fit = benign faint"; exertional trigger reframed as dehydration.
📈 NO ECGThe single highest-yield test in syncope was never done or read.
🏃 RED FLAG IGNOREDSyncope DURING exertion — the loudest alarm — was dismissed.
👨‍👦 FAMILY HISTORYSudden death in a young relative was mentioned but not acted on.
🚪 EASY DISCHARGEWell-looking recovery mistaken for safety; no monitoring, no cardiology.

🛡️ What would have saved him: treat every exertional syncope as cardiac until proven otherwise; get and read an ECG before any discharge; take a family history of sudden death seriously; keep high-risk syncope monitored and refer to cardiology; and never let a well-looking recovery substitute for risk stratification. This is exactly the discipline that saved the patient in Bed 9.

⚔️ ONE-LINER RULE"'It was just a vasovagal faint' has buried more young athletes than any arrhythmia — because it closes the chart before the ECG is ever read. Exertional syncope earns a heart workup, not a reassurance."
