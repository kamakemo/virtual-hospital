"This is typical stable angina with several risk factors. I'm not going to reflexively send him for angiography. 🎯 Her order of operations

  1. 🖥️ Estimate pre-test probability (age, sex, symptom typicality) — recent ESC estimates are notably LOWER than the old Diamond-Forrester figures (S5).
  2. 🩻 Choose the test: CTCA first-line for low–intermediate probability (superb rule-out); functional/ischaemia imaging (stress echo, perfusion MRI/PET) for higher probability or known disease.
  3. 💊 Start OMT now: high-intensity statin, aspirin, a beta-blocker or CCB for symptoms, GTN for breakthrough, and treat BP/lipids/glucose/smoking.
  4. 🧾 Baseline: resting ECG, HbA1c, lipid profile, U&E, FBC, TFTs, and an echo (LV function, valves).
  5. 🩺 Reserve invasive angiography ± revascularisation for refractory symptoms on OMT or high-risk anatomy/large ischaemic burden.
⚔️ ONE-LINER RULE"In stable angina, optimal medical therapy comes FIRST — a stent relieves symptoms but does not save the stable patient's life (ISCHEMIA). Treat everyone; catheterise the few who need it."
🧪 Registrar's self-testThe patient asks: "If there's a blockage, shouldn't we just stent it to stop me having a heart attack?" What's the honest answer?

No — not in stable disease. In stable coronary disease, elective PCI relieves angina but does not reduce death or spontaneous MI compared with optimal medical therapy (ISCHEMIA, COURAGE), and much of its symptom benefit is placebo (ORBITA). Heart attacks arise mostly from non-flow-limiting plaques that rupture — which is why plaque-stabilising therapy (statins, antiplatelets, risk-factor control), not stenting a stenosis, is what changes prognosis. Revascularisation is for symptom relief when OMT fails, or for genuinely high-risk anatomy.

🎓 Consultant Round + Evidence-Based Medicine

The consultant cardiologist at the desk, 11:05:

🧮 Pre-test probability — the gate to sensible testing

The 2019/2024 ESC chronic coronary syndrome guidance replaced the old, inflated Diamond-Forrester probabilities with substantially lower estimates — recognising that we had been over-testing and over-diagnosing. Probability is driven by age, sex and symptom typicality, then refined by risk factors, resting ECG, LV function and coronary calcium.

Clinical pre-test probabilityRecommended approach
Very low (< ~5%)No routine testing — look for non-cardiac causes.
Low–intermediate (~5–15%)Consider deferring testing; if testing, CTCA excels (very high NPV rules disease out).
Intermediate (~15–50%)CTCA or non-invasive functional imaging, guided by local expertise and the clinical question.
High (> ~50–85%)Functional/ischaemia imaging preferred; consider invasive angiography (with physiology) if symptoms are severe or high-risk features.

Our patient — 60, male, typical angina, several risk factors — sits at the intermediate–high end. CTCA (to define anatomy and rule out) or functional imaging (to quantify ischaemia) are both defensible; either way, OMT starts today.

⚔️ ONE-LINER RULE"Rule out coronary disease in the low-risk clinic patient with a CT coronary angiogram — its negative predictive value is its superpower. Reserve invasive angiography for those with high probability, refractory symptoms, or high-risk anatomy."

🧠 Teaching Points + Interactive Simulation

🔬 The mechanism — WHY exertional angina, WHY it's stable (the WHY behind the WHY)

  1. 🧱 Fixed obstructive stenosis: a stable, fibrous atheromatous plaque narrows an epicardial artery. At rest, autoregulation keeps distal flow adequate — no symptoms.
  2. 📉 Limited coronary flow reserve: a stenosis becomes flow-limiting once it exceeds ~70% diameter (or is physiologically significant by FFR ≤ 0.80). The vessel can no longer augment flow on demand.
  3. 🏃 Demand outstrips supply on exertion: exercise raises heart rate, contractility and wall stress → myocardial oxygen demand rises → the stenotic territory becomes ischaemic. Anaerobic metabolites (adenosine, lactate) stimulate cardiac afferents → predictable, exertional, referred angina.
  4. 😌 Rest restores balance: stop exerting → demand falls → ischaemia resolves within minutes. GTN venodilates (↓preload, ↓wall stress) and dilates coronaries, hastening relief. This reversibility is why it is stable.
  5. 💥 Why MI is a different event: most infarcts arise from rupture of a non-flow-limiting, lipid-rich plaque — not the tight stenosis causing the angina. That is why stenting the stenosis relieves symptoms but does not prevent the future MI; plaque stabilisation does.
⚔️ ONE-LINER RULE"The plaque that gives you angina is rarely the plaque that gives you the heart attack. Stents fix the flow-limiter you feel; statins stabilise the vulnerable plaque you don't — which is why prevention, not PCI, saves the stable patient."

⚖️ Contrast pair — Chronic coronary syndrome vs Acute coronary syndrome

🟢 Chronic coronary syndrome (stable angina)

  • Predictable, exertional, relieved by rest/GTN, unchanged over weeks–months
  • Fixed stenosis, stable plaque, reversible demand ischaemia
  • Outpatient work-up; OMT + risk-factor control FIRST; revascularise selectively

🔴 Acute coronary syndrome

  • New, crescendo, at-rest, or prolonged pain — pattern has CHANGED
  • Plaque rupture + thrombus; supply suddenly falls
  • Emergency: ECG, troponin, antithrombotics, urgent/emergent reperfusion

💡 The same coronary tree, two utterly different urgencies. The discriminator is the pattern: predictable and stable → clinic; new/crescendo/at rest → emergency.

⚖️ Contrast pair — Obstructive epicardial disease vs INOCA/ANOCA

🩸 Obstructive epicardial CAD

  • Flow-limiting stenosis on CTCA/angiography
  • Anti-anginals + secondary prevention; revascularise if refractory/high-risk

🔬 INOCA / ANOCA (clean-ish coronaries)

  • Angina with non-obstructive coronaries — microvascular dysfunction or vasospasm
  • Diagnose with coronary function testing (CFR, acetylcholine provocation); treat the endotype

💡 "Clean coronaries" on angiography is not "nothing wrong." Up to half of patients with angina and no obstructive disease have microvascular or vasospastic disease — real, treatable, and prognostically important (S20).

📋 Orders

🖊️ The clinic order set (11:15)

  • 📈 Resting 12-lead ECG (baseline; look for prior MI, LVH, arrhythmia, conduction disease — often normal in stable angina).
  • 🩸 Bloods: FBC (anaemia), U&E, HbA1c/fasting glucose, fasting lipid profile, TFTs, LFTs (statin baseline), and consider Lp(a) once.
  • 🖥️ Coronary CT angiography (first-line for intermediate probability) ± coronary artery calcium score; or functional imaging (stress echo / perfusion MRI / MPI) if higher probability or known disease.
  • 🫀 Transthoracic echo — LV systolic function, wall-motion, valves (exclude aortic stenosis / HCM mimicking angina).
  • 💊 Start OMT today (do not wait for imaging): high-intensity statin, aspirin 75 mg, a first-line anti-anginal (beta-blocker or CCB), GTN spray + how-to-use counselling.
  • 🩺 Risk-factor bundle: BP target, glucose optimisation, smoking-cessation reinforcement, diet/exercise/weight, influenza vaccination.
  • 🚫 Do NOT reflexively list for invasive angiography/PCI — defer that decision to response-to-OMT and imaging-defined anatomy.
⚔️ ONE-LINER RULE"A statin, an antiplatelet, an anti-anginal and a risk-factor conversation cost pennies and change prognosis; the expensive, lower-value reflex is booking the cath lab before you've tried any of them."

👩‍⚕️ Nursing Care

🛏️ Clinic nursing priorities — the nurse builds the self-management

  • 💨 GTN teaching: sit down, one spray/tablet under the tongue, repeat after 5 min; if pain persists > 10–15 min after a second dose (or is severe/at rest) → call emergency services — this may be an ACS. Warn about headache/flushing/postural drop.
  • 📒 Symptom diary: record what brings on angina, how often, and how it responds — the trend guides anti-anginal escalation and flags any crescendo pattern.
  • 💊 Adherence & side-effects: reinforce that the statin and antiplatelet are the prognosis-changers; check for statin myalgia, and set expectations honestly to prevent nocebo discontinuation.
  • 🩸 Baseline observations, weight, BMI, smoking status; draw the fasting bloods and book the imaging.
  • 🏃 Cardiac rehabilitation / lifestyle referral: structured exercise reduces angina and events; support smoking cessation and diet.
  • 📞 Red-flag safety-net: teach the patient exactly when stable angina has become unstable (below) — the single most valuable thing a clinic nurse can hand over.
⚔️ ONE-LINER RULE"The clinic nurse who teaches a patient WHEN their angina stops being stable — new, at rest, or not settling with GTN — has installed the alarm that catches the next heart attack early."

📈 ECG / Imaging + Visual Enhancements

🫀 The exertional ischaemia concept — read it as a supply–demand curve

At rest the ST segment is on the baseline (green). On exertion, the ischaemic territory produces horizontal/down-sloping ST depression that resolves with rest — the electrical signature of reversible demand ischaemia, and why provocation testing (not a resting trace) makes the diagnosis.

💊 Medications

💉 The two pillars — EVENT reduction vs SYMPTOM reduction

🛡️ Pillar 1 — Reduce events (prognosis)

  • High-intensity statin (atorvastatin 80 / rosuvastatin 20) — LDL target < 1.4 mmol/L & ≥50% ↓; add ezetimibe ± PCSK9i if not at goal
  • Antiplatelet — aspirin 75 mg (clopidogrel if aspirin-intolerant)
  • ACE inhibitor / ARB — if hypertension, diabetes, HF, or LV dysfunction
  • Risk-factor control — BP, glucose, smoking cessation, weight/diet/exercise

💓 Pillar 2 — Reduce symptoms (anti-anginals)

  • First-line: beta-blocker OR calcium-channel blocker (CCB)
  • Add-on / second-line: long-acting nitrate, ivabradine, ranolazine, nicorandil
  • Short-acting GTN for acute episodes & pre-emptive use before exertion

📟 Monitoring

📡 What to track in ongoing chronic coronary syndrome

  • 💓 Symptom burden (CCS class) & GTN use — the primary gauge of whether anti-anginal therapy needs escalation, or whether refractoriness now warrants revascularisation.
  • 🧪 Lipids to target — recheck ~6–8 weeks after starting/changing statin; aim LDL < 1.4 mmol/L (and ≥50% reduction); add ezetimibe/PCSK9i if short.
  • 🩸 BP and glucose/HbA1c — treat to guideline targets; each is a lever on both symptoms and events.
  • 🧬 Statin safety — baseline and on-treatment ALT; check CK only if myalgia; distinguish true intolerance from nocebo.
  • 🫀 LV function / new signs — reassess echo if symptoms change, new murmur, or features of heart failure develop.
  • 🚩 Pattern surveillance — the single most important "monitor": any shift to rest pain, crescendo frequency, or lower-threshold angina = suspected ACS → urgent pathway.
⚔️ ONE-LINER RULE"In chronic coronary syndrome the two dials you watch are SYMPTOMS (drives anti-anginal escalation and the revascularisation conversation) and RISK FACTORS to target (drives prognosis) — and you never stop watching for the pattern that turns stable into acute."
S13

⚠️ Complications

💀 What can go wrong — in the disease and in over-treating it

  • 🔴 Progression to acute coronary syndrome — plaque rupture → unstable angina/MI; the reason risk-factor control and plaque stabilisation matter more than the stenosis.
  • 💔 Ischaemic cardiomyopathy / heart failure — chronic or repeated ischaemia and prior infarction reduce LV function.
  • ⚡ Arrhythmia / sudden cardiac death — ischaemic substrate can generate ventricular arrhythmia.
  • 💊 Refractory angina — symptoms persisting despite maximal OMT and revascularisation; needs specialist add-on therapies (S20).
  • 🩹 Procedural harm from unnecessary revascularisation — the "complication of over-treatment": peri-procedural MI, bleeding, stroke, contrast nephropathy, stent thrombosis/restenosis — real risks incurred for no prognostic gain when PCI is done reflexively in stable disease (ISCHEMIA/ORBITA).
  • 🧠 Nocebo statin discontinuation — a "complication" of poor counselling; stopping the statin forfeits the largest prognostic benefit on offer.
⚔️ ONE-LINER RULE"In stable coronary disease there are two ways to harm a patient: under-treat the risk factors, or over-treat the stenosis. The reflex stent trades a real procedural risk for a prognostic benefit that isn't there."

A 60-year-old with 6 months of typical, predictable exertional angina (CCS II), untreated dyslipidaemia and several risk factors, is referred asking for "angiography and a stent." He is medication-naive beyond aspirin and GTN, pain-free in clinic. What is your first move?
S15

🗺️ Plan + Procedure Guides with Checklists

🧭 The management plan

  1. 🫀 Diagnosis: chronic coronary syndrome — typical stable angina, CTCA moderate proximal LAD lesion (CT-FFR 0.84, not flow-limiting), preserved LV function.
  2. 🛡️ Secondary prevention (all patients): high-intensity statin to target, aspirin, ACEi (BP/risk), aggressive risk-factor control, cardiac rehab.
  3. 💓 Anti-anginal therapy: first-line beta-blocker (or CCB), escalate by symptoms; GTN for breakthrough.
  4. 🕰️ Trial OMT, then reassess at 6 weeks: if symptoms well-controlled → continue; if refractory despite optimised OMT → discuss invasive angiography ± revascularisation.
  5. 🩺 Revascularisation is reserved for: symptoms refractory to OMT, OR high-risk anatomy — significant left main, proximal LAD, multivessel disease with large ischaemic burden, or reduced LV function — where it confers prognostic benefit.
⚔️ ONE-LINER RULE"Reserve invasive angiography and revascularisation for refractory symptoms or high-risk anatomy (left main, proximal LAD, multivessel + large ischaemia, or LV dysfunction) — everyone else is treated, and improved, medically first."

🏠 Discharge

📋 Two different worlds — the OMT-managed stable patient vs the post-revascularisation patient

🟢 This patient (OMT-managed CCS)

  • High-intensity statin to LDL target, aspirin, ACEi, anti-anginal; GTN with technique taught
  • Cardiac rehab, exercise, diet, weight, glucose & BP control; smoking stays quit
  • Symptom diary; clear plan to escalate anti-anginals or reconsider angiography if refractory
  • Red flags: new/crescendo pain, rest pain, or pain not settling with GTN → emergency

🩺 If later revascularised (PCI/CABG)

  • DAPT duration per procedure/bleeding risk, then single antiplatelet; statin continues lifelong
  • Same secondary prevention — revascularisation does NOT replace risk-factor control
  • Structured follow-up for symptom recurrence/restenosis; cardiac rehab
⚔️ ONE-LINER RULE"Whether or not a stable patient is ever revascularised, the prescription that changes their prognosis is the same — statin to target, antiplatelet, and aggressive risk-factor control. The stent is optional; the prevention is not."
🚗 Safety-netting the patternGive explicit, written instruction: seek emergency care if angina becomes more frequent, comes on at rest or with minimal effort, lasts longer, or does not settle after two doses of GTN 5 minutes apart. That single instruction is what converts a missed MI into a treated one.

💎 Clinical Pearls

🕰️ OMT comes firstIn stable angina, optimal medical therapy + risk-factor control precede any stent — ISCHEMIA/COURAGE: no mortality benefit from routine revascularisation.
💊 Two pillarsEvents (statin + antiplatelet + risk control) vs symptoms (anti-anginals). Everyone gets pillar 1; escalate pillar 2 by symptoms.
🖥️ CTCA's superpowerFirst-line for low–intermediate probability; its negative predictive value rules disease out (SCOT-HEART).
🧱 Wrong plaqueThe stenosis you feel isn't usually the plaque that ruptures — stabilise plaques, don't just widen lumens.
🔬 Clean coronaries ≠ nothingAngina + non-obstructive coronaries → think INOCA/ANOCA (microvascular/vasospastic); test coronary function.
🚩 Stable can turn acuteNew, crescendo, or rest angina is ACS until proven otherwise — leave the clinic pathway.
🩺 Reserve the cath labRevascularise for refractory symptoms or high-risk anatomy (left main, proximal LAD, multivessel + ischaemia, low EF).
🧪 ORBITA humilityPlacebo-controlled sham PCI showed much of PCI's angina benefit is placebo — try OMT properly before stenting.

🔬 Advanced Topics — Fellowship Level

🧠 Beyond the pathway

  • 📊 Pre-test probability, recalibrated: the 2019/2024 ESC estimates are markedly LOWER than Diamond-Forrester/older CAD-Consortium figures — reducing over-testing. Probability is modulated by risk factors, resting ECG, LV function and coronary calcium; a very low probability warrants deferral, not a stress test.
  • 🖥️ CTCA + CT-FFR + coronary calcium: CTCA defines anatomy and plaque with very high NPV (SCOT-HEART); CT-FFR adds lesion-specific physiology to avoid unnecessary invasive angiography; CAC scoring refines risk (a zero score is powerfully reassuring in selected patients, though it does not exclude non-calcified plaque in the young).
  • 🫀 Functional imaging: stress echo, CMR perfusion, and PET/SPECT quantify ischaemic burden and localise the culprit territory; at invasive angiography, FFR (≤0.80) or iFR (≤0.89) confirm a stenosis is flow-limiting before any stent — physiology-guided PCI (FAME) outperforms angiography-guided.
  • 🔬 INOCA / ANOCA: angina with non-obstructive coronaries is common (esp. women). Endotypes — microvascular angina (impaired coronary flow reserve/high index of microcirculatory resistance) and vasospastic angina (epicardial/microvascular spasm). Diagnose with coronary function testing: CFR/IMR + intracoronary acetylcholine provocation (CorMicA showed stratified therapy improves symptoms). Treat by endotype — microvascular: beta-blockers, ranolazine, ACEi; vasospastic: CCBs + nitrates, avoid non-selective beta-blockers.
  • 💊 Anti-anginal mechanisms in depth: beta-blockers (↓demand), CCBs (vasodilation ± rate control), nitrates (preload/coronary dilation, tolerance), ranolazine (late-Na⁺ current), ivabradine (If/pure HR reduction), nicorandil (KATP opener/nitrate) — mechanism-matched selection (e.g. ivabradine when beta-blockers contraindicated; ranolazine/CCB in microvascular/vasospastic disease).
  • 🩺 Completeness of revascularisation: when revascularisation is indicated, complete (vs incomplete) revascularisation and physiology-/imaging-guided lesion selection matter; SYNTAX score and Heart Team guide PCI vs CABG in left main/multivessel disease, with CABG favoured in complex anatomy/diabetes/low EF.
  • 🔥 Refractory angina therapies: for symptoms persisting despite maximal OMT and unsuitable/failed revascularisation — enhanced external counterpulsation (EECP), coronary sinus reducer device, spinal cord stimulation, and specialist pain/rehab programmes.

🎭 Morbidity & Mortality Conference

🎭 M&M — "The Stent Before the Statin"

The case: A 60-year-old man with 6 months of typical, predictable exertional angina (CCS II) and untreated dyslipidaemia was referred "for angiography and stenting." He was medication-naive — no statin (declined years earlier and never revisited), no anti-anginal, only aspirin and GTN. Rather than trialling optimal medical therapy, he was fast-tracked to invasive angiography, which showed a moderate proximal LAD lesion. Without FFR/physiology, an operator stented it "while we're in here." He suffered a peri-procedural complication (side-branch occlusion with a small procedural MI, plus an access-site bleed). He gained no prognostic benefit — his angina, in a vessel that was never proven flow-limiting, was arguably placebo-responsive anyway (ORBITA) — and his statin, blood pressure and glucose were still never addressed. Months later his LDL remained 4+ mmol/L.

🧀 The Swiss-cheese holes that lined up:

🏃 NO OMT TRIALFast-tracked to PCI before any statin or anti-anginal — the exact error ISCHEMIA/COURAGE/ORBITA warn against.
🧪 NO PHYSIOLOGYModerate lesion stented without FFR/iFR — a lesion never proven to be flow-limiting.
🩹 PROCEDURAL HARMPeri-procedural MI + bleed: real risk incurred for no prognostic gain.
🛡️ PREVENTION IGNOREDStatin, BP and glucose never optimised — the actual prognosis levers left untouched.
🧠 ANCHORING"Blockage = stent" reasoning; referral request treated as the diagnosis and the plan.

🛡️ What would have saved him from harm: treat stable angina with optimal medical therapy and risk-factor control first; use pre-test probability and CTCA/CT-FFR to characterise disease non-invasively; if invasive angiography is done, use FFR/iFR before stenting; reserve revascularisation for symptoms refractory to OMT or genuinely high-risk anatomy; and never let a stent substitute for the statin. This is exactly the discipline that protected the patient in Bed 1 — who got the statin, kept his native LAD, and did better.

⚔️ ONE-LINER RULE"In stable coronary disease, the stent that impresses the patient rarely helps him, and the statin that bores him usually does. Optimal medical therapy first — the cath lab is for the few, not the reflex."
