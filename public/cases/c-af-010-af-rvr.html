🧬 Why THIS patient? — stretch + trigger + a stiff ventricle

AF is a disease of the left atrium: years of hypertension, diabetes and a touch of heart failure stretch and fibrose the atrial wall, and the muscular sleeves of the pulmonary veins become a nest of rapid-firing ectopic triggers. Once those triggers fire into a remodeled, fibrosed atrium, multiple re-entrant wavelets set up and the atrium quivers at 400–600/min instead of contracting. The AV node, bombarded chaotically, lets through whatever it can — giving an irregularly irregular ventricular response, often rapid. This week's chest infection, the alcohol binge and the adrenergic surge were the spark; the remodeled atrium was the kindling. Her stiff, hypertrophied ventricle made the loss of atrial contraction especially costly tonight.

🔥 Risk factors / drivers for AF 🩺 Hypertension (the commonest) · 💙 Heart failure (HFpEF & HFrEF) · 🫁 Pulmonary disease / PE / pneumonia · 🦋 Hyperthyroidism · 🍷 Alcohol ("holiday heart") · 😴 Obstructive sleep apnea · 🫀 Valvular disease (esp. mitral) · 🍬 Diabetes & obesity · 👵 Increasing age · 🔪 Recent surgery / sepsis

🧭 The single most important question: STABLE or UNSTABLE?

AF-with-RVR management forks on one decision. Look for the adverse features (the "4 S's"): Shock (SBP < 90, hypoperfusion), Syncope, Severe ischemia (chest pain, ST changes), Severe heart failure (pulmonary edema). Mrs. A.F. is tachycardic and congested but conscious, perfusing and normotensive-borderline — she is haemodynamically unstable-leaning but currently STABLE. That buys time to rate-control, anticoagulate appropriately and decide on rhythm — not to shock blindly.

⚡ The fork: STABLE → rate control + anticoagulation + planned rhythm decision; UNSTABLE → immediate synchronized cardioversionIf she were in shock, ischemia or frank pulmonary edema from the AF, the answer would be electricity now — synchronized cardioversion regardless of the 48-hour rule, with anticoagulation started as soon as possible. While she remains stable, the priorities are control the rate, start anticoagulation, and make the rhythm decision deliberately — never cardiovert AF of unknown duration without protecting the brain.

👨‍⚕️ Resident Review

The cardiology resident arrives at 14:44. Her reasoning, out loud:

🧠 Name the rhythm, then make three decisions

"Narrow. Irregularly irregular. No P waves, a fibrillatory baseline. That is atrial fibrillation with a rapid ventricular response — I don't need anything fancy to call it. The mistake here would be to skip straight to the defibrillator. She is stable. AF management is three parallel decisions: control the rate, decide on the rhythm, and prevent the stroke. Get the order and the timing right and she does well; rush the rhythm and you can hand her a stroke."

🎯 Immediate priorities (her actual order of operations)

  1. 🩺 Confirm she is stable: no shock, no ischemia, no pulmonary edema crashing her — she has time to think.
  2. 🐌 Rate control first: IV/oral beta-blocker (metoprolol) or non-DHP calcium-channel blocker (diltiazem) to bring resting rate < 110 — but check the EF, because in HFrEF those CCBs are dangerous (negative inotropy).
  3. 🩸 Score the stroke risk now: calculate CHA₂DS₂-VASc and start anticoagulation — a DOAC unless there is a mechanical valve or moderate-severe mitral stenosis.
  4. ⏱️ Date the AF: ask precisely "when did the palpitations start?" The < 48 h vs ≥ 48 h / unknown answer determines whether early cardioversion is safe or needs 3 weeks of anticoagulation or a TEE first.
  5. 🔍 Hunt the precipitant (PIRATES): chest infection, alcohol, thyroid, electrolytes, ischemia, sleep apnea — treating the trigger is treating the AF.
  6. ⚡ Keep the defibrillator ready: not to shock now, but in case she decompensates and the decision flips to unstable.
⚔️ ONE-LINER RULE"Control the rate, score the clot, date the AF — then, and only then, decide whether to chase the rhythm. Cardioversion is the last decision, not the first."

🤔 "But she's fast and breathless — shouldn't we just shock her now?"

"Only if she is unstable. She is breathless from the rate and the lost atrial kick, but she is perfusing and conscious. Slow the rate and most of that breathlessness eases. Shocking AF of unknown duration in an unanticoagulated patient swaps a treatable fast rhythm for a potential embolic stroke. The fast rate is urgent; it is not an excuse to skip the clot question."

🧪 Resident's self-test (answer before reading on) Her last echo showed preserved EF (~55%) with LVH. The team suggests IV diltiazem for rate control. Safe choice here?

Yes — with a caveat. In preserved-EF AF, a non-DHP calcium-channel blocker (diltiazem/verapamil) or a beta-blocker are both reasonable first-line rate agents. The thing to be sure of is that this is NOT HFrEF — in reduced-EF heart failure, verapamil/diltiazem's negative inotropy can precipitate cardiogenic shock, and you reach instead for a beta-blocker or digoxin. Confirm the EF before you commit to a CCB.

🎓 Consultant Round

The on-call consultant cardiac electrophysiologist, by phone at 14:55, then at the bedside:

🗂️ Rate control or rhythm control — choose the philosophy

🐌 Rate control (the default for many)

  • Accept the AF, control the ventricular rate to < 110 (lenient, RACE II) or < 80 if symptomatic
  • Beta-blocker or non-DHP CCB; digoxin as add-on or in hypotension/HF
  • Simpler, fewer pro-arrhythmic risks; AFFIRM showed no mortality penalty historically
  • Always paired with stroke-risk-based anticoagulation

🎵 Rhythm control (restore & keep sinus)

  • Electrical or chemical cardioversion ± antiarrhythmic ± ablation
  • Favored when symptomatic despite rate control, younger, first episode, HFrEF (CASTLE-AF), or early after onset (EAST-AFNET 4)
  • Needs the same anticoagulation rules — and the 48-hour clot rule before any cardioversion
  • Pulmonary-vein-isolation ablation is increasingly an early option
⚔️ ONE-LINER RULE"Rate control accepts the rhythm and protects the brain; rhythm control chases sinus and still protects the brain. Anticoagulation is decided by the CHA₂DS₂-VASc score — never by which philosophy you pick."

⏱️ The cardioversion / clot rule — say it out loud every time

Restoring sinus rhythm makes a stunned atrium resume contraction; if a thrombus has formed in the left atrial appendage during fibrillation, that first organized squeeze can eject it → stroke. So: if AF is reliably < 48 hours (and the patient is low-risk), you may cardiovert. If AF is ≥ 48 hours or of unknown duration, either give 3 weeks of therapeutic anticoagulation first OR perform a transoesophageal echo (TEE) to exclude an appendage thrombus before cardioverting. Anticoagulate for ≥ 4 weeks after cardioversion regardless, because atrial mechanical function — and thrombus risk — persists after the rhythm looks normal.

🧭 Consultant's three commandments for the next hour

  1. 🐌 "Control the rate to settle the breathlessness — beta-blocker or diltiazem if EF is preserved, beta-blocker or digoxin if it isn't. Target resting < 110, < 80 if she stays symptomatic."
  2. 🩸 "Score her CHA₂DS₂-VASc and start a DOAC today — she has hypertension, diabetes, age 65–74 and female sex; she is well past the threshold to anticoagulate."
  3. ⏱️ "Pin down the AF duration. If it's truly < 48 h and she wants sinus, we can cardiovert on anticoagulation. If it's unknown, it's three weeks of a DOAC or a TEE before any shock — and four weeks of anticoagulation after, no matter what."
☠️ The consultant's war memory (more in S13 M&M)"The last AF I saw mishandled was a 72-year-old cardioverted electively for an 'uncertain duration' rhythm — no three weeks of anticoagulation, no TEE, because 'she only felt it this morning.' The rhythm strip was perfect; the brain was not. Date the AF and protect the brain — every single time."

📖 Teaching Points

🔬 The mechanism — WHY AF reduces output (the WHY behind the WHY)

  1. 🌱 The triggers: muscular sleeves around the pulmonary veins fire rapid ectopic bursts into the left atrium.
  2. 🕸️ The substrate: a stretched, fibrosed atrium (from hypertension, diabetes, heart failure, age) supports multiple simultaneous re-entrant wavelets — the atrium quivers at 400–600/min and never organizes a contraction.
  3. 🚦 The filter: the AV node is bombarded chaotically and conducts whatever isn't refractory → an irregularly irregular, often rapid, ventricular response. The AV node's refractoriness — and the drugs that lengthen it — set the rate.
  4. 💔 Consequence 1 — loss of the "atrial kick": the coordinated atrial contraction normally tops up ventricular filling by ~20–30% of stroke volume. Lose it and output falls — worst in a stiff, hypertrophied or HFpEF ventricle that depends on that late filling.
  5. ⏱️ Consequence 2 — poor diastolic filling: a fast, irregular rate shortens diastole; the ventricle has too little time to fill between beats. Together with the lost atrial kick → reduced cardiac output, congestion and demand ischemia.
⚔️ ONE-LINER RULE"AF steals the atrial kick and shortens diastole at the same time — that is why slowing the rate often fixes the breathlessness even before you ever restore sinus rhythm."

⚖️ Contrast pair #1 — AF vs Atrial Flutter / MAT / Sinus Tachycardia

💓 AF IS… (this patient)

  • Irregularly irregular, no two R-R intervals the same
  • No discrete P waves — chaotic fibrillatory baseline
  • Atrial rate 400–600/min; variable ventricular response
  • Variable pulse volume, apical-radial deficit

🔁 The mimics it is NOT…

  • Atrial flutter: regular sawtooth flutter waves, often 2:1 → a regular ~150/min
  • MAT: ≥ 3 distinct P-wave morphologies, irregular, classically severe lung disease
  • Sinus tachycardia: regular, normal upright P before every QRS, rate tracks a stressor
  • Each is "irregular or fast" but the P waves and regularity give them away

😈 The trap: a fast, slightly irregular rhythm gets called "AF" reflexively — but flutter with variable block can mimic it, and MAT in a COPD patient is treated differently (fix the lungs, avoid cardioversion). Read the P waves and the regularity before you commit.

⚖️ Contrast pair #2 — Rate control vs Rhythm control

🐌 Rate control

  • Accept AF, slow the AV node (beta-blocker, diltiazem, digoxin)
  • No mortality penalty in the classic AFFIRM population
  • Lenient target < 110 (RACE II) acceptable for many
  • Simpler, fewer pro-arrhythmic and procedural risks

🎵 Rhythm control

  • Cardioversion ± antiarrhythmic ± ablation to keep sinus
  • Early rhythm control improved outcomes in EAST-AFNET 4
  • Ablation improves outcomes in AF + HFrEF (CASTLE-AF)
  • Best for symptomatic, younger, first-episode or HFrEF patients

💡 Why it matters: both strategies require the same stroke prevention — restoring sinus rhythm does NOT remove the need for CHA₂DS₂-VASc-based anticoagulation, because AF recurs silently. Choosing rhythm control is never a reason to stop the anticoagulant.

🔬 Fellowship Corner — Advanced Topics

  • 🗺️ Pulmonary-vein isolation (PVI) & substrate ablation: point-by-point RF or cryoballoon isolation of the PV triggers; persistent AF may need additional substrate modification. Early ablation (EARLY-AF, STOP-AF First) beats antiarrhythmic drugs for rhythm control.
  • 🧬 Left-atrial-appendage occlusion (Watchman): for patients with high stroke risk but contraindications to long-term anticoagulation — the appendage is where ~90% of AF-related clots form.
  • 💊 Antiarrhythmic selection: flecainide/propafenone (only in structurally normal hearts — "pill-in-the-pocket"), sotalol, dronedarone, amiodarone (most effective but most toxic). Avoid class Ic in structural heart disease/CAD (CAST lesson).
  • 🩸 DOAC nuances: dose-reduce for renal function/age/weight per agent; avoid DOACs in mechanical valves and moderate-severe mitral stenosis (warfarin only). Reversal: idarucizumab (dabigatran), andexanet alfa (factor-Xa inhibitors).
  • 🧮 Risk-score refinements: CHA₂DS₂-VASc for stroke, HAS-BLED to identify and modify bleeding risk (NOT to withhold anticoagulation), and the move toward CHA₂DS₂-VA (dropping the sex point) in newer guidance.
  • 🔁 AV-node ablation + pacing ("ablate and pace"): for refractory rapid AF when rate control fails and rhythm control is not feasible — creates pacemaker dependence but guarantees rate control.

📋 Orders

🖊️ The order set, written as you'd actually write it (14:50)

  • 🐌 Rate control: IV/oral metoprolol (e.g. 2.5–5 mg IV slow, repeat) OR diltiazem if EF preserved; target resting rate < 110 (< 80 if symptomatic). Avoid non-DHP CCB if HFrEF — use beta-blocker or digoxin.
  • 🩸 Anticoagulation: calculate CHA₂DS₂-VASc and start a DOAC today (apixaban/rivaroxaban/dabigatran/edoxaban) unless mechanical valve or moderate-severe mitral stenosis → warfarin.
  • ⏱️ Document AF onset time precisely — the < 48 h vs ≥ 48 h/unknown answer drives the cardioversion-safety decision.
  • 🔍 Find the precipitant (PIRATES): CXR + sputum (this week's chest infection), TSH, U&E/Mg²⁺/Ca²⁺, troponin if ischemic features, ask about alcohol and sleep apnea.
  • 💙 Treat congestion gently: small-dose IV diuretic if frankly congested, oxygen to SpO₂ ≥ 94%; do not over-diurese a rate problem.
  • 🫀 12-lead ECG (confirm AF, exclude pre-excitation/ischemia), continuous telemetry, transthoracic echo to confirm EF, valves and LA size.
  • 📞 Refer cardiology/EP; if she becomes unstable → immediate synchronized cardioversion (and anticoagulate as soon as possible).
  • 🔌 Keep defibrillator and pads attached in case the rhythm decision flips to unstable.
⚔️ ONE-LINER RULE"In stable AF-with-RVR there are three orders that matter: slow the rate, score-and-start the anticoagulant, and date the AF. Everything else exists to make rhythm control safe."

👩‍⚕️ Nursing Care

🛏️ Cardiology nursing priorities — minute by minute

  • 📟 Continuous telemetry with the defibrillator at the bedside, pads attached. Document the rhythm strip and note the irregular, variable R-R pattern.
  • 🫳 Take an apical-radial (apex–wrist) pulse to detect the pulse deficit — automated NIBP/sats can read falsely in irregular rhythms; correlate by hand.
  • 🩸 BP and rate every 5–15 min while rate-controlling; a falling pressure or rising rate means escalate now, not at the next set of obs.
  • 💊 Give rate-control and anticoagulant drugs as prescribed and double-check the EF question before any non-DHP CCB; watch for hypotension/bradycardia after IV beta-blocker or diltiazem.
  • ⏱️ Help pin down the AF onset time — ask the patient and family exactly when symptoms began; this single fact governs cardioversion safety.
  • 🩸 Watch for bleeding once anticoagulation starts (bruising, melena, neuro changes) and check the HAS-BLED-modifiable factors.
  • 📋 Document strips before and after every intervention, the drug, dose and time — invaluable for the EP team and any planned cardioversion.
  • 🔇 Calm the room: anxiety and pain raise catecholamines that speed AV conduction. A composed bedside helps the rate.
  • 👨‍👩‍👦 Keep the family informed and supported (see S16); new-onset AF with breathlessness is frightening.
⚔️ ONE-LINER RULE"The nurse who takes an apical-radial pulse and nails down exactly when the AF started is doing half the diagnostic and safety work in the room."
📢 Escalate IMMEDIATELY forNew hypotension / hypoperfusion · ischemic chest pain or ST changes · worsening pulmonary edema or hypoxia · syncope (→ unstable → synchronized cardioversion) · symptomatic bradycardia/pauses after rate-control drugs · any plan to cardiovert AF of unknown duration without anticoagulation or a TEE · bleeding after anticoagulation.

📈 ECG / Imaging

🫀 The presenting rhythm — read it like a detective

Narrow QRS, irregularly irregular R-R intervals, and no discrete P waves — just a chaotic fibrillatory baseline. This triad is diagnostic of atrial fibrillation.

🔎 The AF-recognition toolkit (memorize the high-yield ones)

FeatureWhy it means AF
💓 Irregularly irregular R-RNo two intervals the same — the AV node conducts chaotic atrial impulses unpredictably. The single most useful bedside-and-ECG sign.
〰️ Absent P wavesNo organized atrial contraction → no discrete P waves; a fibrillatory baseline replaces them.
📈 Fibrillatory (f) wavesFine or coarse undulations of the baseline at 400–600/min — the quivering atrium itself.
📐 Narrow QRS (usually)Conduction is via the normal His-Purkinje system, so the QRS is narrow unless there is pre-existing/rate-related bundle branch block or pre-excitation.
🫳 Pulse deficitApical rate > radial rate — short-diastole beats fill too little to generate a palpable pulse, a clinical fingerprint of AF.
⚠️ Beware pre-excitationAF in WPW gives a fast, irregular, broad tachycardia with varying QRS width — do NOT give AV-nodal blockers; cardiovert or use procainamide.
⚔️ ONE-LINER RULE"Irregularly irregular + no P waves = AF. But if that irregular rhythm is also broad and very fast, think pre-excited AF and keep your hands off the AV node."

🫀 Echo & further imaging

  • 🩻 Transthoracic echo (TTE): EF ~55% (preserved) with LVH and a dilated left atrium — confirms HFpEF physiology and the atrial substrate; helps choose rate agents and assess valves.
  • 🫧 Transoesophageal echo (TEE): the test to exclude a left-atrial-appendage thrombus before cardioverting AF of ≥ 48 h / unknown duration — the alternative to 3 weeks of pre-cardioversion anticoagulation.
  • 🫁 CXR: mild pulmonary congestion and a possible consolidation matching the chest infection precipitant.
⚠️ The ECG's favorite lie in irregular tachycardia"It's fast and a bit irregular, so it's AF." Atrial flutter with variable AV block, and MAT in lung disease, both mimic AF. Look hard for regular sawtooth flutter waves (flutter) or ≥ 3 P-wave morphologies (MAT) before you commit — they change the treatment.

💊 Medications

💉 The drugs — what, why, and the traps

DrugRole hereMechanism / caution
Metoprolol (beta-blocker)First-line rate control; slows AV conductionWorks in preserved AND reduced EF (cautious dosing in decompensated HF). Watch for hypotension/bradycardia; avoid IV bolus into severe hypotension.
Diltiazem / verapamil (non-DHP CCB)First-line rate control if EF preservedNegative inotropy → AVOID in HFrEF (can precipitate cardiogenic shock). Good when EF is preserved, as in this patient.

⚔️ ONE-LINER RULE"In HFrEF, reach for a beta-blocker or digoxin, not verapamil/diltiazem — and anticoagulate by the CHA₂DS₂-VASc score, with a DOAC unless it's a mechanical valve or tight mitral stenosis."
🚫 The 'never' list in AF❌ Non-DHP CCB (verapamil/diltiazem) in HFrEF (negative inotropy → shock) · ❌ AV-nodal blockers (adenosine, verapamil, diltiazem, digoxin, IV beta-blocker) in pre-excited AF (WPW) → can precipitate VF · ❌ Cardioverting AF of unknown duration without anticoagulation or a TEE · ❌ Class Ic antiarrhythmics (flecainide) in structural heart disease / CAD · ❌ Withholding anticoagulation purely because HAS-BLED is high — modify the bleeding risk instead.

🩸 CHA₂DS₂-VASc & the anticoagulation decision

  • 🧮 CHA₂DS₂-VASc components: Congestive HF (1) · Hypertension (1) · Age ≥ 75 (2) · Diabetes (1) · Stroke/TIA/thromboembolism (2) · Vascular disease (1) · Age 65–74 (1) · Sex category female (1).
  • 👵 This patient: HTN (1) + diabetes (1) + HFpEF (1) + age 65–74 (1) + female (1) = score 5 → clearly anticoagulate.
  • 🩹 HAS-BLED estimates bleeding risk to identify and fix modifiable factors (BP, alcohol, interacting drugs) — it is not a reason to withhold anticoagulation.

📟 Monitoring

📡 What to watch and why

  • 📈 Continuous telemetry until rate-controlled and stable; document conversion to sinus or persistence of AF, and any pauses on rate-control drugs.
  • 💓 Ventricular rate trend — aim resting < 110 (lenient) or < 80 if symptomatic; correlate the monitor with an apical-radial pulse because automated counts misread irregular rhythms.
  • 🩸 BP and perfusion — a falling pressure or new ischemia/edema flips her to "unstable" and changes the plan to synchronized cardioversion.
  • 🩹 Bleeding surveillance once anticoagulated — bruising, GI bleeding, neuro changes; recheck renal function for DOAC dosing.
  • 🫁 Oxygenation and congestion — sats, work of breathing, crackles; gentle diuresis if congested, avoid over-diuresing a rate problem.
  • 🦋 Precipitant follow-through — repeat thyroid function, response of the chest infection, electrolytes; the AF often settles when the trigger does.
  • ⏱️ The 48-hour clock — keep the documented onset time visible so the cardioversion-safety decision is never made on a guess.
⚔️ ONE-LINER RULE"Once the rate is controlled, the monitor's job changes from 'how fast is she?' to 'is she stable, is she bleeding, and is the trigger treated?' — and the onset time decides if and when she can be cardioverted."
S13

⚠️ Complications

💀 What can go wrong — and the mechanism

  • 🧠 Embolic stroke — the great fear; a left-atrial-appendage clot embolizes, especially around cardioversion of unanticoagulated AF of unknown duration. This is why we score, anticoagulate and respect the 48-hour rule.
  • 💙 Tachycardia-induced cardiomyopathy — weeks-to-months of poorly controlled rapid AF can reversibly weaken the ventricle; rate or rhythm control reverses it.
  • 🌊 Decompensated heart failure / pulmonary edema — loss of atrial kick + short diastole in a stiff ventricle floods the lungs; can tip a stable patient into "unstable."
  • 🐌 Drug complications — hypotension/bradycardia/pauses from beta-blocker or diltiazem; cardiogenic shock if a non-DHP CCB is given in unrecognized HFrEF; digoxin toxicity.
  • 🩸 Bleeding from anticoagulation — the trade-off for stroke protection; modify the modifiable HAS-BLED factors, don't withhold.
  • ⚡ VF from AV-nodal blockade in pre-excited AF — giving adenosine/verapamil/digoxin to WPW + AF can accelerate accessory-pathway conduction into VF.

🎭 Morbidity & Mortality Conference — "The Rhythm Was Perfect; The Brain Was Not"

The case: A 72-year-old woman, hypertensive and diabetic, came to a busy clinic with palpitations and AF at 150. She "only felt it this morning," so the team booked an elective synchronized cardioversion the same afternoon — no three weeks of anticoagulation, no TEE, because the duration was assumed to be < 48 h. Sinus rhythm was restored on the first shock. Six hours later she developed a dense right hemiplegia and aphasia — a left middle-cerebral-artery embolic stroke. The "this morning" onset had been wrong; she had been in silent AF for days. (A second classic version of this story: IV diltiazem given to a patient with undiagnosed HFrEF → profound negative inotropy → cardiogenic shock.)

🧀 The Swiss-cheese holes that lined up:

⏱️ DURATION ASSUMED"Only since this morning" taken at face value — AF is often silent; the real onset was days earlier.
🫧 NO TEENo transoesophageal echo to exclude an appendage thrombus before the shock.
🩸 NOT ANTICOAGULATEDNo 3 weeks of pre-cardioversion anticoagulation — the brain was unprotected.
🏃 RUSHED RHYTHMCardioversion chased for a stable patient who could have been rate-controlled and anticoagulated first.
💔 EF UNCHECKED (variant)Diltiazem into unrecognized HFrEF → cardiogenic shock.

🛡️ What would have saved her: treat AF duration as unknown unless it is unambiguously < 48 h; before cardioverting unknown-duration AF, give 3 weeks of therapeutic anticoagulation OR a TEE; anticoagulate ≥ 4 weeks afterward regardless; rate-control and anticoagulate the stable patient before chasing sinus; and confirm the EF before any non-DHP CCB. This is exactly why your nurse paused before cardioverting tonight.

⚔️ ONE-LINER RULE"'She only felt it this morning' is the sentence written on the chart of patients who then have an embolic stroke. Assume unknown duration unless you can prove < 48 hours."

🔀 Differentials

🧩 The irregular / fast narrow-complex tachycardia differential

DiagnosisCluesWhy it matters
💓 Atrial fibrillation (this patient)Irregularly irregular, no P waves, fibrillatory baseline, pulse deficitDefault for an irregularly irregular narrow tachycardia. Rate control + anticoagulation + rhythm decision.
🪥 Atrial flutterRegular sawtooth flutter waves; often 2:1 → regular ~150/min (variable block can look irregular)Similar anticoagulation rules; very amenable to cardioversion and cavotricuspid-isthmus ablation.
📐 Multifocal atrial tachycardia (MAT)≥ 3 distinct P-wave morphologies, irregular, classically severe COPD/lung diseaseTreat the lungs and electrolytes; cardioversion is ineffective; avoid blanket AF management.
📈 Sinus tachycardiaRegular, normal upright P before every QRS, rate tracks pain/fever/hypovolemia/anemiaTreat the cause, not the rate — rate control here can be harmful.
🌀 Pre-excited AF (WPW + AF)Irregular, broad, very fast, varying QRS width; delta-wave historyAV-nodal blockers can precipitate VF → use procainamide or cardiovert. The dangerous mimic.
🔁 AVNRT / AVRT (SVT)Regular, narrow, sudden onset/offset; may break with vagal/adenosineDifferent mechanism and treatment; regularity separates it from AF at the bedside.
⚔️ ONE-LINER RULE"IRREGULAR narrow tachycardia → think AF (then flutter and MAT). IRREGULAR broad tachycardia → think pre-excited AF and keep your hands off the AV node."

💎 Clinical Pearls

💓 Name it by the pulseIrregularly irregular with no P waves is AF until proven otherwise — confirm on a 12-lead, then score it.

🧠 A clot ejected is a stroke deliveredDon't cardiovert AF of unknown duration without anticoagulation or a clear TEE — the first organized squeeze can launch the clot.
