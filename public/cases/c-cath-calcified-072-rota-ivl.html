🚦 Why calcium is the enemy of a good stent

Heavy coronary calcium is common in the diabetic, the elderly and the renal patient. It matters because a stent must fully expand to work. Calcium resists dilation, so a stent deployed into unmodified calcium under-expands — and under-expansion is the single strongest predictor of stent thrombosis and in-stent restenosis. The vicious cycle is: calcium → undilatable lesion → under-expanded stent → thrombosis / restenosis → repeat intervention on an even harder target. Break the cycle by modifying the calcium before the stent.

⚔️ ONE-LINER RULE"A stent is only as good as its expansion. Calcium you do not prepare is a stent you will not expand — and an under-expanded stent is a thrombosis waiting to happen."

📋 Pre-Procedure Workup & Planning

🧾 Planning a calcified-lesion PCI — anticipate, do not improvise

  • ✍️ Consent — document the added risks of calcium modification: perforation, dissection, slow-flow/no-reflow, burr entrapment, and the possible need for atherectomy/lithotripsy and emergency surgery.
  • 🫘 Renal status — CKD is common in calcified disease; plan a low-contrast strategy, hydrate, and consider imaging-guided (IVUS) rather than contrast-heavy runs.
  • 📽️ Read the fluoroscopy — calcium seen before contrast (tramline / radiopaque) flags a hard lesion; plan modification up front rather than discovering it with a stuck balloon.
  • 🛠️ Have the kit ready — rotational atherectomy console + burrs, orbital atherectomy, an intravascular lithotripsy (Shockwave) catheter, scoring/cutting balloons, high-pressure NC balloons, a temporary pacing wire (rota of the RCA/dominant vessel), and a covered stent + pericardiocentesis set on standby.
  • 🩸 Antithrombotic review — DAPT loaded; procedural anticoagulation planned (see S3).
⚔️ ONE-LINER RULE"Diagnose the calcium BEFORE you cross it. Tramlines on plain fluoroscopy mean you plan modification up front — do not find out with a balloon that will not open."

🩸 Vascular Access & Setup

🖐️ Access & support — calcium demands a strong platform

🖐️ Radial (default)

  • Fewer access-site bleeds and lower mortality in ACS
  • A 7 Fr radial (or sheathless) may be needed for larger rota burrs / bulky equipment
  • Confirm patency first (Barbeau/Allen)

🦵 Femoral (when you need muscle)

  • For big-bore support, larger burrs, or if radial anatomy will not deliver the atherectomy system
  • Ultrasound + micropuncture; higher bleeding risk
⚔️ ONE-LINER RULE"Calcium is unforgiving of a floppy platform. A well-supported guide and a strong wire deliver the burr or the lithotripsy balloon — pick the guide that gives you backup before you fight the rock."

💉 Guide, wire & anticoagulation for atherectomy

  • 🧭 Guide catheter: for the LAD (left system) use an EBU/XB left guide for maximal backup; a bigger bore (7 Fr) accommodates larger burrs and imaging simultaneously.
  • 🧵 Rotational atherectomy wire: rotablation runs over a dedicated RotaWire (floppy or extra-support). You must exchange onto it via a microcatheter — never rotablate over a standard workhorse wire.
  • 🩸 Anticoagulation: unfractionated heparin to an ACT ~250–300 s. Keep the rota flush running (heparinised saline ± vasodilator/nitroglycerin/verapamil "rota cocktail") to cool the burr and reduce slow-flow.
  • 📟 Temporary pacing: consider for atherectomy of a dominant RCA or a large LAD (transient heart block / bradycardia during runs).

🔬 Calcium Assessment — Physiology & Imaging

🖥️ In calcified disease, imaging is kingAngiography under-calls calcium — it only sees the densest deposits. Intravascular imaging (IVUS/OCT) is the reference standard for calcium: it quantifies the arc (how far around the vessel), thickness, and length, and predicts under-expansion. Use physiology (FFR ≤0.80 / iFR ≤0.89) to confirm the lesion is ischaemic and worth treating — but once you commit, let imaging decide HOW.

📏 Physiology first — is the lesion even ischaemic?

  • FFR (fractional flow reserve) — pressure-wire ratio during hyperaemia (adenosine). FFR ≤ 0.80 = ischaemic → treat. Above 0.80 → defer.
  • iFR (instantaneous wave-free ratio) — a resting index, no adenosine. iFR ≤ 0.89 = significant.
  • ⚠️ Caveat: crossing a rigid calcified lesion with a pressure wire can be tricky, and severe calcium is usually flow-limiting on angiography anyway.

🖥️ IVUS vs OCT — imaging the calcium

🔵 IVUS

  • Ultrasound — deep penetration; sizes the vessel and plaque burden
  • Calcium appears as a bright arc with acoustic shadowing (you cannot measure its thickness behind the shadow)
  • No contrast — ideal for CKD

🟠 OCT

  • Light — ultra-high resolution; calcium is signal-poor with sharp, measurable borders
  • Measures calcium arc, thickness AND length → the OCT calcium score
  • Needs a contrast flush (mind the CKD)

🧮 The OCT calcium score — predicting under-expansion

A validated score (Fujino et al.) awards points for: maximum calcium arc >180° (2 points), maximum thickness >0.5 mm (1 point), and length >5 mm (1 point). A score of ≥3–4 predicts stent under-expansion → plan calcium modification before stenting. IVUS surrogates: an arc >270°, a >5 mm length of superficial calcium, or a calcified nodule similarly flag a high-risk lesion.

🛠️ Intervention — Step-by-Step Execution

🎈 After the calcium is modified, a balloon expands fully and the stent can be deployed and post-dilated. Source: Wikimedia Commons (Blausen)

🌀 The calcium-modification toolbox — how each one works

  • 🌀 Rotational atherectomy (Rotablator): a diamond-coated burr spins at 135,000–180,000 rpm, preferentially ablating hard, inelastic calcium ("differential cutting"). Burr sizing to a burr:artery ratio ≈ 0.5–0.6 (modification, not debulking). Advance with a gentle pecking motion, short runs (~15–20 s), and avoid deceleration >5,000 rpm (excessive load → heat, slow-flow, entrapment).
  • 🛰️ Orbital atherectomy (Diamondback): an eccentric diamond crown sanders calcium bidirectionally; a single crown treats a range of vessel sizes; centrifugal orbit modifies both antegrade and retrograde.
  • ⚡ Intravascular lithotripsy (Shockwave IVL): a balloon with emitters delivers sonic pressure (shock) waves that fracture calcium — including deep calcium a burr cannot reach — while sparing soft tissue. Delivered at low balloon pressure, so low dissection/perforation risk. It must be able to cross the lesion.
  • ✂️ Scoring / cutting balloons: nitinol scoring wires or microblades concentrate force to crack a fibrocalcific plaque without melon-seeding.
  • 🎈 High-pressure non-compliant balloons: for moderate calcium; a persistent waist means escalate, do not just push harder.

🪜 The sequence — modify, then treat

  1. 🖥️ Image & score the calcium (IVUS/OCT) — arc, thickness, length; predict under-expansion.
  2. 🌀 Modify the calcium — atherectomy if uncrossable/nodular, IVL if it crosses but will not expand, scoring for moderate disease.
  3. 🎈 Confirm the calcium yields — a balloon now fully expands with no waist; re-image to confirm calcium fracture.
  4. 📏 Size the stent 1:1 to the reference vessel (imaging-guided); cover the modified segment.
  5. 🎈 Post-dilate with a non-compliant balloon to full expansion and apposition, then re-image to prove expansion (break the under-expansion → thrombosis cycle).
⚔️ ONE-LINER RULE"Modify, stent, post-dilate, image — in that order. A beautiful angiogram over an under-expanded stent in unprepared calcium is a future thrombosis dressed up as success."

🏠 Post-Procedure Care, Debrief & Assessment

🩹 Access-site & renal care

  • 🖐️ Radial: patent-haemostasis band; watch for radial occlusion and bleeding.
  • 🦵 Femoral: closure device / manual pressure; watch for haematoma, pseudoaneurysm, retroperitoneal bleed.
  • 🫘 Contrast nephropathy watch — CKD patients: hydrate, recheck creatinine, avoid nephrotoxins; a low-contrast, imaging-guided procedure protects the kidneys.

💊 Secondary prevention (start day 1)

  • DAPT — aspirin + a P2Y₁₂ inhibitor; duration individualised by ischaemic vs bleeding risk (complex calcified PCI leans toward the longer end if bleeding risk allows).
  • High-intensity statin (atorvastatin 80 mg) regardless of LDL; add ezetimibe/PCSK9i to target.
  • Glycaemic & renal optimisation — SGLT2 inhibitor where appropriate; tight risk-factor control (calcium reflects diffuse atherosclerosis).
  • 🏃 Cardiac rehab; smoking cessation; surveillance for restenosis given the high-risk substrate.
⚔️ ONE-LINER RULE"Calcified disease is a marker of diffuse, aggressive atherosclerosis. The stent treats one segment; the statin, the sugar, and the kidneys treat the patient."

🔭 Advanced Topics — Fellowship Level

  • 🌀 Burr sizing & RotaFloppy vs RotaWire: burr:artery ratio ≈ 0.5–0.6 for modification; start small and step up only if needed. RotaLink Plus, dynaglide for retrieval, and the wireClip torquer are part of the workflow.
  • ⚡ RotaTripsy: combining rotational atherectomy (to cross/superficially ablate) with IVL (to fracture deep calcium) for the most resistant, concentric, undilatable lesions.
  • 🪤 Burr entrapment (Kokeshi phenomenon): avoid with pecking, short runs, adequate wire support and a low burr:artery ratio; retrieve with deep guide intubation, mother-child catheters, buddy-wire/balloon, or ultimately surgery.
  • 🚫 Slow-flow physiology: particulate embolisation + vasospasm + microvascular plugging; prophylaxis with a vasodilator "rota cocktail", short runs, and haemodynamic support; treat with intracoronary adenosine/nicardipine/nitroprusside.
  • 🛰️ Orbital vs rotational atherectomy: orbital treats bidirectionally and adapts crown speed to lumen size; rotational offers a range of fixed burrs — both target calcium via differential cutting.
  • 🖥️ Imaging-guided calcium strategy: OCT quantifies fracture after IVL; IVUS penetrates behind calcium and guides sizing; the min stent area drives late outcomes.
  • 🫀 Mechanical support: for high-risk calcified PCI (last patent vessel, severe LV dysfunction), consider Impella / percutaneous LVAD to tolerate slow-flow and prolonged runs.

🎭 Morbidity & Mortality Conference

🎭 M&M — "The Burr That Would Not Come Out"

The case: A heavily calcified proximal LAD was tackled with rotational atherectomy. The operator, behind schedule, chose an oversized burr (burr:artery ratio ~0.7) and ran long, continuous ablation runs, forcing forward through resistance. The console tone dropped and RPM decelerated repeatedly — ignored. The burr entrapped in the calcium (the Kokeshi / stuck-burr phenomenon) and would neither advance nor withdraw. In a forceful attempt at retrieval, the calcified vessel perforated. The operator pulled equipment out to "see better"; contrast poured into the pericardium, the patient tamponaded and arrested. Reversal, a covered stent and pericardiocentesis came minutes too late. He did not survive.

🧀 The Swiss-cheese holes that lined up:

📏 OVERSIZED BURRA burr:artery ratio ~0.7 (debulking, not modifying) grabbed hard calcium and stalled.
⏱️ LONG CONTINUOUS RUNSNo pecking, no short runs → heat, particulate load, and mechanical grab.
🔧 IGNORED DECELERATIONRepeated RPM drops >5,000 were the warning to STOP — and were pushed through.
🎈 REMOVED EQUIPMENT AT PERFORATIONThe first move in a perforation is to INFLATE across it — not withdraw.
🫗 NO REHEARSED DRILLCovered stent and pericardiocentesis kit were not ready; tamponade won the race.

🛡️ What would have saved him: a modification (not debulking) burr:artery ratio ≈ 0.5–0.6; short pecking runs with the burr never buried; STOP at any deceleration >5,000 rpm; and — the instant extravasation appeared — re-inflate a balloon across the perforation to tamponade it, reverse the heparin, deploy a covered (PTFE) stent, and open the pericardiocentesis kit already on the table. Patience with the burr, and a rehearsed perforation drill (S9), save lives.

⚔️ ONE-LINER RULE"A decelerating burr is begging you to stop. Peck, keep it small, and never force — and if the vessel bleeds, your hands INFLATE, they do not withdraw."
