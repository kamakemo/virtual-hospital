⚔️ ONE-LINER RULE"Valsalva and standing make hypertrophic cardiomyopathy LOUDER and aortic stenosis SOFTER; squatting does the reverse. One bedside manoeuvre separates a dynamic obstruction from a fixed one."

🧭 Why the manoeuvres work — the physiology in one line

Anything that empties the ventricle (↓preload: standing, Valsalva strain, nitrates, diuresis) or lets blood leave more easily (↓afterload: vasodilators) — or that squeezes harder (↑contractility) — brings the hypertrophied septum and the mitral leaflet closer together, worsening SAM and the gradient. Anything that fills and splints the ventricle open (↑preload/afterload: squatting, handgrip, leg raise) relieves it. Hold that sentence — it drives the drugs, the danger, and the simulation in S6.

⚠️ The trap: "he's an athlete, this is just an athletic heart"Trained hearts hypertrophy too. But an athlete's heart is symmetric, ≤15 mm, with a large cavity, normal/enhanced diastolic function, a normal ECG-for-athlete pattern, and it regresses with detraining. Exertional syncope, a family history of sudden death, deep T-wave inversion and asymmetric ≥15 mm hypertrophy are NOT athlete's heart — they are HCM (full contrast in S14/S20).

👨‍⚕️ Resident Review

The cardiology registrar frames the case:

🧠 Two questions, held in parallel from the first minute

"This is textbook obstructive HCM. But 'confirming HCM' is the easy part — a phenotype echo does that. My job is the two questions that actually change his life: (1) Is he at high risk of sudden death? — because if so he needs an ICD, and (2) How obstructed and symptomatic is he? — because that drives the drugs and possible septal reduction. And a third, non-negotiable: his whole first-degree family needs screening."

🎯 The registrar's order of operations

  1. 🫀 Transthoracic echo with provocation — quantify wall thickness, confirm asymmetric septal LVH, SAM, and the resting and Valsalva/exercise LVOT gradient (latent obstruction is missed at rest).
  2. ⚡ SCD risk stratification — history (syncope, family SCD), 48-hour Holter (NSVT), max wall thickness, LV apical aneurysm, LGE — feed the HCM Risk-SCD model (S5).
  3. 🧲 Cardiac MRI — accurate wall thickness, apical aneurysm, and late gadolinium enhancement (fibrosis burden = arrhythmic substrate).
  4. 🚫 Do NOT reach for a nitrate, a vasodilator or a heavy diuretic for his 'chest pain' — they deepen the obstruction. Reach for a negative inotrope (beta-blocker first).
  5. 🧬 Genetic counselling + cascade family screening, and restrict competitive sport pending full assessment.
⚔️ ONE-LINER RULE"Every HCM patient gets a sudden-death risk assessment — the arrhythmia can arrive before the symptoms, and the ICD is the only thing that catches it."
🧪 Registrar's self-testHe's breathless and gets "chest pain" on exertion. A junior suggests sublingual GTN "for the angina." Give it?

Absolutely not. In obstructive HCM, GTN (a venodilator) drops preload, the ventricle empties, SAM worsens, the LVOT gradient soars and the blood pressure can collapse — precisely the mechanism of the M&M death in S21. Vasodilators, nitrates and aggressive diuresis are dangerous here. Treat the obstruction with negative inotropes, not vasodilators.

S6

🧠 Teaching Points + Interactive Simulation

🔬 The mechanism — sarcomere to systolic anterior motion (the WHY behind the WHY)

  1. 🧬 Sarcomere mutation: autosomal-dominant mutations in sarcomeric-protein genes (most commonly MYH7 β-myosin heavy chain and MYBPC3 myosin-binding protein C) cause disordered myocyte hypertrophy and myofibrillar disarray with interstitial fibrosis.
  2. 🧱 Unexplained hypertrophy: the LV wall thickens (classically asymmetric, septum > free wall) with no pressure/volume cause — the defining phenotype. Disarray + fibrosis create both the arrhythmic substrate and a stiff, poorly-relaxing ventricle (diastolic dysfunction).
  3. 🎚️ Dynamic outflow obstruction: the bulging septum narrows the LV outflow tract; rapid ejection drags the anterior mitral leaflet toward the septum (a Venturi/drag effect) — systolic anterior motion (SAM) — which both obstructs the outflow and creates a posteriorly-directed mitral regurgitation.
  4. 💫 Symptoms & danger: obstruction + stiff ventricle → exertional dyspnoea, angina (supply–demand mismatch in thick muscle) and exertional syncope; the fibrotic, disarrayed myocardium generates ventricular arrhythmia → sudden cardiac death, often in the young and during exertion.
⚔️ ONE-LINER RULE"In hypertrophic cardiomyopathy the obstruction is DYNAMIC — nitrates, diuretics and vasodilators make it WORSE and can kill; reach for negative inotropes, not vasodilators."

⚖️ Contrast pair — HCM (dynamic) vs Aortic Stenosis (fixed) obstruction

🎚️ HCM — DYNAMIC obstruction

  • Gradient WORSENS with ↓preload / ↓afterload / ↑contractility (standing, Valsalva, GTN, diuretics, vasodilators, inotropes)
  • Murmur louder on Valsalva/standing; jerky bisferiens pulse
  • Treat with negative inotropes; AVOID nitrates/vasodilators/heavy diuresis

🚪 Aortic stenosis — FIXED obstruction

  • Fixed orifice; murmur softer on Valsalva (less flow), radiates to carotids
  • Slow-rising, plateau (parvus et tardus) pulse
  • Also avoid vasodilators — but because a fixed output can't compensate for a dropped afterload, a different reason

💡 Same watchword ("careful with vasodilators") but opposite physiology: in HCM you are avoiding collapse of a dynamic gradient; in AS you are avoiding hypotension a fixed valve can't overcome.

🎮 Simulation — The Loading Dial & the Dynamic LVOT Gradient

One dial sets the ventricle's loading state. Drag it and watch the LVOT gradient — note that a full, relaxed, controlled ventricle (the middle) is the sweet spot, and emptying it (GTN, diuretics, standing, Valsalva) or whipping up contractility makes the obstruction WORSE — the exact opposite of fixed aortic stenosis.

Set the dial to explore the dynamic gradient.

🎯 Keep him full & relaxed with a negative inotrope (the green sweet spot). Slash preload/afterload or drive contractility → the gradient soars and he can collapse. This is why HCM patients get beta-blockers, not nitrates.

📋 Orders

🖊️ The clinic work-up order set

  • 📈 12-lead ECG — LVH, deep T-wave inversions, prominent Q waves (pseudo-infarct), giant negative T waves if apical HCM.
  • 🫀 Transthoracic echo with Valsalva provocation — max wall thickness & distribution, SAM, resting and provoked LVOT gradient, mitral regurgitation, LA size, diastolic function, EF.
  • 🏃 Exercise (stress) echo if resting/Valsalva gradient <50 mmHg but symptomatic — unmask latent obstruction; also assess exertional BP response (a flat/falling BP is a risk marker).
  • 🧲 Cardiac MRI with late gadolinium enhancement — accurate wall thickness, apical aneurysm, fibrosis burden.
  • 💥 48-hour (or longer) ambulatory ECG / Holter — screen for non-sustained VT and AF.
  • 🧬 Genetic counselling + sarcomere gene panel (MYBPC3, MYH7, etc.) to enable cascade family screening.
  • 🩸 Bloods incl. FBC, U&E, TFTs, BNP/NT-proBNP; consider work-up for phenocopies (see S14: Fabry, amyloid) if atypical.
  • 🚫 Explicitly withhold nitrates, other vasodilators and aggressive diuresis; restrict competitive/high-intensity sport pending full assessment.
⚔️ ONE-LINER RULE"Provoke the gradient — a resting echo can look almost normal while a latent LVOT obstruction only shows up on Valsalva or exercise. If you don't provoke it, you'll miss it."
S8

👩‍⚕️ Nursing Care

👩‍⚕️ Clinic nursing priorities — safety, education, and the family

  • 🩺 Accurate BP and heart rate, and be ready to record an exertional/standing BP response during stress testing — a fall in BP on exercise is a sudden-death risk marker.
  • 💧 Counsel to avoid dehydration and hypovolaemia (hot weather, vigorous exercise, missed fluids, alcohol excess) — these drop preload and worsen the dynamic gradient; correct the impulse to over-diurese.
  • 🚫 Medication safety net: flag and question any prescription of nitrates, dihydropyridine calcium blockers, high-dose diuretics or vasodilators — dangerous in obstructive HCM.
  • 💥 Symptom & syncope diary — teach him to report palpitations, presyncope, syncope or chest pain; these change the risk picture.
  • 🧬 Family screening coordination — arrange first-degree relative clinical/genetic screening; support the emotional weight of an inherited, sudden-death disease.
  • ⚽ Reinforce activity advice — no competitive/high-intensity sport until the team has completed risk assessment; shared decision-making thereafter.
  • 🔋 If an ICD is implanted: wound care, device-check schedule, driving rules, and what to do after a shock.
⚔️ ONE-LINER RULE"In hypertrophic cardiomyopathy the nurse's most life-saving act is often a sentence: 'don't let him get dehydrated, don't give him a nitrate, and get his family screened.'"

📈 ECG / Imaging + Visual Enhancements

🫀 The HCM 12-lead — big voltages, deep T-wave inversion

Massive precordial voltages (LVH) with deep, symmetric lateral T-wave inversion and prominent Q waves. Apical HCM classically gives giant negative T waves. A normal ECG makes HCM unlikely.

🩻 Provocation matters

  • Valsalva during echo unmasks latent obstruction when the resting gradient is low.
  • Exercise stress echo reveals exertional (physiological) obstruction and an abnormal exercise BP response (a sudden-death risk marker).
  • Serial imaging over years tracks progression, LA dilatation (AF risk) and any drift toward "burnt-out" systolic dysfunction.

S12

📟 Monitoring

📡 What to watch and why

  • 📈 Continuous telemetry with defib to hand — VT/VF peaks in the first hours; ST-segment monitoring catches re-occlusion.
  • 🧪 Serial troponin to define the trajectory; repeat 12-leads with every symptom change.
  • 🩸 BP each 5–15 min — hypotension may mean RV involvement, over-nitration, or evolving shock.
  • 🫁 SpO₂ (target ≥ 94% only if hypoxic), respiratory rate for evolving LV failure.
  • 🧮 Recompute risk (GRACE) once troponin returns — GRACE > 140 pushes toward early (< 24 h) invasive management.
⚔️ ONE-LINER RULE"After you decide 'ACS,' the monitor's question changes from 'is he ischaemic?' to 'is he about to arrest or re-occlude?' — and the answers are the rhythm and the recurring pain."

⚠️ Complications

💀 What can go wrong in the ED phase

  • ⚡ VT / VF / cardiac arrest — commonest early killer; why pads stay on.
  • 🫀 Evolution to STEMI / re-occlusion — the "NSTEMI" that declares itself on the 4th ECG.
  • 💔 Acute LV failure / cardiogenic shock — large territory at risk.
  • 🔊 Mechanical (later, days 3–7): papillary muscle rupture (acute MR), VSD, free-wall rupture — a new murmur + crash.
  • 🧠 Bleeding from antithrombotics; contrast nephropathy around PCI.
  • 🩹 The system complication: premature discharge — the biggest "complication" of all in the ED.
⚔️ ONE-LINER RULE"The deadliest ED complication of ACS isn't in the patient — it's the decision to send them home. Treat disposition as a procedure with its own checklist."

  • Cardiology + GP follow-up; no driving per local rules

🟢 The genuinely low-risk chest pain

  • HEART 0–3 + negative serial hs-troponin + non-ischaemic ECG
  • Shared decision-making, written safety-net, timely outpatient testing if needed
  • Explicit "come back immediately if…" advice
⚔️ ONE-LINER RULE"A safe chest-pain discharge is an active decision with a score, a negative troponin, a clean ECG and a written safety-net — never a silent 'looked fine.'"

⏱️ 10-minute ECGEvery chest pain gets a 12-lead within 10 minutes — and a repeat with any change.

🔬 Advanced Topics — Fellowship Level

🧠 Beyond the pathway

  • 🩻 The "Occlusion MI (OMI)" paradigm: the STEMI/NSTEMI dichotomy misses acute coronary occlusion without ST-elevation criteria. OMI-vs-NOMI thinking (subtle occlusion patterns, hyperacute T waves, modified-Sgarbossa, AI-ECG) identifies patients who benefit from emergent reperfusion despite "non-STEMI" labels.
  • 🧪 hs-troponin nuance: sex-specific 99th-percentile cut-offs; the value of very low single values (< limit of detection) for immediate rule-out; assay-specific 0/1-h and 0/2-h absolute delta thresholds (ESC 2023).
  • 🫀 MINOCA (MI with non-obstructive coronaries): work up plaque disruption (intravascular imaging — OCT/IVUS), coronary spasm (provocation), spontaneous coronary artery dissection (SCAD — think young women, peripartum), microvascular disease, and cardiac MRI to distinguish true MI from myocarditis/Takotsubo.
  • 🧬 Antiplatelet timing debate: routine pre-treatment with P2Y₁₂ before angiography in NSTE-ACS is no longer recommended (no ischaemic benefit, more bleeding — ISAR-REACT 5 favoured prasugrel over ticagrelor with a deferred-loading strategy).
  • 🖥️ Coronary CT angiography in low–intermediate-risk ED chest pain: high NPV for ruling out CAD (ROMICAT-II, PROMISE), useful when troponin/ECG are equivocal and pre-test probability is modest.
  • 🩸 Cardiogenic-shock ACS: culprit-only PCI (CULPRIT-SHOCK), early revascularisation (SHOCK), judicious use of mechanical support.

🎭 Morbidity & Mortality Conference

🎭 M&M — "The Antacid That Killed"

The case: A 61-year-old diabetic man came to a crowded ED at 02:00 with "burning" epigastric/chest discomfort and one vomit. Triaged as "dyspepsia." He was given an antacid "GI cocktail," felt "a bit better," and — with the department full — was moved to the corridor to await review. The single ECG taken at triage showed tall R waves and ST depression in V1–V3; no one recorded posterior leads and no one repeated it. Ninety minutes later he was found unresponsive in the corridor chair — a posterior STEMI that had extended, then arrested in VF. Resuscitation failed.

🧀 The Swiss-cheese holes that lined up:

🧠 ANCHORING"Dyspepsia" label stuck; the GI cocktail "response" falsely reassured.
📈 ECG NOT READPosterior STEMI pattern (ST↓ V1–V3) unrecognised; posterior leads never done.
🔁 NO SERIAL ECGOne snapshot, never repeated despite ongoing symptoms.
🏥 SYSTEMOvercrowding → corridor care → no monitoring, no defib to hand.
🩺 RISK IGNOREDDiabetic with atypical presentation treated as low-risk.

🛡️ What would have saved him: treat every diabetic with chest/epigastric pain as high-risk; read ST depression in V1–V3 as a possible posterior STEMI and record V7–V9; repeat the ECG on any ongoing symptom; keep suspected ACS monitored with defib to hand — never in an unmonitored corridor; and never let a "GI cocktail response" close the door. This is exactly the discipline that saved the patient in Bed 1.

⚔️ ONE-LINER RULE"'Responded to antacid' has killed more patients than it has ever diagnosed. Symptom relief is not a diagnosis — the ECG and the troponin are."
