  2. 🫧 Controlled high-flow oxygen, titrate to SpO₂ 94–98%.
  3. 💨 Back-to-back / continuous nebulised salbutamol 5 mg + ipratropium 0.5 mg, oxygen-driven.
  4. 💊 Steroids early — hydrocortisone 100 mg IV (or prednisolone 40–50 mg PO if able).

⚔️ ONE-LINER RULE"Call for the airway team on the way UP, not on the way DOWN. In near-fatal asthma the decision you must never make late is 'who intubates when he tires' — have them in the room before he needs them."
🧪 Resident's self-testThe nurse asks whether to give a big dose of IV morphine or midazolam because he is agitated. Do it?

No — not as a sedating "calming" dose on a spontaneously breathing, tiring asthmatic. Agitation here is HYPOXIA and HYPERCAPNIA, not anxiety. Sedatives blunt his respiratory drive and can precipitate the arrest. Sedation and paralysis belong to a controlled rapid-sequence induction by the airway team who are then ready to ventilate — never as a standalone anxiolytic before support is in the room.

🧠 Teaching Points + Interactive Simulation

🔬 The mechanism — WHY the silent chest, WHY the rising CO₂ (the WHY behind the WHY)

  1. 🌪️ Bronchospasm + mucosal oedema + mucus plugging narrow the airways. Expiration — normally passive — becomes slow and incomplete.
  2. 🎈 Dynamic hyperinflation / breath-stacking: the next breath comes in before the last one has fully left. Air stacks in the lungs, trapped behind narrowed airways at end-expiration. This trapped pressure is auto-PEEP (intrinsic PEEP).
  3. 💪 The exhaustion spiral: hyperinflated lungs put the diaphragm at a mechanical disadvantage; each breath costs enormous work against auto-PEEP. The patient generates huge negative pressures — until the respiratory muscles simply fatigue.
  4. 🔇 The silent chest: so little air is moving that there is no turbulence to make a wheeze. Silence = minimal ventilation = the end-stage, not the calm.
  5. 📈 Rising CO₂: early in an attack the frightened, driven patient hyperventilates → LOW CO₂. As they tire, minute ventilation falls and CO₂ rises back to "normal" then above it. So a CO₂ climbing toward normal is the fatigue curve, not recovery.
⚔️ ONE-LINER RULE"In an acute severe attack, a rising CO₂ is the sound of the respiratory muscles giving up. 'Normalising' gases in a tiring asthmatic mean he is running OUT of the strength to over-breathe — escalate, do not relax."

⚖️ Contrast pair — the frightened early attack vs the exhausted late attack

😤 Early / compensating

  • Agitated, tachypnoeic, loud wheeze, tripod posture
  • PaCO₂ LOW (blowing it off)
  • Working hard but keeping up

😴 Late / decompensating (this patient)

  • Drowsy, slowing RR, SILENT chest, poor effort
  • PaCO₂ normal then RISING
  • Failing — pre-arrest

💡 The two look opposite on the monitor (loud/fast vs quiet/slow) but the second is far sicker. Read the direction of travel.

📋 Orders

🖊️ The order set (21:44) — the treatment ladder, delivered in parallel

  • 🫧 Controlled high-flow oxygen, titrate to SpO₂ 94–98% (this is not COPD — aim high, but titrate).
  • 💨 Nebulised salbutamol 5 mg back-to-back / continuous + ipratropium bromide 0.5 mg (repeat 4–6 hourly), oxygen-driven.
  • 💊 Steroids EARLY: hydrocortisone 100 mg IV or prednisolone 40–50 mg PO — give in the first minutes.
  • 🧲 Magnesium sulfate 1.2–2 g IV over 20 min for severe/life-threatening not responding.
  • ⚡ Escalation (senior/ICU only): IV salbutamol or IV aminophylline (load 5 mg/kg over 20 min unless already on theophylline, then infusion with levels).
  • 🆘 Refer ICU + anaesthetics now; continuous SpO₂/ECG/BP; ABG for CO₂ trend; portable CXR (exclude pneumothorax).
  • 🚫 Do NOT: give routine antibiotics (most attacks are viral/allergic); do NOT sedate a spontaneously breathing tiring asthmatic outside a controlled RSI.
⚔️ ONE-LINER RULE"OSHIMA delivered together, not in sequence: Oxygen, Salbutamol, Hydrocortisone, Ipratropium, Magnesium, Anaesthetics/ICU. The dying asthmatic gets everything at once and the airway team called early."
S8

👩‍⚕️ Nursing Care

🛏️ Resus-bay priorities — the nurse runs the safety net

  • 📟 Continuous SpO₂, ECG and BP; never leave a near-fatal asthmatic unobserved — arrest can be minutes away.
  • 💨 Keep nebulisers running oxygen-driven and back-to-back; a dry nebuliser chamber is lost bronchodilator time — refill immediately.
  • 😴 Flag the "settling" patient loudly: a falling RR, quieter chest, or drowsiness is deterioration — call the doctor, do not dim the lights.
  • 🩸 Draw and time the ABG/VBG accurately; the CO₂ trend is a headline number for the team.
  • 🧰 Have the airway trolley and a chest-drain set at the bedside before they are needed — sudden hypotension may be a tension pneumothorax.
  • 🚫 Challenge any request to give sedation or "just move him to the ward"; confirm the escalation plan is documented and ICU is aware.
⚔️ ONE-LINER RULE"The nurse who refuses to let a quiet, drowsy asthmatic 'rest' on a ward bed is the safety catch on a loaded gun."

📈 ABG / Imaging + Visual Enhancements

🩸 The PaCO₂ trend — read it like a movie

The CO₂ starts low (compensating), drifts up through "normal" (the reassuring-looking trap), and climbs into the danger zone as the muscles fail. The diagnosis lived in the TREND, not any single value.

🩻 Chest X-ray & the imaging you actually need

StudyLook forMeaning / action
🩻 Portable CXRHyperinflation (flat diaphragms, >9 posterior ribs); pneumothorax / pneumomediastinum; focal consolidationNot routine for every attack — but do it in life-threatening/near-fatal asthma, if not responding, or if ventilated. A pneumothorax here is lethal.
🫁 Bedside lung ultrasoundAbsent lung sliding = pneumothorax; B-lines suggest an alternative (oedema)Fast bedside rule-out of tension pneumothorax when the ventilated patient drops their BP.
📟 Continuous capnography (once airway secured)Obstructive "shark-fin" EtCO₂ upstroke; trend of EtCO₂Confirms tube, tracks ventilation and obstruction in real time.
🫀 Bedside echo (if shocked)Under-filled, hyperdynamic LV; obstructed venous return from auto-PEEPHelps separate auto-PEEP hypotension from tension pneumothorax from other shock.
⚔️ ONE-LINER RULE"In the ventilated asthmatic who suddenly drops their BP: think auto-PEEP and tension pneumothorax FIRST — DISCONNECT the circuit and decompress the chest, do not just crank up the numbers."

🎈 The picture of dynamic hyperinflation

  • CXR: hyperexpanded, dark lung fields, flattened diaphragms — air the patient cannot get out.
  • Ventilator waveform: expiratory flow that does NOT return to zero before the next breath = auto-PEEP / breath-stacking — the mechanical signature of the trapped air that kills.

S12

📟 Monitoring

📡 What to watch and why

  • 😮‍💨 Work of breathing & conscious level — the most sensitive monitors are your eyes: rising drowsiness or a FALLING respiratory rate = failing, escalate.
  • 🩸 Serial ABG for the CO₂ trend — repeat after treatment and with any deterioration; the direction matters more than the value.
  • 🫁 SpO₂ (target 94–98%) and continuous capnography once the airway is secured.
  • 💓 Continuous ECG — β-agonists + hypokalaemia + hypoxia = arrhythmia risk; watch K⁺.
  • 🩸 BP — a sudden fall in a ventilated asthmatic = auto-PEEP or tension pneumothorax until proven otherwise.
  • 📉 PEF once well enough to perform it — trend recovery, but never let a "can't-do-PEF" patient be under-treated.
⚔️ ONE-LINER RULE"After maximal therapy the monitor's question changes from 'is the asthma severe?' to 'is he tiring toward arrest?' — and the answers are the conscious level, the respiratory rate, and the CO₂ trend."
S13

⚠️ Complications

💀 What can go wrong — the asthma-specific killers

  • 🛑 Respiratory arrest from exhaustion — the commonest fatal pathway; the silent chest and drowsiness are its heralds.
  • 🎈 Dynamic hyperinflation / auto-PEEP — trapped air raises intrathoracic pressure, obstructs venous return → hypotension and PEA; the fix is disconnect the circuit and let the chest deflate.
  • 💥 Tension pneumothorax / pneumomediastinum — from high pressures and air-trapping; sudden hypotension/hypoxia in a ventilated asthmatic → decompress.
  • 💓 Arrhythmia — hypokalaemia (β-agonist), hypoxia, acidosis, aminophylline toxicity.
  • 🧠 Hypoxic-ischaemic brain injury — from a late-recognised arrest.
  • 🩺 The system complication: under-recognition of severity and moving a tiring asthmatic to an unmonitored ward — the biggest killer of all.
⚔️ ONE-LINER RULE"When a ventilated asthmatic arrests or drops their pressure: DISCONNECT (let them exhale), decompress a possible tension pneumothorax, then reventilate slowly. Do not fight auto-PEEP with more breaths."

🗺️ Plan + Procedure Guides with Checklists

🧭 The disposition plan

  1. 🫁 Diagnosis: near-fatal asthma (rising PaCO₂, silent chest, exhaustion, falling GCS). Maximal medical therapy delivered in parallel; ICU + anaesthetics at the bedside.
  2. 🫧 Oxygen 94–98%; continuous nebulised salbutamol + ipratropium; IV steroid; IV magnesium; escalate to IV bronchodilators with ICU.
  3. 🫀 The decision to intubate is CLINICAL — exhaustion, falling GCS, silent chest, failure to respond — not a single ABG number. Prepare for a difficult, high-risk induction.
  4. 🏥 Admit to ICU/HDU; nobody with near-fatal asthma goes to an unmonitored ward. Address the reasons the attack got this far (adherence, preventer, follow-up).
⚔️ ONE-LINER RULE"Intubation in asthma is a decision made at the bedside about a TIRING patient, not a threshold crossed on a blood-gas printout. If you are asking 'should the airway team be here?', the answer is already yes."
🫁 Ventilating the asthmatic — the perils (why the ICU team must lead)
  • Permissive hypercapnia: accept a high CO₂ and low-normal pH to avoid barotrauma — do NOT chase a normal CO₂.
  • Low respiratory rate + LONG expiratory time (low I:E), lower tidal volumes — give the trapped air time to escape and prevent auto-PEEP.
  • Watch for auto-PEEP → hypotension. If BP falls: DISCONNECT the circuit and let the chest deflate, then decompress a possible tension pneumothorax.
  • Peri-intubation is the most dangerous moment: sedation removes the drive that was keeping them alive, and positive pressure can worsen air-trapping and drop the BP.

🏠 Discharge

📋 The near-fatal survivor vs the routine attack — two different discharges

🫁 This patient (near-fatal survivor)

  • Inhaled corticosteroid preventer restarted + ≥5 days oral steroids; inhaler technique checked
  • Personalised written asthma action plan; peak-flow diary
  • Specialist respiratory follow-up (near-fatal = difficult/severe asthma pathway); address adherence & psychosocial risk
  • Red flags: reliever not lasting, night waking, PEF falling → seek help early / call 999

🟢 The routine mild–moderate attack

  • Good response, PEF > 75%, stable off nebs for hours before discharge
  • Preventer + technique reviewed; short oral steroid course
  • GP/asthma-nurse review within 48 h; safety-net advice
⚔️ ONE-LINER RULE"A near-fatal attack is a preventer prescription, a written action plan, a technique check and a specialist follow-up — never 'nebs, feels better, home'. The next attack is the one that decides whether he becomes an NRAD statistic."

💎 Clinical Pearls

🔇 Silent chest = pre-arrestNo air moving means no wheeze. Silence is the end-stage, not the calm.
📈 Rising/"normal" CO₂ is a red flagA tiring asthmatic who stops over-breathing is failing — escalate, do not relax.
🪜 One feature = life-threateningGrade every attack against BTS/SIGN; a single life-threatening feature is enough.
💊 Steroids early, togetherDeliver O₂ + nebs + steroid + magnesium in parallel, not in sequence.
🫀 Intubate on CLINICAL groundsExhaustion and falling GCS decide it — not a blood-gas number.
🎈 Beware auto-PEEPVentilate slowly, long expiratory time, permissive hypercapnia; if BP drops, DISCONNECT.
💥 Sudden hypotension = decompressTension pneumothorax and auto-PEEP kill the ventilated asthmatic — think of them first.
🚫 No routine antibiotics / no lone sedationAttacks are viral/allergic; sedating a breathing tiring asthmatic can precipitate arrest.

🔬 Advanced Topics — Fellowship Level

🧠 Beyond the ladder

  • 🌬️ Ventilator strategy in detail: the enemy is dynamic hyperinflation. Use low minute ventilation (low rate ~6–10, tidal volume ~6 mL/kg), high inspiratory flow to maximise expiratory time, monitor plateau pressure (keep < ~30 cmH₂O) rather than peak, and measure intrinsic PEEP with an expiratory-hold. Accept a high CO₂ (permissive hypercapnia) provided pH is tolerable.
  • 💉 Ketamine as an induction/sedation agent has bronchodilator properties and preserves respiratory drive/BP — often favoured for RSI in severe asthma.
  • 🫁 NIV in asthma is controversial: unlike COPD it is not standard; may be trialled in selected cooperative patients in a critical-care setting, but must NEVER delay intubation in the tiring patient.
  • 🫧 Heliox (helium–oxygen) lowers airway resistance to turbulent flow; evidence is limited but occasionally used as a bridge in refractory obstruction.
  • ❤️ ECMO / extracorporeal CO₂ removal is a rescue for refractory life-threatening bronchospasm with uncontrollable hypercapnia/acidosis or barotrauma — refer to an ECMO centre early rather than late.
  • 💊 After the crisis — the "difficult/severe asthma" work-up: phenotyping (eosinophilic vs allergic), adherence and inhaler-technique review, and biologics (anti-IgE omalizumab; anti-IL-5 mepolizumab/benralizumab; anti-IL-4Rα dupilumab) to prevent the next near-fatal attack.

🎭 Morbidity & Mortality Conference

🎭 M&M — "The 'Normal' Gas That Killed"

The case: A 24-year-old asthmatic came in at 23:00 with an acute severe attack. Nebulisers and steroids were given and, after an hour, the team noted she was "quieter and more settled." A repeat ABG showed a PaCO₂ that had risen from 3.4 to 5.2 kPa — labelled "normal, improving" — with a lactate of 4. She was breathing more slowly and looked tired. With the ED full, she was moved to a general medical ward for "observation overnight", off continuous monitoring, no ICU referral made. Her chest had in fact gone silent. At 03:10 she was found in respiratory arrest; the crash team achieved return of circulation but she sustained severe hypoxic brain injury and died five days later.

🧀 The Swiss-cheese holes that lined up:

🧠 MISREAD TRENDA CO₂ rising from low → "normal" was read as improvement, not fatigue.
🔇 SILENT CHEST MISSED"Quieter/settled" was decompensation — no air movement, not calm.
🆘 NO ESCALATIONLife-threatening features present but no ICU/anaesthetic referral.
🏥 WRONG PLACEA near-fatal asthmatic sent to an unmonitored ward.
📟 NO MONITORINGContinuous SpO₂/ECG removed at the worst possible moment.

🛡️ What would have saved her: read a rising/"normalising" CO₂ in a tiring asthmatic as failing ventilation; recognise the silent chest and drowsiness as pre-arrest; refer ICU + anaesthetics at the first life-threatening feature; keep every near-fatal asthmatic monitored in critical care — never a dark ward; and never let "she seems settled" close the book. This is exactly the discipline that saved the patient in Bed 1.

⚔️ ONE-LINER RULE"'Settled and quiet' has killed more asthmatics than any wheeze. In an unwell asthmatic, silence and a normalising CO₂ are the sound of the lungs giving up — act on the trend, not the calm."
