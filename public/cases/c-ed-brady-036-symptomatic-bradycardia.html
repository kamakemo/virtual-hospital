🧭 The ACLS "adverse signs" you screen for in seconds

The moment you see a bradycardia, ask whether any of the four adverse features are present: shock (hypotension, pale, sweaty, confused), syncope, myocardial ischaemia (chest pain / ischaemic ECG), or heart failure. Any one of them turns "observe" into "atropine and prepare to pace." This patient has three of the four.

⚠️ The trap of "he's talking, so he's fine"A patient on a wide, slow escape can be conversant one minute and asystolic the next — the escape focus is unreliable. Rousable is not stable. Keep the pads on, keep him monitored, and get the pacing and drugs ready before he deteriorates, not after.

🧠 Teaching Points + Interactive Simulation

🔬 The mechanism — WHY the block, WHY the danger (the WHY behind the WHY)

  1. 🚦 The conduction highway fails: in third-degree (complete) block, no atrial impulse crosses to the ventricles. The atria and ventricles are fully dissociated — P waves and QRS complexes march to independent drummers.
  2. 🕯️ Survival depends on an escape rhythm: a subsidiary pacemaker below the block takes over. Where it arises decides everything.
  3. 🫀 Junctional escape (high in the AV node / His): narrow QRS, ~40–60/min, relatively reliable — often perfuses and buys time.
  4. 💀 Ventricular escape (low His–Purkinje): wide QRS, ~20–40/min, unreliable — a candle in the wind that can gutter out into asystole.
  5. 🧠 Why the symptoms: too few beats per minute → low cardiac output → cerebral hypoperfusion (presyncope/Stokes–Adams), hypotension, ischaemia, and pulmonary congestion.
⚔️ ONE-LINER RULE"A wide, slow escape is a candle in the wind — pace before it blows out. The narrower and faster the escape, the more time you have; the wider and slower, the less."

⚖️ Contrast pair — atropine-responsive vs atropine-unresponsive block

💉 AV-nodal block (atropine helps)

  • Block IS the AV node — vagally influenced
  • Mobitz I (Wenckebach); many inferior-MI blocks
  • Narrow escape, ~40–60, often transient
  • Atropine / vagolysis frequently improves conduction

🚫 Infranodal block (atropine won't help)

  • Block is below the node, in His–Purkinje
  • Mobitz II; anterior-MI complete block
  • Wide escape, ~20–40, unreliable, ominous
  • Needs PACING — atropine may do nothing or worsen it

💡 The width of the QRS in the escape rhythm is your bedside proxy for the level of the block — and therefore for whether atropine has any chance of working.

📈 ECG / Imaging + Visual Enhancements

🫀 The rhythm strip — read the dissociation

The little P waves march at their own regular rate; the wide QRS escape complexes are slower and unrelated — the atria and ventricles are completely dissociated. More P waves than QRS, no fixed PR: third-degree (complete) AV block with a broad ventricular escape.

🔎 The AV-block spectrum you must read cold

PatternLook forMeaning
🟢 First-degreeFixed long PR > 200 ms, every P conductsUsually benign; watch, don't pace.
🟡 Mobitz I (Wenckebach)Progressive PR lengthening then a dropped beat; grouped beatingAV-nodal, usually benign, often no pacing.
🔴 Mobitz IIConstant PR with sudden non-conducted P; often wide QRSInfranodal, dangerous → pacemaker.
⚫ High-grade / 2:1≥2 consecutive non-conducted P wavesFrequently progresses to complete block → pace.
💀 Third-degree (complete)AV dissociation; P and QRS independent; escape rhythmVentricles survive only on the escape — narrow=safer, wide=ominous.
⚔️ ONE-LINER RULE"PR lengthening then a drop is Wenckebach; a PR that stays fixed then suddenly drops a beat is Mobitz II — and Mobitz II, wide, is the one that earns a wire before it earns a coffin."

🩻 Adjunct imaging & bedside

  • 12-lead: inferior ST changes (RCA — AV-nodal, often transient) vs anterior changes/new bundle branch block (LAD — infranodal, ominous).
  • Bedside echo (POCUS): assess LV function and rule out a large effusion; confirm mechanical capture once pacing (a QRS with a matching pulse and ventricular contraction).

S12

📟 Monitoring

📡 What to watch and why

  • 📈 Continuous ECG with pacer/defib to hand — watch the escape rate and QRS width; a widening, slowing escape signals imminent failure.
  • ⏱️ Ventricular pauses — any pause > 3 s is a red flag for asystole; pace.
  • 🩸 BP every 3–5 min — the block only matters through perfusion; falling BP = escalate.
  • ⚡ If pacing: confirm electrical capture (pacing spike → QRS) AND mechanical capture (palpable pulse / femoral Doppler / echo) — a spike without a pulse is not capturing.
  • 🧪 Serial potassium and repeat ECG as hyperkalaemia is treated; watch the block improve as the biochemistry corrects.
⚔️ ONE-LINER RULE"A pacing spike is not a heartbeat. Confirm mechanical capture — a spike marching across the screen with no pulse is a false sense of security while the patient dies."
S13

⚠️ Complications

💀 What can go wrong in the ED phase

  • ⚡ Asystole / cardiac arrest — the escape focus fails; the reason pads stay on and the pacer stays ready.
  • 🌀 Bradycardia-dependent ventricular arrhythmia — long pauses provoke torsades de pointes ("pause-dependent" VT).
  • 💔 Cardiogenic shock / pulmonary oedema from sustained low output.
  • 😖 Failure to capture / painful pacing — transcutaneous pacing is agonising; under-sedation and non-capture are common pitfalls.
  • 🧠 Falls & injury from recurrent Stokes–Adams syncope (head injury, fractures).
  • 🩹 The decision complication: treating a wide-complex block with repeated atropine while pacing is delayed — the single most dangerous management error here.
⚔️ ONE-LINER RULE"The deadliest complication of high-grade block is the minute you spend giving a third dose of atropine instead of pressing the pacer. Time you spend on a drug that can't work is time the escape rhythm may not have."

🏠 Discharge

📋 Two very different endings — reversible cause vs permanent block

🔁 This patient (reversible)

  • Cause reversed (K⁺, digoxin, drugs) → conduction recovered, no permanent pacemaker
  • Rationalise rate-control drugs; avoid stacking AV-nodal agents; renal-adjust dosing
  • Monitor renal function; safety-net for recurrent syncope
  • Red flags: blackouts, dizziness, chest pain, breathlessness → 999

🔋 The irreversible high-grade block

  • Mobitz II / complete / symptomatic block with NO reversible cause → permanent pacemaker
  • Device education, wound care, follow-up device checks
  • Driving restrictions per local rules after syncope/device
  • Cardiology follow-up; secondary prevention of ischaemia
⚔️ ONE-LINER RULE"A recovered block from a reversible cause goes home WITHOUT a pacemaker and with a de-prescribing plan; a Mobitz II or complete block with no reversible cause goes home WITH one. Match the box to the cause."

🔬 Advanced Topics — Fellowship Level

🧠 Beyond the algorithm

  • 🎚️ Level of block & why atropine helps one not the other: atropine is vagolytic and accelerates AV-nodal conduction, so it aids AV-nodal block (Mobitz I, many inferior-MI blocks). In infranodal (His–Purkinje) block — Mobitz II, wide-complex complete block — conduction failure is structural and vagally independent; atropine does nothing and, by speeding the atrial rate, can even worsen the ratio of conducted beats.
  • 🫀 Inferior vs anterior MI block — prognosis: inferior-MI (RCA) block is usually AV-nodal, narrow-escape, vagally mediated, atropine-responsive and typically resolves within days. Anterior-MI (LAD) block reflects extensive septal necrosis of the His–Purkinje system — wide-escape, atropine-resistant, pacing-dependent, and a marker of large infarct with high mortality.
  • 👶 Congenital vs acquired complete block: congenital CHB (often maternal anti-Ro/anti-La) usually has a stable, narrow, faster junctional escape and may be tolerated for years; acquired block in the elderly tends to have lower, less reliable escapes and warrants pacing far sooner.
  • 🦠 Infective/infiltrative causes: Lyme carditis (reversible AV block, often needs only temporary pacing + antibiotics — always ask about tick exposure in young patients with unexplained block), cardiac sarcoidosis (patchy granulomatous conduction disease — think in young/middle-aged unexplained high-grade block; cardiac MRI/PET), and infective endocarditis with an aortic-root/perivalvular abscess eroding into the conduction system (a new AV block in endocarditis is a surgical alarm).
  • 🔋 Permanent pacemaker indications: acquired symptomatic or high-grade/complete AV block, Mobitz II (even asymptomatic), alternating bundle branch block, and symptomatic sinus node disease — after excluding reversible causes (drugs, hyperkalaemia, ischaemia, hypothyroidism, Lyme).
  • ⚡ Transvenous vs transcutaneous vs epicardial pacing: transcutaneous is the immediate bridge (fast, painful, capture must be confirmed); transvenous is the reliable temporary solution; leadless and His-bundle/conduction-system pacing are advancing the permanent-device landscape.

🎭 Morbidity & Mortality Conference

🎭 M&M — "Three Doses of Atropine"

The case: A 68-year-old man arrived with chest pain and collapse. The monitor showed a complete heart block with a wide ventricular escape at 28/min; the 12-lead showed an anterior STEMI. He was hypotensive and drowsy. The team gave atropine 500 mcg — no response — then a second, then a third dose, while one nurse was sent across the department to find the transcutaneous pacing pads and another paged cardiology. Roughly seven minutes passed with a drug that could not work on an infranodal block. The escape rhythm slowed further, then failed into asystole before pacing pads were ever placed. Resuscitation was unsuccessful.

🧀 The Swiss-cheese holes that lined up:

🧠 WRONG DRUG, WRONG BLOCKAtropine repeated in a wide-complex infranodal (anterior-MI) block that it cannot fix.
⏱️ PACING DELAYEDPads not immediately to hand; capture never achieved before the escape failed.
📈 LEVEL NOT READWide QRS escape = infranodal = pace, not more atropine — the ECG clue was missed.
🚨 CAUSE UNTREATEDAnterior STEMI needing emergent reperfusion + pacing, not drug titration.
🏥 SYSTEMPacer/pads not pre-positioned at the bedside of a high-grade block.

🛡️ What would have saved him: recognise a wide-complex complete block as infranodal and atropine-resistant; put the pacing pads on first and pace within a minute of an inadequate atropine response; bridge with adrenaline/dopamine; treat the underlying anterior MI with emergent reperfusion; and never let repeated atropine substitute for the pacer. Contrast the M&M M with hyperkalaemic bradycardia treated as merely 'a slow rhythm' — where the fatal miss is failing to check a potassium and give calcium. This is exactly the discipline that saved the patient in Bed 6.

⚔️ ONE-LINER RULE"Atropine three times for a wide-complex block is three doses of nothing while the clock runs out. When the QRS is broad and the block is complete, the pacer — not the syringe — is the treatment."
