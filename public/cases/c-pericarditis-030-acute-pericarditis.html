🧬 Why THIS patient? — virus, pericardium, a diffuse inflammatory burn

The pericardium is a two-layered sac wrapped around the heart. A virus (or an autoimmune, uraemic, post-MI or other trigger) inflames it, and a thin fibrinous exudate forms between the layers. Three things follow directly from that. First, the roughened, inflamed surfaces grate over one another with each beat — the friction rub. Second, the parietal pericardium carries somatic pain fibres, and they are exquisitely positional and pleuritic — hence pain worse lying flat, eased sitting forward. Third, because the inflammation coats the whole heart rather than the territory of one blocked artery, the ECG changes are diffuse and concave, not territorial and convex — and there are no reciprocal changes. The same diffuse inflammation explains why this is the great STEMI mimic and why it is treated with anti-inflammatories, not the cath lab.

🔥 Causes of acute pericarditis (one mnemonic-worthy list) 🦠 Viral / idiopathic (most common) · 🧬 Autoimmune (SLE, RA, scleroderma) · 🫘 Uraemic (advanced renal failure) · 🫀 Post-MI: early epistenocardiac (days) & late Dressler syndrome (weeks, immune-mediated) · 🔪 Post-cardiotomy / post-procedure · 🦠 Bacterial (purulent — sick, septic) · 🫁 Tuberculous (subacute, effusive-constrictive) · 🎗️ Malignant infiltration · 💊 Drug-induced & radiation

🧭 The single most important question: is this STEMI, or pericarditis — and is there a high-risk feature?

This case forks on reading the ECG and the patient correctly. Apply the diagnostic criteria — you need ≥ 2 of 4: (1) typical pleuritic/positional chest pain; (2) a pericardial friction rub; (3) widespread ST elevation OR PR depression; (4) a new/worsening pericardial effusion. Mr. T.O. already meets three. Then screen for high-risk features that change disposition (admission/work-up): fever >38°C, subacute onset, large effusion or tamponade, immunosuppression, oral anticoagulant use, trauma, raised troponin (myopericarditis), or failure to respond to NSAIDs.

The cardiology resident arrives at 14:46. Her reasoning, out loud:

🧠 Pattern recognition first, then physiology

"Young, viral prodrome, sharp pleuritic pain that eases sitting forward, a friction rub I can hear, and an ECG with diffuse concave ST elevation and PR depression and no reciprocal change. That is acute pericarditis — and he already meets three of the four diagnostic criteria. The dangerous instinct here is the opposite of a STEMI miss: it's to be stampeded into the cath lab by an auto-read 'ACUTE MI' and a glance. I'll commit to pericarditis — but I will not relax until I've excluded the things that mimic it the other way and the high-risk features that change disposition."

🎯 Immediate priorities (her actual order of operations)

  1. 🛑 Stop the thrombolysis / stand down the lab for now: a stable patient with a textbook pericarditis ECG must not be lysed. Say it out loud and document the reasoning.
  2. 📐 Re-read the 12-lead deliberately: diffuse vs territorial, concave vs convex, PR depression (and PR elevation in aVR), reciprocal changes (absent except aVR/V1), Spodick sign.
  3. 🎻 Examine for the rub properly — left sternal edge, leaning forward, held expiration — and for any sign of tamponade (raised JVP, pulsus paradoxus, muffled sounds, hypotension).
  4. 🧪 Bloods + troponin: CRP/ESR (supportive, and to track), troponin (a rise means myopericarditis), U&E (uraemic cause), FBC, and tailored tests for autoimmune/TB if the story fits.
  5. 🫀 Echocardiogram: look for a new/worsening effusion (the 4th criterion) and, crucially, for tamponade physiology — RA/RV diastolic collapse, IVC plethora.
  6. 💊 Start treatment: high-dose NSAID (ibuprofen or aspirin) PLUS colchicine, with gastroprotection and activity restriction — and treat any specific identified cause.
⚔️ ONE-LINER RULE"Confirm pericarditis with ≥2 of 4 criteria; start an NSAID AND colchicine together; then hunt the high-risk features (fever >38°, big effusion/tamponade, raised troponin, immunosuppression, anticoagulant, failure to respond) that decide whether he goes home or gets admitted."

🤔 "But what if I'm wrong and it IS a STEMI I'm refusing to lyse?"

"Then I'd be making the mirror-image error — and it would be just as lethal. That is exactly why I don't rely on vibes: I apply the ECG discriminators (diffuse concave with PR depression and NO reciprocal change vs territorial convex WITH reciprocal depression and evolving Q waves), get an immediate echo for regional wall-motion abnormality, and keep cardiology in the loop. If anything is genuinely ambiguous in a patient with coronary risk, the dangerous diagnosis wins and we treat as STEMI. But a young man with a viral prodrome, a rub, and a textbook diffuse pattern is pericarditis — and lysing him is the harm."

🧪 Resident's self-test (answer before reading on) Someone suggests starting corticosteroids straight away "because they're a powerful anti-inflammatory and he'll feel better fast." Good idea first-line?

No. First-line is an NSAID plus colchicine. Corticosteroids are second-line — they feel good acutely but are associated with more recurrences, and they breed relapsing recurrent pericarditis. Reserve steroids for a specific indication: NSAID contraindication, an autoimmune or uraemic cause, pregnancy, or genuinely refractory disease — and even then add colchicine. Starting steroids first is one of the classic mistakes in this disease.

🎓 Consultant Round

The on-call consultant cardiologist, at the bedside at 15:05:

🗂️ Name the disease precisely — it changes the management

🔴 Acute pericarditis (this patient)

  • Diffuse concave ST elevation + PR depression, no reciprocal change (except aVR/V1)
  • Pleuritic, positional pain; friction rub; viral/idiopathic most common
  • Diagnose with ≥ 2 of 4 criteria; CRP/ESR supportive
  • Treat: NSAID + colchicine, activity restriction, gastroprotection

🔵 Myopericarditis / myocarditis

  • Troponin rises when the myocardium is involved (myopericarditis)
  • Myopericarditis: troponin up but LV function preserved
  • Myocarditis: troponin up with LV dysfunction ± arrhythmia/heart failure
  • More cautious: telemetry, restrict exercise (~3–6 months), cardiac MRI; avoid high-dose NSAIDs if significant myocardial involvement
⚔️ ONE-LINER RULE"Check a troponin in every pericarditis. Normal = pure pericarditis; raised with good LV = myopericarditis; raised with poor LV = myocarditis. Same pain, escalating seriousness — the troponin and the echo tell you which."

🌀 Is he high-risk — and could he tamponade?

Most viral/idiopathic pericarditis is benign and managed as an outpatient. The consultant's job is to find the patients who are not. High-risk features (2015 ESC) warranting admission/work-up: fever >38°C, subacute onset, large effusion or tamponade, immunosuppression, oral anticoagulant use, trauma, troponin rise (myopericarditis), or failure to respond to NSAIDs. The feared acute complication is cardiac tamponade — a tense effusion that throttles diastolic filling. Watch for Beck's triad (hypotension, raised JVP, muffled sounds), pulsus paradoxus, and on echo RA/RV diastolic collapse with IVC plethora. The feared late complication is constrictive pericarditis — a stiff, scarred sac (think TB, post-cardiac-surgery, radiation).

📖 Teaching Points

🔬 The mechanism — WHY pericarditis looks like a STEMI but isn't (the WHY behind the WHY)

  1. 🦠 The trigger: a virus (most commonly), or an autoimmune, uraemic, post-MI, post-surgical, TB or malignant process inflames the pericardium. Inflammation, not coronary occlusion, is the engine.
  2. 🧫 The exudate & the rub: a fibrinous exudate roughens the visceral and parietal layers; they grate over each other each beat → the pericardial friction rub, the most specific sign.
  3. 🔪 The pain: the parietal pericardium carries somatic pain fibres (and shares the phrenic nerve), so pain is sharp, pleuritic and positional — worse lying flat, eased sitting forward, worse on inspiration. This is unlike the steady, exertional pressure of ischaemia.
  4. 📐 The ECG: because the inflammation coats the whole epicardial surface rather than one arterial territory, the injury current is diffuse and concave (ST elevation in most leads) with PR depression (atrial injury) — and there are no reciprocal changes except in aVR/V1 (which show PR elevation / ST depression). A STEMI is territorial, convex, with reciprocal depression.
  5. 💧 Why it can still kill: if fluid accumulates faster than the sac can stretch, intrapericardial pressure rises and throttles diastolic filling → tamponade. And a stiff scarred sac later → constriction. The inflammation is usually benign — but the effusion is what you must not miss.
⚔️ ONE-LINER RULE"Pericarditis is a diffuse inflammatory burn of the whole heart's wrapper — so its ST elevation is everywhere and concave with PR depression and no reciprocal change. A STEMI is one blocked artery — territorial, convex, with reciprocal depression. Diffuse vs territorial is the whole game."

⚖️ Contrast pair #1 — Pericarditis vs STEMI (the lethal mimic, both ways)

📐 Pericarditis IS…

  • DIFFUSE concave ("saddle") ST elevation across many leads
  • PR depression (and PR elevation / ST depression in aVR & V1)
  • NO reciprocal ST depression (except aVR/V1)
  • No evolving Q waves; Spodick sign (down-sloping TP segment)
  • Sharp pleuritic positional pain, friction rub, viral prodrome

🎯 STEMI is NOT that…

  • TERRITORIAL convex ("tombstone") ST elevation in one artery's leads
  • Reciprocal ST depression in the opposite leads
  • Evolving Q waves and loss of R wave over hours
  • Steady, crushing, exertional pain; diaphoresis; at-risk patient
  • Goes to primary PCI (or thrombolysis) — not anti-inflammatories

😈 The trap runs both ways: mislabel pericarditis as STEMI → thrombolysis → a small effusion becomes a haemorrhagic effusion and tamponade. Mislabel STEMI as pericarditis → you withhold reperfusion and watch myocardium die. The discriminators — diffuse vs territorial, concave vs convex, PR depression, reciprocal change, evolving Q waves, and the clinical story — are how you avoid both.

⚖️ Contrast pair #2 — Acute vs recurrent vs constrictive pericarditis

🆕 Acute (tonight)

  • First episode, < 4–6 weeks; usually viral/idiopathic
  • Diffuse ST elevation + PR depression; CRP up
  • Treat: NSAID + colchicine; most resolve fully
  • Benign in most — but exclude effusion/tamponade

🔁 Recurrent / Constrictive

  • Recurrent: relapse after a symptom-free interval; colchicine reduces it; steroids drive it
  • Constrictive: stiff scarred sac (TB, post-surgery, radiation)
  • Right heart failure, raised JVP with Kussmaul sign, pericardial knock, calcification
  • Definitive treatment may be pericardiectomy

💡 Why it matters: the colchicine you add today, and the steroids you withhold, are precisely the levers that decide whether this becomes a single self-limiting illness or a relapsing one. Get the first prescription right and you change the natural history.

🔬 Fellowship Corner — Advanced Topics

  • 🧬 Recurrent/refractory pericarditis & IL-1: a subset is autoinflammatory and IL-1 driven; anakinra and rilonacept (RHAPSODY) reduce recurrences in colchicine-resistant, steroid-dependent disease — a steroid-sparing escalation.
  • 🫁 Tuberculous & effusive-constrictive pericarditis: subacute, often with large effusions; needs anti-TB therapy, and adjunctive steroids are debated (IMPI trial). Effusive-constrictive = effusion plus constrictive physiology persisting after drainage.
  • 🧲 Cardiac MRI: late gadolinium enhancement of the pericardium and pericardial oedema confirm active inflammation, distinguish myopericarditis, and guide tapering of therapy in recurrent disease.
  • 💧 Tamponade physiology: exaggerated ventricular interdependence on a non-compliant sac → pulsus paradoxus (inspiratory SBP fall > 10 mmHg), RA/RV diastolic collapse, IVC plethora — treat with pericardiocentesis, never aggressive diuresis.
  • 🔪 Constrictive pericarditis: Kussmaul sign, pericardial knock, septal bounce, discordant LV/RV pressures on simultaneous catheterisation; transient constriction may resolve with anti-inflammatories, chronic calcific constriction needs pericardiectomy.
  • 🫀 Post-cardiac-injury syndromes: Dressler (post-MI), post-pericardiotomy and post-traumatic pericarditis share an immune-mediated mechanism; colchicine (COPPS) reduces post-pericardiotomy syndrome.

📋 Orders

🖊️ The order set, written as you'd actually write it (15:10)

  • 🛑 DO NOT thrombolyse / stand down emergent PCI for this textbook pericarditis ECG in a stable patient — flag the reasoning on the chart.
  • 💊 NSAID first-line: high-dose ibuprofen 600–800 mg PO TDS (or aspirin 750–1000 mg TDS, especially if post-MI) until symptom resolution and CRP normalises, then taper. Add a PPI for gastroprotection.
  • 💊 Colchicine 0.5 mg PO once or twice daily for 3 months (weight/renal-adjusted) — halves recurrence (ICAP/CORP). Counsel re: diarrhoea; check renal/hepatic function and interactions.
  • 🚫 NO first-line corticosteroids — reserve for NSAID contraindication, autoimmune/uraemic cause, pregnancy, or refractory disease (and add colchicine if used).
  • 🛋️ Activity restriction until symptom-free and CRP normal (longer, ~3–6 months, if myopericarditis on troponin/echo).
  • 🧪 Bloods: troponin (myopericarditis?), CRP/ESR (track response), U&E (uraemia), FBC, and targeted autoimmune/TB/HIV testing if the story fits.
  • 🫀 Echocardiogram to assess for effusion and tamponade; 12-lead and observation; repeat echo if any deterioration.
  • 🩻 Treat any specific cause identified (e.g. dialysis for uraemic, anti-TB therapy, autoimmune control); for post-MI/Dressler use aspirin + colchicine, not other NSAIDs.
  • 📞 Cardiology review; admit if any high-risk feature (fever >38°C, subacute onset, large effusion/tamponade, immunosuppression, anticoagulant use, trauma, raised troponin, failure to respond) — otherwise outpatient management with safety-net advice.
⚔️ ONE-LINER RULE"In acute pericarditis there are only two prescriptions that matter: an NSAID AND colchicine, together. Activity restriction and gastroprotection make them safer; steroids withheld keep recurrence away; the echo makes sure you haven't missed an effusion."

👩‍⚕️ Nursing Care

🛏️ CCU / ward nursing priorities

  • 📟 Continuous monitoring with serial obs. A stable pericarditis can develop an effusion — watch BP, HR, JVP and any breathlessness for the drift toward tamponade.
  • 🩸 Know the tamponade alarm signs cold: falling BP, rising HR, raised JVP, muffled heart sounds and pulsus paradoxus — escalate immediately if they appear.
  • 💊 Give the NSAID and colchicine as prescribed, with the PPI; reinforce that both drugs matter and that the course is taken to completion to prevent recurrence.
  • 🚽 Warn about and monitor for colchicine diarrhoea; check it is renally/hepatically dose-adjusted; review interacting drugs (e.g. macrolides, statins).
  • 🛋️ Reinforce activity restriction — no strenuous exercise until symptom-free and CRP normal; longer if myopericarditis.
  • 🪑 Position for comfort: sitting forward eases pericardial pain. Help him find and hold that position; document pain scores and response to analgesia.
  • 📋 Document the ECG and any rub before and after treatment; record troponin/CRP trends — they guide tapering and disposition.
  • 🧠 Reassure and educate: a young patient told "we thought it was a heart attack" is frightened — explain clearly that this is an inflamed sac, usually benign, and treatable.
  • 👨‍👩‍👦 Keep the family updated and supported (see S16); a near-miss with thrombolysis is unsettling for everyone.
⚔️ ONE-LINER RULE"The nurse who watches the JVP and the pulse for tamponade, and who makes sure BOTH the NSAID and the colchicine actually go in, is doing half the work of preventing the two things that hurt these patients."

⚔️ ONE-LINER RULE"In pericarditis, three numbers do the heavy lifting: the troponin (myopericarditis?), the CRP (how active, and your target for taper), and the echo (effusion and tamponade?). Get all three before you call it benign."
🧠 Why a normal troponin here is genuinely reassuringPure pericarditis does not raise troponin; the myocardium is uninvolved. A normal troponin lets you treat this as benign pericarditis with outpatient NSAID + colchicine. Had the troponin risen with a preserved LV, it would be myopericarditis — manage more cautiously (telemetry, longer exercise restriction, cardiac MRI). A rise with LV dysfunction would be myocarditis — a different, more serious disease.
S10

📈 ECG / Imaging

🫀 The presenting ECG — read it like a detective

Widespread concave ("saddle") ST elevation across the limb and precordial leads, with PR-segment depression and no reciprocal depression (except aVR/V1). The diffuse, concave, PR-depressed pattern — not territorial, not convex — is the signature of pericarditis.

🔎 The pericarditis-vs-STEMI ECG toolkit (memorize the high-yield ones)

FeatureWhy it means pericarditis (not STEMI)
📐 Diffuse concave ST elevationInflammation coats the whole epicardium, so the injury current is widespread and "saddle-shaped," not the convex "tombstone" of a single occluded artery.
📉 PR depressionAtrial-layer injury depresses the PR segment in most leads — a near-specific pericarditis sign; the reciprocal PR elevation appears in aVR.
🚫 No reciprocal ST depressionSTEMI produces reciprocal depression in the opposite territory; diffuse pericarditis does not (except aVR/V1). Reciprocal change should make you think STEMI.
🧭 Spodick signA down-sloping TP segment supports pericarditis — a subtle but high-yield clue.
📏 ST/T ratio in V6An ST-to-T-wave amplitude ratio > 0.25 in V6 favours pericarditis over benign early repolarisation, the other diffuse-ST-elevation mimic.
🔁 Four-stage evolutionStage I diffuse ST elevation + PR depression → II normalisation → III diffuse T-wave inversion → IV normalisation. Evolving Q waves and territorial change belong to STEMI, not pericarditis.
⚔️ ONE-LINER RULE"PR depression with diffuse concave ST elevation and no reciprocal change closes the argument — it's pericarditis. But the cost of being wrong toward STEMI is huge, so when truly ambiguous in an at-risk patient, image (echo) and treat the dangerous diagnosis."

🫀 Imaging & staging

  • 📈 The four ECG stages: I — diffuse concave ST elevation with PR depression (PR elevation/ST depression in aVR); II — ST and PR normalise; III — diffuse T-wave inversion; IV — normalisation. Recognise that ongoing T-inversion is part of the natural course, not a new event.
  • 🩻 Echocardiogram: the key bedside imaging — quantify any effusion and look for tamponade physiology (RA/RV diastolic collapse, IVC plethora, respiratory mitral inflow variation).
  • 🧲 Cardiac MRI (selected): pericardial late gadolinium enhancement and oedema confirm active inflammation, identify myopericarditis, and help guide treatment duration in recurrent disease.
⚠️ The ECG's favorite lie in diffuse ST elevation"ST elevation everywhere, so it must be a huge STEMI." A territorial STEMI does not elevate every lead with reciprocal sparing — diffuse, concave, PR-depressed elevation without reciprocal change is pericarditis. The other diffuse mimic is benign early repolarisation; use the V6 ST/T ratio and the clinical story to separate them. The dangerous reverse error — calling a true STEMI "pericarditis" — is avoided by hunting for territory, convexity, reciprocal change and a regional wall-motion abnormality on echo.

💊 Medications

💉 The drugs — what, why, and the traps

DrugRoleMechanism / caution

⚔️ ONE-LINER RULE"NSAID PLUS colchicine, every time, for the first episode — colchicine halves recurrence and is not optional. Steroids feel good and breed recurrence, so they are second-line. After an MI, use aspirin, not ibuprofen."
🚫 The 'never' list in acute pericarditis❌ Thrombolysis / emergent PCI for a textbook pericarditis ECG in a stable patient (can cause haemorrhagic effusion → tamponade) · ❌ Anticoagulation reflexively in a patient with an effusion (bleeding risk) · ❌ First-line corticosteroids (recurrence) · ❌ NSAID monotherapy without colchicine (misses the recurrence-halving benefit) · ❌ non-aspirin NSAIDs post-MI (impair infarct healing).

🏠 Treating the cause (not just the inflammation)

  • 🦠 Viral/idiopathic (most): supportive — NSAID + colchicine, no antivirals routinely.
  • 🫘 Uraemic: intensify dialysis — the pericarditis tracks the uraemia.
  • 🧬 Autoimmune: treat the underlying disease (and here steroids/immunosuppression are appropriately used).
  • 🫁 Tuberculous / bacterial / malignant: specific anti-TB therapy, antibiotics + drainage, or oncological management — and drain significant effusions.
  • 🫀 Post-MI / Dressler / post-pericardiotomy: aspirin + colchicine (colchicine also prevents post-pericardiotomy syndrome — COPPS).
S12

📟 Monitoring

📡 What to watch and why

  • 🩸 Haemodynamics & tamponade screen: serial BP, HR, JVP, heart sounds and pulsus paradoxus — the small effusion today can grow into tamponade; a falling BP with a rising JVP is the alarm.
  • 🫀 Repeat echo if symptoms worsen, the patient becomes breathless, or there is any haemodynamic drift — to re-quantify the effusion and look for diastolic collapse.
  • 📈 Telemetry if myopericarditis (troponin rise) — myocardial involvement carries an arrhythmia risk that pure pericarditis does not.
  • 🧪 Serial CRP — your objective marker of activity and the target for tapering therapy; treat until pain resolves AND CRP normalises.
  • 🌡️ Temperature — a new fever >38°C is a high-risk feature and may signal a bacterial/purulent or evolving course.
  • 📐 Serial ECGs — recognise the four-stage evolution (especially the later diffuse T-inversion) so it isn't misread as a new ischaemic event.
  • 🚽 Colchicine tolerance & renal/hepatic function — watch for diarrhoea and adjust dose; review interacting drugs.
⚔️ ONE-LINER RULE"After you diagnose pericarditis, the monitor's job changes from 'is this a STEMI?' to 'is an effusion building toward tamponade, and is the myocardium involved?' — and the answers live in the JVP, the echo and the troponin."
S13

⚠️ Complications

💀 What can go wrong — and the mechanism

  • 💧 Pericardial effusion → cardiac tamponade — the great acute fear; fluid (or blood) accumulates faster than the sac can stretch, throttling diastolic filling → obstructive shock. Treat with pericardiocentesis.
  • 🩸 Iatrogenic haemorrhagic effusion — thrombolysing or anticoagulating a mislabelled "STEMI" can bleed a small effusion into a tamponading one (the M&M story below).
  • 🫀 Myopericarditis / myocarditis — myocardial involvement (troponin rise) brings arrhythmia risk and, if LV function falls, heart failure.
  • 🔁 Recurrent pericarditis — relapse after a symptom-free interval; far more likely if colchicine is omitted or steroids are used first-line.
  • 🔪 Constrictive pericarditis — a stiff, scarred (sometimes calcified) sac later restricts filling: right heart failure, Kussmaul sign, pericardial knock; may need pericardiectomy. Think TB, post-surgery, radiation.
  • 🦠 Purulent / specific-cause progression — a missed bacterial, TB or malignant cause can deteriorate without targeted treatment and drainage.

🎭 Morbidity & Mortality Conference — "The STEMI That Wasn't"

The case: A 34-year-old man, fit and well, arrived at a busy ED with sharp chest pain after a flu-like week. The triage ECG auto-read "ACUTE MI" with ST elevation in many leads. A tired night team, anchored on that read and on "young man, chest pain, ST up — don't miss the STEMI," gave thrombolysis without anyone reading the strip for diffuse concave elevation, PR depression or reciprocal change. There were none — it was textbook pericarditis. Within the hour his small inflammatory effusion turned haemorrhagic; he became hypotensive with a rising JVP and muffled sounds and tamponaded. An emergency pericardiocentesis drained frank blood and saved him — but it was an entirely self-inflicted near-death.

🧀 The Swiss-cheese holes that lined up:

🤖 AUTO-READ TRUSTEDThe machine's "ACUTE MI" was acted on without a human reading the ECG.
📐 ECG NOT READDiffuse concave elevation, PR depression and absent reciprocal change were all there, unread.
🧠 ANCHORING"Don't miss the STEMI" overrode the obvious pericarditis pattern and the viral story.
💉 LETHAL THERAPYThrombolysis into pericarditis → haemorrhagic effusion → tamponade.
🌙 SYSTEMNight fatigue, no senior ECG review, no "read-before-you-lyse" default.

🛡️ What would have saved the time and the blood: read the ECG for diffuse vs territorial, concave vs convex, PR depression and reciprocal change; apply the ≥2-of-4 criteria; listen for a rub; get an echo before committing; and never lyse a textbook pericarditis. This is exactly why your nurse held the thrombolysis today. (The mirror-image error — calling a true STEMI "pericarditis" and withholding reperfusion — is just as lethal; the discriminators protect against both.)

⚔️ ONE-LINER RULE"'The machine said ACUTE MI' is written on the chart of patients thrombolysed into tamponade. The auto-read is a prompt, not a diagnosis — read the strip yourself."

🧩 The "ST elevation / chest pain" differential

🗺️ Plan

🧭 Today → this month → preventing recurrence

  1. 📐 Today (done): ECG read as diffuse concave ST elevation + PR depression, no reciprocal change → pericarditis confirmed (≥2 of 4 criteria); thrombolysis stood down; echo excludes tamponade; NSAID + colchicine + PPI started; activity restriction advised.
  2. 🫀 Identify & treat the cause: viral/idiopathic by default; troponin (myopericarditis), U&E (uraemia), and targeted autoimmune/TB testing as indicated; treat any specific cause directly.
  3. 🚩 Disposition: no high-risk feature → outpatient management with safety-net advice; admit if fever >38°C, subacute onset, large effusion/tamponade, immunosuppression, anticoagulant use, trauma, raised troponin, or failure to respond.
  4. 💊 Complete the course: NSAID tapered against symptoms and CRP; colchicine for ~3 months to prevent recurrence; avoid first-line steroids.
  5. 🔁 Plan for recurrence: if it relapses, extend colchicine, consider why (under-treatment, premature steroid use), and escalate to IL-1 blockade in refractory, steroid-dependent disease.
  6. 👨‍👩‍👦 Education & safety net: explain this is an inflamed sac (usually benign), why both drugs and the activity restriction matter, and the red flags (breathlessness, syncope, worsening pain) that mean return immediately.
⚔️ ONE-LINER RULE"NSAID + colchicine treat the episode and prevent the relapse; the echo and the high-risk screen catch the effusion; completing the colchicine course is what keeps him from coming back. Send him out with all three."

🏠 Discharge

📋 Discharge plan & safety net

  • 💊 Medications: high-dose NSAID (ibuprofen, or aspirin if post-MI) tapered against symptoms and CRP, with a PPI; colchicine for ~3 months to prevent recurrence — emphasise that BOTH matter and the course is completed.
  • 🛋️ Activity restriction: avoid strenuous exercise until symptom-free and CRP normal (longer, ~3–6 months, if myopericarditis); return-to-play guidance for athletes.
  • 🧪 Follow-up: clinic review with repeat CRP and ECG; recognise the expected stage-III T-inversion so it isn't mistaken for a new event.
  • 🚽 Colchicine counselling: diarrhoea common; renal/hepatic dose adjustment; avoid interacting drugs (macrolides, certain statins) and grapefruit.
  • 🚩 Red flags → return / call emergency services: worsening or different chest pain, breathlessness, fainting or near-fainting, palpitations, swelling, or fever — any of which may signal effusion/tamponade, myopericarditis or a recurrence.
  • 📚 Education: what pericarditis is in plain language, why the colchicine and activity restriction matter, why steroids were avoided, and that recurrence is possible but reduced by completing treatment.
⚔️ ONE-LINER RULE"Discharge after pericarditis is not 'pain better, go home' — it is an NSAID taper, a full colchicine course, activity restriction, an excluded effusion, and a patient who knows the red flags that mean come straight back."

💎 Clinical Pearls

📐 Diffuse vs territorialDIFFUSE concave ST elevation + PR depression + NO reciprocal change = pericarditis. Territorial convex elevation with reciprocal depression = STEMI.
🔪 Positional pleuritic painSharp pain worse lying flat, eased sitting forward, worse on inspiration — the single most useful bedside clue.
🎻 The rub is most specificListen at the left sternal edge, leaning forward, in held expiration. A scratchy triphasic rub clinches it.
✅ ≥ 2 of 4 criteriaPleuritic/positional pain · friction rub · widespread ST elevation or PR depression · new/worsening effusion. Two of four diagnoses it.
💊 NSAID + colchicineFirst-line is BOTH. Colchicine halves recurrence (ICAP/CORP) — it is not optional.
🚫 Steroids breed recurrenceCorticosteroids feel good but are associated with more recurrences — they are second-line, not first.
